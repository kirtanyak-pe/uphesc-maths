Why it matters for the exam

Ring theory gives about one question in every paper (2025 Q37 on maximal ideals, 2026 Q47 on the kernel of a ring homomorphism, 2026 Q36 on a quotient ring that is a field). The usual forms are: “\(M\) is maximal iff \(R/M\) is what kind of ring?”, a count (units, idempotents, nilpotents, ideals of \(\mathbb Z_n\)), and a kernel or quotient in \(\mathbb Z[x]\) or \(\mathbb Z[i]\). Every option differs by one hypothesis, so know exactly which results need “commutative with unity”.

Core ideas

Rings: definitions and vocabulary

A ring \((R,+,\cdot)\) has \((R,+)\) an abelian group, \(\cdot\) associative, and both distributive laws. Unless a question says otherwise, assume commutative with unity \(1\).

Term Meaning
zero divisor \(a\neq 0\) with \(ab=0\) for some \(b\neq 0\)
unit \(a\) with \(ab=1\) for some \(b\); the units form a group \(R^{*}\)
integral domain commutative, \(1\neq 0\), no zero divisors
division ring \(1\neq 0\) and every nonzero element is a unit (not nec. commutative)
field commutative division ring
subring closed under \(+,\cdot\), contains \(0\) (and \(1\) if unital convention)

Basic facts: \(0\cdot a=0\); \((-a)b=-(ab)\); \((-1)a=-a\). In a ring with \(1\neq 0\), a unit is never a zero divisor. In an integral domain the cancellation law holds: \(ab=ac,\ a\neq 0\Rightarrow b=c\).

Chain of implications (each arrow is strict):

\[\text{field}\Rightarrow\text{ED}\Rightarrow\text{PID}\Rightarrow\text{UFD}\Rightarrow\text{integral domain}.\]

The middle part is in the next topic. Two facts to know now:

  • A finite integral domain is a field (multiplication by \(a\neq 0\) is injective, hence surjective, so \(ab=1\) is solvable).
  • A finite division ring is a field (Wedderburn). So noncommutative finite examples must have zero divisors, e.g. \(M_2(\mathbb Z_2)\).

Standard examples

  • \(\mathbb Z,\ \mathbb Q,\ \mathbb R,\ \mathbb C\); \(\mathbb Z[i]=\{a+bi\}\); \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt d]\); \(F[x]\); \(M_n(R)\) (noncommutative for \(n\ge 2\), has zero divisors); \(C[0,1]\) (continuous functions, pointwise operations, has zero divisors); \(2\mathbb Z\) (a ring without unity).
  • \(\mathbb Z_n\): units are the \(a\) with \(\gcd(a,n)=1\), so \(\lvert\mathbb Z_n^{*}\rvert=\varphi(n)\). The nonzero non-units are exactly the zero divisors. So \(\mathbb Z_n\) is a field \(\iff\) integral domain \(\iff n\) is prime.
  • Direct product \(R\times S\): componentwise operations; never an integral domain if both are nonzero (\((1,0)(0,1)=0\)); units \((R\times S)^{*}=R^{*}\times S^{*}\).
  • Boolean ring (\(a^2=a\) for all \(a\)): automatically commutative of characteristic \(2\). More generally (Jacobson) if \(a^n=a\) for all \(a\) with fixed \(n>1\), then \(R\) is commutative.

Characteristic

\(\operatorname{char}R\) is the least \(n>0\) with \(n\cdot 1=0\), or \(0\) if none. Facts:

  • For an integral domain, \(\operatorname{char}\) is \(0\) or a prime \(p\).
  • \(\operatorname{char}\mathbb Z_n=n\); \(\operatorname{char}(R\times S)=\operatorname{lcm}\) of the two; so \(\operatorname{char}(\mathbb Z_4\times\mathbb Z_6)=12\). \(\operatorname{char}F[x]=\operatorname{char}M_n(F)=\operatorname{char}F\).
  • In characteristic \(p\) (commutative): \((a+b)^p=a^p+b^p\) (“freshman’s dream”), and \(a\mapsto a^p\) is a ring homomorphism (Frobenius).
  • A finite field has prime-power order \(p^n\) with \(p=\operatorname{char}\) (see fields topic).

Idempotents and nilpotents

\(e\) is idempotent if \(e^2=e\); \(a\) is nilpotent if \(a^k=0\) for some \(k\).

  • In an integral domain the only idempotents are \(0,1\) and the only nilpotent is \(0\). So a nonzero idempotent \(\neq 1\) (or a nonzero nilpotent) forces zero divisors.
  • If \(e\) is idempotent then so is \(1-e\), and \(R\cong eR\times(1-e)R\) (when \(e\) is central).
  • If \(a\) is nilpotent then \(1+a\) is a unit: \((1+a)^{-1}=1-a+a^2-\cdots\pm a^{k-1}\).
  • In \(\mathbb Z_n\) with \(n=p_1^{k_1}\cdots p_r^{k_r}\): number of idempotents \(=2^{r}\) (Chinese remainder theorem: choose \(0\) or \(1\) mod each prime power); nilpotents are the multiples of \(p_1\cdots p_r\), so there are \(n/(p_1\cdots p_r)\) of them.
  • The nilpotents of a commutative ring form an ideal, the nilradical.

Ideals

\(I\subseteq R\) is an ideal if \((I,+)\) is a subgroup and \(ra\in I\) for all \(r\in R,a\in I\) (commutative case). Facts:

  • If an ideal contains a unit it is \(R\). Hence a commutative ring with \(1\) is a field iff its only ideals are \(0\) and \(R\).
  • The ideal generated by \(a\) is \((a)=aR\) (principal). \((a_1,\dots,a_k)=\{\sum r_ia_i\}\).
  • \(I+J\), \(I\cap J\), \(IJ\) are ideals; \(IJ\subseteq I\cap J\). In \(\mathbb Z\): \((a)+(b)=(\gcd(a,b))\), \((a)\cap(b)=(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b))\), \((a)(b)=(ab)\).
  • Ideals of \(\mathbb Z\) are exactly \(n\mathbb Z\). Ideals of \(\mathbb Z_n\) correspond one-to-one with positive divisors \(d\mid n\) (the ideal \(d\mathbb Z_n\)); there are \(\tau(n)\) of them.
  • Ideals of \(R\times S\) are exactly \(I\times J\).
  • \(M_n(F)\) over a field is simple (no ideals except \(0\) and itself) although it is not a field. The ideals of \(M_n(R)\) are \(M_n(I)\).
  • Ideals of \(F[x]\) and \(\mathbb Z\) are all principal. Not all ideals are principal in general: \((2,x)\subset\mathbb Z[x]\) and \((x,y)\subset F[x,y]\) are not principal. In \(C[0,1]\) the ideal \(\{f:f(\tfrac12)=0\}\) is not principal either.
  • Two ideals are comaximal if \(I+J=R\). Then \(I\cap J=IJ\) and (CRT) \(R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J\). Example: \(\mathbb Z_{mn}\cong\mathbb Z_m\times\mathbb Z_n\) iff \(\gcd(m,n)=1\).

Prime and maximal ideals

Let \(R\) be commutative with \(1\).

  • \(P\) is prime if \(P\neq R\) and \(ab\in P\Rightarrow a\in P\) or \(b\in P\).
  • \(M\) is maximal if \(M\neq R\) and no ideal lies strictly between \(M\) and \(R\).

Theorem. (a) \(P\) prime \(\iff R/P\) is an integral domain. (b) \(M\) maximal \(\iff R/M\) is a field. (c) Hence maximal \(\Rightarrow\) prime. (d) Every proper ideal lies in a maximal ideal (Zorn; needs \(1\)).

Proof of (b): \(R/M\) has only the ideals \(0\) and itself iff \(M\) is maximal (correspondence theorem), and a commutative ring with \(1\) with only trivial ideals is a field. This is 2025 Q37: option D (a field), not “integral domain” (that characterises prime).

The converse of (c) is false. Standard examples:

Ideal Quotient Status
\((0)\subset\mathbb Z\) \(\mathbb Z\) prime, not maximal
\((x)\subset\mathbb Z[x]\) \(\mathbb Z\) prime, not maximal
\((2)\subset\mathbb Z[x]\) \(\mathbb Z_2[x]\) prime, not maximal
\((2,x)\subset\mathbb Z[x]\) \(\mathbb Z_2\) maximal
\((x^2+1)\subset\mathbb Z[x]\) \(\mathbb Z[i]\) prime, not maximal
\((x^2+1)\subset\mathbb R[x]\) \(\mathbb C\) maximal
\((x^2-1)\subset\mathbb Q[x]\) \(\mathbb Q\times\mathbb Q\) not prime
\((6)\subset\mathbb Z\) \(\mathbb Z_6\) not prime

In \(\mathbb Z\): \((n)\) is prime iff \(n\) is \(0\) or a prime; maximal iff \(n\) is prime. In a PID, every nonzero prime ideal is maximal. In \(\mathbb Z_n\) the prime ideals are the maximal ideals \(p\mathbb Z_n\), one for each prime \(p\mid n\) (number of prime ideals of \(\mathbb Z_{60}\) is \(3\)). In \(F[x]\): \((f)\) is maximal iff \(f\) is irreducible. In \(C[0,1]\): \(M_c=\{f:f(c)=0\}\) is maximal (quotient \(\cong\mathbb R\)) and every maximal ideal is of this form.

Without unity the theorem fails: in the ring \(2\mathbb Z\), the ideal \(4\mathbb Z\) is maximal (index \(2\)) but not prime (\(2\cdot2\in4\mathbb Z\), \(2\notin4\mathbb Z\)), and \(2\mathbb Z/4\mathbb Z\) has zero product, so it is not a field. Always check “commutative with unity” in options.

A ring \(\mathbb Z_{p^k}\), \(k\ge 1\), has a unique maximal ideal \((p)\) (a local ring); its non-units are exactly \((p)\).

Quotient rings

\(R/I=\{a+I\}\) with \((a+I)+(b+I)=(a+b)+I\), \((a+I)(b+I)=ab+I\). It is commutative/unital if \(R\) is. Useful computations:

  • \(\mathbb Z[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb Z[i]\) and \(\mathbb R[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb C\) (send \(x\mapsto i\)).
  • \(\mathbb Z[x]/(x)\cong\mathbb Z\), \(\mathbb Z[x]/(n,x)\cong\mathbb Z_n\), \(\mathbb Z[x]/(x-a)\cong\mathbb Z\).
  • \(\mathbb Z[i]/(a+bi)\) has \(a^2+b^2\) elements (the norm). \(\mathbb Z[i]/(1+i)\cong\mathbb Z_2\); \(\mathbb Z[i]/(2+i)\cong\mathbb Z_5\) because \((2+i)\cap\mathbb Z=5\mathbb Z\); \(\mathbb Z[i]/(2)\) has \(4\) elements and is not a field (\((1+i)^2=2i\in(2)\), so \(1+i\) is a nonzero nilpotent).
  • \(\mathbb Z_n/(d)\cong\mathbb Z_d\) for \(d\mid n\).
  • \(R[x]/(x^2)\) (\(R\) a field) has the nilpotent \(x\): not a domain.

Ring homomorphisms

\(\varphi:R\to S\) with \(\varphi(a+b)=\varphi(a)+\varphi(b)\), \(\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)\). Convention to watch: some books also demand \(\varphi(1)=1\) (“unital”). Say which you use.

  • \(\ker\varphi\) is an ideal; \(\operatorname{im}\varphi\) is a subring; \(\varphi\) injective iff \(\ker\varphi=0\).
  • First isomorphism theorem: \(R/\ker\varphi\cong\operatorname{im}\varphi\). Second and third theorems: \((A+I)/I\cong A/(A\cap I)\) and \((R/I)/(J/I)\cong R/J\) for \(I\subseteq J\).
  • Correspondence theorem: ideals of \(R/I\) correspond to ideals of \(R\) containing \(I\), and prime/maximal ideals correspond too.
  • A nonzero homomorphism from a field is injective (the kernel is \(0\) or the whole field), so the image of a field under a nonzero hom is a field.
  • Evaluation homomorphism: for commutative \(S\supseteq R\) and \(s\in S\), \(R[x]\to S\), \(f\mapsto f(s)\). Its kernel is the set of polynomials vanishing at \(s\).
  • Unital ring homs \(\mathbb Z_m\to\mathbb Z_n\) exist iff \(n\mid m\), and then there is exactly one (\(1\mapsto 1\)). Not necessarily unital: such a hom is determined by \(e=\varphi(1)\), which must satisfy \(e^2=e\) and \(me=0\) in \(\mathbb Z_n\). The number of such homs is the number of such \(e\).
  • The only ring homomorphism (unital) \(\mathbb Z\to R\) is \(n\mapsto n\cdot 1\). \(\mathbb Q\) and \(\mathbb R\) have only the identity automorphism.

Field of fractions

Every integral domain \(D\) embeds in a field \(\operatorname{Frac}(D)=\{a/b\}\), the smallest field containing it. \(\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbb Z)=\mathbb Q\), \(\operatorname{Frac}(F[x])=F(x)\), \(\operatorname{Frac}(\mathbb Z[i])=\mathbb Q(i)\).

Worked examples

Example 1: structure of \(\mathbb Z_{12}\)

Units: \(\gcd(a,12)=1\), so \(\{1,5,7,11\}\) (\(\varphi(12)=4\)). Zero divisors: \(\{2,3,4,6,8,9,10\}\) (every nonzero non-unit). Idempotents: \(a^2\equiv a\pmod{12}\) gives \(\{0,1,4,9\}\) (\(2^2=4\) of them, since \(12\) has two prime factors). Nilpotents: \(\{0,6\}\) (\(12/(2\cdot 3)=2\)). Maximal ideals: \((2)=\{0,2,4,6,8,10\}\) and \((3)=\{0,3,6,9\}\), quotients \(\mathbb Z_2\) and \(\mathbb Z_3\). Ideals: \(\tau(12)=6\).

Example 2: counting ring homomorphisms \(\mathbb Z_{12}\to\mathbb Z_{18}\)

A hom is fixed by \(e=\varphi(1)\) with \(e^2\equiv e\) and \(12e\equiv 0\pmod{18}\). Idempotents of \(\mathbb Z_{18}\): \(\{0,1,9,10\}\). \(12e\equiv 0\pmod{18}\) means \(2e\equiv 0\pmod 3\), so \(3\mid e\): \(e\in\{0,9\}\). So there are \(2\) ring homomorphisms if \(\varphi(1)=1\) is not required, and none with \(\varphi(1)=1\) (as \(18\nmid 12\)).

Example 3: kernel of \(\varphi:\mathbb Z[x]\to\mathbb Z_6\), \(f\mapsto f(1)\bmod 6\) (2026 Q47)

Write \(f(x)=f(1)+(x-1)g(x)\) with \(g\in\mathbb Z[x]\) (factor theorem for the monic \(x-1\)). Then \(f\in\ker\varphi\) iff \(6\mid f(1)\) iff \(f\in(6,x-1)\): if \(6\mid f(1)\) then \(f\in(6)+(x-1)\); conversely \(6\) and \(x-1\) both map to \(0\). So \(\ker\varphi=(6,x-1)\). It is not principal: a generator would divide \(6\) and \(x-1\) in \(\mathbb Z[x]\), forcing it to be \(\pm1\), but \(1\notin\ker\varphi\). Also \(\mathbb Z[x]/(6,x-1)\cong\mathbb Z_6\) is not a domain, so the kernel is not prime.

Example 4: is the ideal prime or maximal?

(a) \(I=(x^2+1)\subset\mathbb Z[x]\): \(\mathbb Z[x]/I\cong\mathbb Z[i]\), a domain but not a field (\(2\) is not invertible). Prime, not maximal. (b) \(J=(2,x)\): \(\mathbb Z[x]/J\cong\mathbb Z_2\), a field. Maximal. (c) \(K=(x^2-1)\subset\mathbb Q[x]\): \(x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)\) with neither factor in \(K\). Not prime. Quotient \(\cong\mathbb Q\times\mathbb Q\).

Example 5: \(\mathbb Z[i]/(2+i)\)

\(N(2+i)=5\), so \(5\) elements. Since \((2+i)(2-i)=5\in(2+i)\) and \(1\notin(2+i)\), the composite \(\mathbb Z\to\mathbb Z[i]\to\mathbb Z[i]/(2+i)\) is onto (because \(i\equiv -2\), every \(a+bi\equiv a-2b\)) with kernel \(\supseteq 5\mathbb Z\), and kernel is proper, so kernel \(=5\mathbb Z\). Hence \(\cong\mathbb Z_5\), a field, so \((2+i)\) is maximal.

Example 6: product ring

\(R=\mathbb Z_5\times\mathbb Z_8\). Units: \(\varphi(5)\varphi(8)=4\cdot4=16\). Characteristic: \(\operatorname{lcm}(5,8)=40\). Maximal ideals: the maximal ideals of \(R\times S\) are \(M\times S\) and \(R\times N\) with \(M,N\) maximal in \(R,S\). Here that gives exactly two: \(\{0\}\times\mathbb Z_8\) and \(\mathbb Z_5\times 2\mathbb Z_8\).

Common traps

  • Maximal vs prime. “\(R/I\) is an integral domain” is prime; “field” is maximal. 2025 Q37 offers integral domain, Euclidean domain, UFD, field: only field is equivalent to maximal.
  • Hypotheses. The prime/maximal criteria need a commutative ring with unity. In the ring \(2\mathbb Z\), \(4\mathbb Z\) is maximal but not prime, and the quotient is not a field.
  • Zero ideal. \((0)\) is prime iff \(R\) is an integral domain; \((0)\) is maximal iff \(R\) is a field. So \((0)\) is prime but not maximal in \(\mathbb Z\).
  • \(\mathbb Z_n\) zero divisors. Zero divisors are the nonzero elements sharing a factor with \(n\), not just “even numbers”. In \(\mathbb Z_9\), \(3\) and \(6\) are the zero divisors; \(\mathbb Z_9\) is not a domain.
  • Units of \(\mathbb Z_n\) vs \(\mathbb Z\). Units of \(\mathbb Z[x]\) and \(\mathbb Z\) are \(\pm 1\); \(\mathbb Z_n\) has \(\varphi(n)\) units. In \(\mathbb Z_4[x]\), \(1+2x\) is a unit (\((1+2x)^2=1\)): the “units of \(R[x]\) are the units of \(R\)” rule holds only for domains.
  • Number of roots. In \(\mathbb Z_8\), \(x^2-1\) has four roots \(1,3,5,7\); in \(\mathbb Z_6\), \(x^2=x\) has four roots. The “at most \(\deg\) roots” rule needs a domain.
  • Kernel is principal? \((6,x-1)\) is not principal. Many students write the kernel as \((6)\) or \((x-1)\), each too small.
  • Homomorphism count. Do not forget the non-unital homs when the question does not require \(\varphi(1)=1\).
  • Direct product. \(R\times S\) is never a domain, and its ideals are only products \(I\times J\) (no “diagonal” ideals).
  • Integral domain \(\neq\) field. \(\mathbb Z\), \(\mathbb Z[i]\), \(F[x]\) are domains, not fields. Finite domains are fields; infinite ones need not be.

Previous-year questions

2025 Q37 (official key: D)

Let \(R\) be a commutative ring with unity. An ideal \(M\) of \(R\) is maximal iff \(R/M\) is: (A) an integral domain (B) a Euclidean domain (C) a UFD (D) a field.

Answer: D. \(R/M\) has only trivial ideals iff \(M\) is maximal (correspondence theorem), and a commutative ring with \(1\) with only trivial ideals is a field. “Integral domain” characterises prime ideals.

2026 Q47 (worked key: B, kernel \((6,x-1)\))

\(\varphi:\mathbb Z[x]\to\mathbb Z_6\), \(\varphi(f)=f(1)\bmod 6\). Determine the kernel.

Answer: \((6,x-1)\) (the option with both generators, not principal). Write \(f=f(1)+(x-1)g\); \(f\in\ker\) iff \(6\mid f(1)\) iff \(f\in(6,x-1)\).

The related 2026 Q36 (\(\mathbb Z_5[x]/(x^2+2)\) is a field of \(25\) elements) is solved in the polynomial-rings topic.

Quick revision

  • Maximal \(\iff R/M\) field; prime \(\iff R/P\) integral domain (commutative with \(1\)).
  • Maximal \(\Rightarrow\) prime; \((x)\subset\mathbb Z[x]\) is prime, not maximal; \((2,x)\) is maximal.
  • \(\mathbb Z_n\): units \(\varphi(n)\); field iff \(n\) prime; idempotents \(2^r\); nilpotents \(n/\operatorname{rad}n\); ideals \(\tau(n)\).
  • Finite integral domain is a field; ring with only trivial ideals (commutative, \(1\)) is a field.
  • \(\ker\varphi\) is an ideal; \(R/\ker\varphi\cong\operatorname{im}\varphi\).
  • Kernel of \(f\mapsto f(1)\bmod 6\) on \(\mathbb Z[x]\) is \((6,x-1)\), not principal.
  • Unital hom \(\mathbb Z_m\to\mathbb Z_n\) exists iff \(n\mid m\); non-unital ones come from idempotents \(e\) with \(me=0\).
  • \(\mathbb Z[i]/(a+bi)\) has \(a^2+b^2\) elements; \(\mathbb Z[i]/(2+i)\cong\mathbb Z_5\).
  • \(\operatorname{char}(R\times S)=\operatorname{lcm}\); \(R\times S\) is never a domain.
  • Nilpotent \(a\): \(1+a\) is a unit. Domains have only idempotents \(0,1\).
  • Comaximal \(I+J=R\): \(R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J\).
