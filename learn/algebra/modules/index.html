Why it matters for the exam

The syllabus marks this part “A+” (advanced) and no module question has appeared yet in the 2019, 2025 or 2026 papers, so expect at most one question, usually a true/false item on Noetherian rings, free modules, or a count of homomorphisms. The facts below are short and mechanical: learn the examples and counterexamples (which rings are Noetherian, which modules are free, \(\mathbb Q\) as a \(\mathbb Z\)-module).

Core ideas

Modules: definition and basic examples

Let \(R\) be a ring with \(1\). A (left, unital) \(R\)-module is an abelian group \(M\) with a scalar multiplication \(R\times M\to M\) satisfying \(r(m+n)=rm+rn\), \((r+s)m=rm+sm\), \((rs)m=r(sm)\), \(1m=m\).

Ring \(R\) \(R\)-modules are
a field \(F\) vector spaces over \(F\)
\(\mathbb Z\) abelian groups (the action \(nm\) is forced)
\(\mathbb Z_n\) abelian groups with \(nm=0\) for all \(m\)
\(F[x]\) pairs \((V,T)\): a vector space with a linear operator, \(x\cdot v=T(v)\)
\(R\) itself \(R\) is a module over itself; its submodules are the left ideals

More examples: \(R^n\); \(R/I\) for an ideal \(I\); any ideal \(I\subseteq R\); \(F^n\) over \(F[x]\) via a matrix \(A\) (\(x\cdot v=Av\)); \(\mathbb Q\) and \(\mathbb Z_n\) over \(\mathbb Z\). A module over a field behaves like a vector space (always has a basis); over a general ring it need not.

Submodules, quotients, homomorphisms

  • \(N\subseteq M\) is a submodule if it is a subgroup closed under scalars. Submodules of \(R\) (as a module over itself) are ideals. For \(F[x]\)-modules \((V,T)\), submodules are the \(T\)-invariant subspaces.
  • Sum \(N+N'\) and intersection \(N\cap N'\) of submodules are submodules. The submodule generated by \(S\) is \(\{\sum r_is_i\}\). \(M\) is cyclic if \(M=Rm\), finitely generated if generated by finitely many elements.
  • Quotient \(M/N\) with \(r(m+N)=rm+N\).
  • Homomorphism \(f:M\to N\): additive and \(f(rm)=rf(m)\). Kernel and image are submodules. \(\operatorname{Hom}_R(M,N)\) is an abelian group (a module if \(R\) is commutative).
  • First isomorphism theorem: \(M/\ker f\cong\operatorname{im}f\). Second: \((A+B)/B\cong A/(A\cap B)\). Third: \((M/A)/(B/A)\cong M/B\) for \(A\subseteq B\). Correspondence: submodules of \(M/N\) correspond to submodules of \(M\) containing \(N\).
  • \(\operatorname{Hom}_R(R,M)\cong M\) via \(f\mapsto f(1)\). A hom out of a cyclic module \(R/I\) is determined by \(f(\bar1)=m\), which must satisfy \(Im=0\).

Counting homomorphisms of abelian groups. \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(\mathbb Z_m,\mathbb Z_n)\cong\mathbb Z_{\gcd(m,n)}\): send \(1\mapsto a\) with \(ma\equiv0\pmod n\); there are \(\gcd(m,n)\) choices. \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(\mathbb Z,M)\cong M\). \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(\mathbb Q,\mathbb Z)=0\) and \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(\mathbb Q,\mathbb Z_n)=0\): for a hom \(f\) and every integer \(m\), \(f(q)=m\,f(q/m)\), so \(f(q)\) is divisible by every integer, which forces \(f(q)=0\) in \(\mathbb Z\); in \(\mathbb Z_n\) take \(m=n\).

Annihilators and torsion

  • \(\operatorname{Ann}(m)=\{r:rm=0\}\) is a (left) ideal; \(\operatorname{Ann}(M)=\bigcap_m\operatorname{Ann}(m)\) is a two-sided ideal.
  • Over a domain, \(\operatorname{Tor}(M)=\{m:\operatorname{Ann}(m)\neq0\}\) is a submodule; \(M\) is torsion if \(\operatorname{Tor}M=M\) and torsion-free if \(\operatorname{Tor}M=0\). \(\mathbb Z_n\) is torsion; \(\mathbb Q\) and \(\mathbb Z\) are torsion-free; \(\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z\) is torsion.
  • For an \(F[x]\)-module \((V,T)\), \(\operatorname{Ann}(V)\) is generated by the minimal polynomial of \(T\). This is the bridge to Jordan and rational canonical forms in linear algebra.

Free modules

\(M\) is free if it has a basis (a linearly independent generating set), equivalently \(M\cong R^{(I)}\), a direct sum of copies of \(R\).

  • Universal property: a hom from a free module is determined by arbitrary values on a basis.
  • Over a commutative ring any two bases of a free module have the same cardinality (rank).
  • Every module is a quotient of a free module (map \(R^{(M)}\to M\)). So \(M\cong F/K\).
  • Vector spaces: every module over a field is free. Over other rings, not so: \(\mathbb Z_n\) is not free over \(\mathbb Z\) (torsion), \(\mathbb Q\) is not free over \(\mathbb Z\) (any two rationals are linearly dependent over \(\mathbb Z\), and \(\mathbb Q\) is not cyclic). On the other hand \(\mathbb Z^n\) is free over \(\mathbb Z\) and \(F[x]\) is free over \(F\).
  • Free over \(\mathbb Z_6\): a free \(\mathbb Z_6\)-module of rank \(n\) has \(6^n\) elements, so \(\mathbb Z_2\) and \(\mathbb Z_3\) are \(\mathbb Z_6\)-modules that are not free (they are direct summands of \(\mathbb Z_6\), hence “projective”).
  • Submodules of free modules over a PID are free (of rank at most that of the ambient module). This fails over a non-PID: the ideal \((2,x)\subset\mathbb Z[x]\) is a submodule of the free module \(\mathbb Z[x]\) but is not free (any two elements \(a,b\) satisfy \(b\cdot a-a\cdot b=0\), so a basis would have at most one element; a one-element basis would make the ideal principal).
  • Finitely generated torsion-free modules over a PID are free. Not true over a non-PID.

Structure theorem over a PID

Let \(R\) be a PID and \(M\) finitely generated. Then \(M\cong R^r\oplus R/(d_1)\oplus\dots\oplus R/(d_k),\quad d_1\mid d_2\mid\dots\mid d_k,\) (invariant factor form), or equivalently a sum of \(R^r\) and cyclic modules \(R/(p_i^{e_i})\) for primes \(p_i\) (elementary divisor form). The rank \(r\) and the \(d_i\) (up to units) are unique.

Applications:

  • Abelian groups (\(R=\mathbb Z\)): a finitely generated abelian group is \(\mathbb Z^r\oplus\) finite. A finite abelian group of order \(p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_k^{a_k}\) is a product of its Sylow parts, and the number of such groups is \(\prod_i P(a_i)\), where \(P(a)\) is the number of partitions of \(a\). For \(72=2^3\cdot3^2\): \(P(3)P(2)=3\cdot2=6\).
  • Reading off the group from a presentation: \(\mathbb Z^2/\langle(2,4),(0,6)\rangle\): the relation matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\0&6\end{pmatrix}\) has gcd of entries \(2\) and determinant \(12\), so invariant factors \(2,6\): the group is \(\mathbb Z_2\oplus\mathbb Z_6\), not cyclic. By contrast \(\mathbb Z^2/\langle(2,0),(0,3)\rangle\cong\mathbb Z_6\) (invariant factors \(1,6\)).
  • Canonical forms: for \(V=F^n\) with operator \(T\), the invariant factors of the \(F[x]\)-module \(V\) are the invariant factors of \(xI-A\); the last one is the minimal polynomial, their product is the characteristic polynomial. Rational canonical form = invariant-factor form; Jordan form = elementary-divisor form (when the eigenvalues lie in \(F\)).
  • \(\mathbb Z_m\oplus\mathbb Z_n\cong\mathbb Z_{mn}\) iff \(\gcd(m,n)=1\); \(\mathbb Z_4\oplus\mathbb Z_6\cong\mathbb Z_2\oplus\mathbb Z_{12}\).
  • CRT for modules: \(\mathbb Q[x]/(x^2(x-1))\cong\mathbb Q[x]/(x^2)\oplus\mathbb Q[x]/(x-1)\) (coprime factors), but it is not \(\mathbb Q[x]/(x)\oplus\mathbb Q[x]/(x)\oplus\mathbb Q[x]/(x-1)\).

Simple modules and length

\(M\neq0\) is simple if its only submodules are \(0\) and \(M\). Over commutative \(R\) the simple modules are \(R/\mathfrak m\) for maximal ideals \(\mathfrak m\). \(\mathbb Z_p\) is a simple \(\mathbb Z\)-module; \(\mathbb Z_n\) is simple iff \(n\) is prime. A module of finite length has a composition series with simple factors; the factors are unique (Jordan-Hölder). Length of \(\mathbb Z_n\) is the number of prime factors counted with multiplicity: \(\mathbb Z_{12}\) has length \(3\).

Noetherian modules and rings

An \(R\)-module \(M\) is Noetherian if it satisfies the ascending chain condition (ACC): every chain \(N_1\subseteq N_2\subseteq\cdots\) of submodules is eventually constant. A ring \(R\) is Noetherian if \(R\) is a Noetherian module over itself, i.e. ACC on ideals.

Equivalent conditions (for a module \(M\)):

  1. ACC on submodules.
  2. Every nonempty set of submodules has a maximal element.
  3. Every submodule of \(M\) is finitely generated.

So a ring is Noetherian iff every ideal is finitely generated (an infinite strictly ascending chain would give a non-f.g. ideal as the union).

Closure properties.

  • Exact sequences: in \(0\to N\to M\to M/N\to0\), \(M\) is Noetherian iff both \(N\) and \(M/N\) are Noetherian.
  • A finite direct sum of Noetherian modules is Noetherian. If \(R\) is a Noetherian ring, every finitely generated \(R\)-module is Noetherian (a quotient of \(R^n\)).
  • A quotient of a Noetherian ring is Noetherian. A finite direct product of Noetherian rings is Noetherian.
  • A subring of a Noetherian ring need not be Noetherian (see below).
  • A surjective endomorphism of a Noetherian module is injective (the chain \(\ker f\subseteq\ker f^2\subseteq\cdots\) stabilises). The converse fails: multiplication by \(2\) on \(\mathbb Z\) is injective but not surjective.
  • Cohen: a commutative ring is Noetherian iff every prime ideal is finitely generated.
  • Hopkins-Levitzki: a ring with \(1\) that is Artinian (DCC on ideals) is Noetherian. The converse fails: \(\mathbb Z\).

Examples of Noetherian rings. Fields; \(\mathbb Z\); every PID (\(F[x]\), \(\mathbb Z[i]\)); \(\mathbb Z_n\) and every finite ring; \(\mathbb Z[x]\), \(F[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) (Hilbert); \(\mathbb Z[x_1,\dots,x_n]\); quotients of these; \(R[[x]]\) for Noetherian \(R\); finitely generated commutative algebras over a field or over \(\mathbb Z\).

Non-Noetherian rings (the traps).

Ring Why not Noetherian
\(F[x_1,x_2,x_3,\dots]\) (infinitely many variables) \((x_1)\subsetneq(x_1,x_2)\subsetneq(x_1,x_2,x_3)\subsetneq\cdots\)
\(C[0,1]\) \(J_n=\{f:f=0\text{ on }[0,1/n]\}\) gives \(J_1\subsetneq J_2\subsetneq\cdots\)
\(\mathbb Z+x\mathbb Q[x]\) \((x)\subsetneq(x/2)\subsetneq(x/4)\subsetneq\cdots\), though \(\mathbb Q[x]\) is Noetherian
ring of all real functions on an infinite set \(\{x_1,x_2,\dots\}\) ideals of functions supported on \(\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}\) grow strictly with \(n\)

In \(C[0,1]\) the interval \([0,1/n]\) shrinks as \(n\) grows, so the condition “\(f=0\) on \([0,1/n]\)” weakens and the ideals grow; a continuous \(f\) that vanishes on \([0,\frac1{n+1}]\) but not everywhere on \([0,\frac1n]\) lies in \(J_{n+1}\setminus J_n\).

Non-Noetherian modules. \(\mathbb Q\) and \(\mathbb Z[1/2]\) over \(\mathbb Z\) (chain \(\mathbb Z\subsetneq\frac12\mathbb Z\subsetneq\frac14\mathbb Z\subsetneq\cdots\)); the Prüfer group \(\mathbb Z(p^\infty)\) over \(\mathbb Z\) (Artinian, not Noetherian); \(\mathbb Z^{(\infty)}=\bigoplus_{\mathbb N}\mathbb Z\); \(\mathbb Q\) over \(\mathbb Q\) is Noetherian (a one-dimensional vector space); a finite-dimensional vector space is Noetherian and Artinian, an infinite-dimensional one is neither.

A Noetherian domain is atomic: every nonzero nonunit is a product of irreducibles. Uniqueness of factorisation is not automatic: \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\) is Noetherian but not a UFD.

Hilbert basis theorem

Theorem. If \(R\) is a Noetherian ring (commutative with \(1\)), then \(R[x]\) is Noetherian.

Proof idea: for an ideal \(I\subseteq R[x]\), let \(L_k\) be the ideal of leading coefficients of elements of \(I\) of degree \(\le k\) (including \(0\)). The \(L_k\) form an ascending chain in \(R\), stabilising at some \(L_N\). Choose finitely many polynomials in \(I\) whose leading coefficients generate each \(L_k\) for \(k\le N\); they generate \(I\) by induction on degree.

Corollaries.

  • \(R[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) is Noetherian by induction. So \(\mathbb Z[x]\), \(\mathbb Z[x,y]\), \(F[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) are Noetherian; every ideal there is finitely generated (e.g. \((2,x)\subset\mathbb Z[x]\)).
  • Every finitely generated commutative algebra over a Noetherian ring is Noetherian (a quotient of a polynomial ring).
  • \(R[[x]]\) is Noetherian for Noetherian \(R\).
  • Fails for infinitely many variables: \(F[x_1,x_2,\dots]\) is not Noetherian. Hilbert’s theorem gives finite generation of ideals, not of the ring; it also does not say \(R[x]\) is a PID (\(\mathbb Z[x]\) is Noetherian and not a PID).
  • Hilbert basis theorem also implies the ideal of a polynomial system is generated by finitely many polynomials: any algebraic set is the zero set of finitely many polynomials.

Worked examples

Example 1: counting module homomorphisms

\(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(\mathbb Z_{12},\mathbb Z_{18})\): send \(1\mapsto a\in\mathbb Z_{18}\) with \(12a\equiv0\pmod{18}\), i.e. \(2a\equiv0\pmod3\), i.e. \(3\mid a\): \(a\in\{0,3,6,9,12,15\}\). Six homomorphisms, \(=\gcd(12,18)\). (Contrast with ring homomorphisms \(\mathbb Z_{12}\to\mathbb Z_{18}\): only \(2\), since \(\varphi(1)\) must be idempotent.) Also \(\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(\mathbb Z_8,\mathbb Z_{12})\) has \(\gcd=4\) elements.

Example 2: the ideal \((2,x)\subset\mathbb Z[x]\) is not a free \(\mathbb Z[x]\)-module

The ideal \(I=(2,x)\) is generated by \(2\) and \(x\). Any two elements \(a,b\in I\) satisfy the nontrivial relation \(ba+(-a)b=0\) (coefficients \(b,-a\in\mathbb Z[x]\), not both zero since \(\mathbb Z[x]\) is a domain and \(a,b\neq0\)). So a basis of \(I\) has at most one element, and then \(I\) would be principal, which it is not (Polynomial topic). Hence \(I\) is not free, although it is a submodule of the free module \(\mathbb Z[x]\). This is why “submodules of free modules are free” needs a PID.

Example 3: \(\mathbb Q\) is not a Noetherian \(\mathbb Z\)-module

The chain \(\mathbb Z\subsetneq\tfrac12\mathbb Z\subsetneq\tfrac14\mathbb Z\subsetneq\tfrac18\mathbb Z\subsetneq\cdots\) of \(\mathbb Z\)-submodules of \(\mathbb Q\) never stabilises (\(\tfrac1{2^{k+1}}\notin\tfrac1{2^k}\mathbb Z\)). Equivalently, \(\bigcup_k\tfrac1{2^k}\mathbb Z=\mathbb Z[\tfrac12]\) is a submodule of \(\mathbb Q\) that is not finitely generated. (Over the field \(\mathbb Q\) itself, \(\mathbb Q\) is Noetherian.)

Example 4: number of abelian groups and invariant factors

Abelian groups of order \(72=2^3\cdot3^2\): partitions of \(3\) (three) times partitions of \(2\) (two) \(=6\). The \(2\)-part is one of \(\mathbb Z_8\), \(\mathbb Z_4\oplus\mathbb Z_2\), \(\mathbb Z_2^3\) and the \(3\)-part is one of \(\mathbb Z_9\), \(\mathbb Z_3^2\); the six combinations are the six groups. Invariant factors of \(\mathbb Z_4\oplus\mathbb Z_6\): elementary divisors \(4,2,3\), so \(\mathbb Z_{12}\oplus\mathbb Z_2\).

Example 5: an \(F[x]\)-module

Let \(M=\mathbb Q[x]/(x^3-x^2)\) and note \(x^3-x^2=x^2(x-1)\). By CRT, \(M\cong\mathbb Q[x]/(x^2)\oplus\mathbb Q[x]/(x-1)\); \(\dim_{\mathbb Q}M=3\). As an operator (\(T=\) multiplication by \(x\)) it has Jordan blocks \(J_2(0)\oplus J_1(1)\) and minimal polynomial \(x^2(x-1)\). It is cyclic (generated by \(\bar1\)). \(\mathbb Q[x]/(x)\oplus\mathbb Q[x]/(x)\oplus\mathbb Q[x]/(x-1)\) is a different module of the same dimension (minimal polynomial \(x(x-1)\)).

Example 6: Noetherian or not?

(a) \(\mathbb Z[x,y,z]\): Noetherian (Hilbert, three times). (b) \(\mathbb Q[x_1,x_2,\dots]\): not Noetherian, \((x_1)\subsetneq(x_1,x_2)\subsetneq\cdots\). (c) \(\mathbb Z+x\mathbb Q[x]\): not Noetherian, even though it sits inside the Noetherian ring \(\mathbb Q[x]\). (d) \(\mathbb Z_6[x]\): Noetherian (\(\mathbb Z_6\) is a finite ring). (e) \(\mathbb Z[x]/(x^2+1)=\mathbb Z[i]\): Noetherian (quotient of a Noetherian ring).

Common traps

  • Subrings of Noetherian rings. Quotients are Noetherian; subrings need not be (\(\mathbb Z+x\mathbb Q[x]\subset\mathbb Q[x]\); also \(F[x,xy,xy^2,\dots]\subset F[x,y]\)).
  • Hilbert is about finitely many variables. \(R[x_1,x_2,\dots]\) is not Noetherian even for \(R\) a field.
  • \(\mathbb Q\) over \(\mathbb Z\) vs over \(\mathbb Q\). Noetherian over \(\mathbb Q\) (dimension \(1\)), not over \(\mathbb Z\).
  • Free vs projective vs torsion-free. \(\mathbb Z_n\) is torsion, so not free over \(\mathbb Z\); \(\mathbb Q\) is torsion-free but not free; \(\mathbb Z_2\) is a non-free summand of \(\mathbb Z_6\) over \(\mathbb Z_6\).
  • Submodules of free modules. Free over a PID; not over \(\mathbb Z[x]\) or \(\mathbb Z_6\) (\(2\mathbb Z_6\cong\mathbb Z_3\) is not free).
  • Homomorphism count. \(\gcd(m,n)\) for \(\mathbb Z\)-module homs \(\mathbb Z_m\to\mathbb Z_n\). For ring homs the answer is different (idempotents).
  • Noetherian is not PID. \(\mathbb Z[x]\) and \(F[x,y]\) are Noetherian but not PIDs; \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\) is Noetherian but not a UFD.
  • Injective vs surjective. Surjective endomorphisms of Noetherian modules are injective; the converse is false (\(\times2\) on \(\mathbb Z\)). For Artinian modules the converse holds instead.
  • Noetherian and Artinian are independent for modules: \(\mathbb Z\) is Noetherian not Artinian; \(\mathbb Z(p^\infty)\) is Artinian not Noetherian. Rings with \(1\): Artinian implies Noetherian.
  • Rank-nullity does not hold for modules: \(\mathbb Z\xrightarrow{\times2}\mathbb Z\) has kernel \(0\) and cokernel \(\mathbb Z_2\).

Quick revision

  • \(\mathbb Z\)-modules = abelian groups; \(F[x]\)-modules = (vector space, linear operator); submodules = invariant subspaces.
  • Noetherian module \(\iff\) ACC \(\iff\) every submodule finitely generated.
  • Hilbert basis theorem: \(R\) Noetherian \(\Rightarrow R[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) Noetherian; fails for infinitely many variables.
  • PID, field, finite ring, \(\mathbb Z[x]\), \(F[x,y]\): Noetherian. \(F[x_1,x_2,\dots]\), \(C[0,1]\), \(\mathbb Z+x\mathbb Q[x]\): not.
  • \(N\) and \(M/N\) Noetherian \(\iff\) \(M\) Noetherian; f.g. modules over Noetherian rings are Noetherian.
  • \(\mathbb Q\) is not a Noetherian (or f.g., or free) \(\mathbb Z\)-module.
  • Surjective endomorphism of a Noetherian module is injective; \(\times2\) on \(\mathbb Z\) is injective, not surjective.
  • \(\#\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb Z}(\mathbb Z_m,\mathbb Z_n)=\gcd(m,n)\).
  • Submodules of free modules over a PID are free; \((2,x)\subset\mathbb Z[x]\) is not free.
  • F.g. module over a PID \(=R^r\oplus\bigoplus R/(d_i)\) with \(d_1\mid\dots\mid d_k\).
  • Number of abelian groups of order \(\prod p_i^{a_i}\) is \(\prod P(a_i)\); order \(72\) gives \(6\).
  • Simple \(R\)-modules (\(R\) commutative) are \(R/\mathfrak m\).
