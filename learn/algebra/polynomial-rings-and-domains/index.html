Why it matters for the exam

This topic carries the “when is \(F[x]/(f)\) a field” question (2026 Q36 asked it for \(\mathbb Z_5[x]/(x^2+2)\)) and the standard set of true/false statements on ED, PID and UFD, with \(\mathbb Z[x]\) and \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\) as the counterexamples. Irreducibility tests (Eisenstein, reduction mod \(p\), rational roots) appear in almost every algebra section of NET-level papers. Gaussian integers are the favourite concrete example.

Core ideas

The polynomial ring \(R[x]\)

For a commutative ring \(R\) with \(1\), \(R[x]\) is the set of polynomials with coefficients in \(R\).

  • If \(R\) is an integral domain then \(\deg(fg)=\deg f+\deg g\), \(R[x]\) is an integral domain, and the units of \(R[x]\) are the units of \(R\). In particular \(\mathbb Z[x]^{*}=\{\pm1\}\) and \(F[x]^{*}=F\setminus\{0\}\).
  • If \(R\) is not a domain this fails: in \(\mathbb Z_4[x]\), \((1+2x)^2=1\), so \(1+2x\) is a unit; \((2x)(2x)=0\); \(\deg(fg)<\deg f+\deg g\) is possible.
  • \(R[x]\) is never a field (the element \(x\) has no inverse). \(R[x]\) is a PID iff \(R\) is a field. \(R[x]\) is a UFD if \(R\) is a UFD (Gauss). \(R[x]\) is Noetherian if \(R\) is (Hilbert, see modules).
  • A polynomial over an integral domain of degree \(n\) has at most \(n\) roots in the domain. Over \(\mathbb Z_8\), \(x^2-1\) has four roots (\(1,3,5,7\)): the bound needs a domain.
  • \(a\) is a root of \(f\) iff \((x-a)\mid f\) (factor theorem; the remainder of \(f\) on division by \(x-a\) is \(f(a)\)).

Division algorithm and gcd

Division algorithm. Let \(F\) be a field (more generally let \(g\) have a unit leading coefficient). For \(f,g\in F[x]\), \(g\neq0\), there are unique \(q,r\) with \(f=qg+r\) and \(r=0\) or \(\deg r<\deg g\).

Consequences: \(F[x]\) is a Euclidean domain with \(d(f)=\deg f\); every ideal is \((g)\) with \(g\) of least degree in it; gcds exist and \(\gcd(f,g)=uf+vg\) (Bezout); the gcd does not change when \(F\) is enlarged. For example \(\gcd(x^4-1,x^6-1)=x^2-1\) in \(\mathbb Q[x]\).

The division algorithm fails over \(\mathbb Z[x]\): dividing \(x\) by \(2\) leaves no remainder of smaller degree. \(\mathbb Z[x]\) is not Euclidean.

Irreducibility: definition and tests

\(f\in F[x]\) of degree \(\ge1\) is irreducible if \(f=gh\Rightarrow g\) or \(h\) is a constant (unit). Reducible means a product of two nonconstants.

Situation Rule
degree \(1\) always irreducible
degree \(2\) or \(3\) over a field irreducible iff no root in \(F\)
degree \(\ge4\) no root does not suffice (\(x^4+4\), \(x^4+x^2+1\) have no rational roots yet factor)
over \(\mathbb C\) only linear polynomials are irreducible
over \(\mathbb R\) linear, or quadratic with negative discriminant
over \(\mathbb Q\) irreducible polynomials of every degree exist (\(x^n-2\))

Gauss’s lemma. A primitive polynomial in \(\mathbb Z[x]\) is irreducible in \(\mathbb Z[x]\) iff it is irreducible in \(\mathbb Q[x]\). (Primitive: gcd of coefficients is \(1\).) So rational factorisation can be done with integer factors.

Rational root theorem. If \(p/q\) in lowest terms is a root of \(a_nx^n+\dots+a_0\in\mathbb Z[x]\), then \(p\mid a_0\) and \(q\mid a_n\). A monic integer polynomial has only integer rational roots.

Eisenstein’s criterion. Let \(f=a_nx^n+\dots+a_0\in\mathbb Z[x]\) and \(p\) prime with \(p\nmid a_n\), \(p\mid a_i\) for \(i<n\), \(p^2\nmid a_0\). Then \(f\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\). Examples: \(x^n-p\); \(x^3+6x^2+10\) with \(p=2\). Try a shift \(f(x+c)\) when the plain test fails:

  • \(\Phi_p(x)=x^{p-1}+\dots+x+1\): \(\Phi_p(x+1)=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\binom pk\,x^{k-1}\) (leading coefficient \(1\), constant term \(p\), all middle coefficients divisible by \(p\)) is Eisenstein at \(p\). So \(\Phi_p\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\) for every prime \(p\).

Reduction mod \(p\). If \(p\nmid a_n\) and \(\bar f\) is irreducible in \(\mathbb Z_p[x]\), then \(f\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\). Converse false. Example: \(x^3+x+1\) has no root in \(\mathbb Z_2\) (\(f(0)=f(1)=1\)), so it is irreducible mod \(2\), hence irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\). A factorisation mod \(p\) tells nothing about \(\mathbb Q\).

Famous trap polynomials.

  • \(x^4+1\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\) (it is \(\Phi_8\); \(f(x+1)=x^4+4x^3+6x^2+4x+2\) is Eisenstein at \(2\)) but reducible modulo every prime. So mod-\(p\) tests can fail for every \(p\).
  • \(x^4+x^2+1=(x^2+x+1)(x^2-x+1)\) is reducible though it has no real roots.
  • \(x^4+4=(x^2+2x+2)(x^2-2x+2)\) (Sophie Germain identity).
  • \(x^2+1\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb R\) and over \(\mathbb Z_p\) iff \(p\equiv3\pmod4\) (over \(\mathbb Z_2\): \((x+1)^2\); over \(\mathbb Z_5\): \((x-2)(x-3)\)).

Showing a quartic is irreducible over \(\mathbb F_p\). A quartic is reducible iff it has a root (a linear factor) or is a product of two quadratics. So check two things: (1) no root in \(\mathbb F_p\), by evaluating at every element; (2) it is not a product of two monic irreducible quadratics, by trying each monic quadratic that has no root (there are \((p^2-p)/2\) of them) or by comparing coefficients. Example: \(x^4+x+1\) over \(\mathbb F_2\) has \(f(0)=f(1)=1\), and the only irreducible quadratic is \(x^2+x+1\), whose square is \(x^4+x^2+1\neq x^4+x+1\). So it is irreducible, and \(\mathbb F_2[x]/(x^4+x+1)\) is a field of order \(16\). In contrast \(x^4+x^2+1=(x^2+x+1)^2\) over \(\mathbb F_2\), and \(x^4+1=(x^2+x+2)(x^2+2x+2)\) over \(\mathbb F_3\), both without roots yet reducible.

Irreducible polynomials over finite fields. Over \(\mathbb F_q\) there are irreducibles of every degree. The number of monic irreducibles of degree \(n\) is

\[N_q(n)=\frac1n\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\,q^{n/d}.\]

Over \(\mathbb F_2\): \(N(2)=1\) (\(x^2+x+1\)), \(N(3)=2\) (\(x^3+x+1\), \(x^3+x^2+1\)), \(N(4)=3\) (\(x^4+x+1\), \(x^4+x^3+1\), \(x^4+x^3+x^2+x+1\)), \(N(6)=9\). Over \(\mathbb F_3\): \(N(2)=3\), \(N(3)=8\). Over \(\mathbb F_5\): \(N(2)=10\).

When is \(F[x]/(f)\) a field?

Let \(F\) be a field and \(f\in F[x]\) of degree \(n\ge1\).

  • \(F[x]/(f)\) is a field \(\iff\) an integral domain \(\iff f\) is irreducible \(\iff(f)\) is maximal. (Since \(F[x]\) is a PID, nonzero prime ideals are maximal, and \((f)\) prime iff \(f\) is prime iff irreducible.)
  • As a vector space over \(F\), \(F[x]/(f)\) has basis \(1,\bar x,\dots,\bar x^{n-1}\), so its order is \(\lvert F\rvert^{n}\) when \(F\) is finite.
  • If \(f=gh\) with \(\gcd(g,h)=1\), then \(F[x]/(f)\cong F[x]/(g)\times F[x]/(h)\) (CRT): not a domain.
  • If \(f\) has a repeated factor, \(F[x]/(f)\) has nilpotents.

Examples:

  • \(\mathbb Z_5[x]/(x^2+2)\): squares mod \(5\) are \(0,1,4\) and \(-2\equiv3\) is not a square, so no root, so irreducible: a field of \(25\) elements (2026 Q36).
  • \(\mathbb Z_5[x]/(x^2+1)\): \(x^2+1=(x-2)(x-3)\), so the quotient is \(\cong\mathbb Z_5\times\mathbb Z_5\), not a field.
  • \(\mathbb Z_p[x]/(x^2+1)\) is a field iff \(p\equiv3\pmod4\) (for \(p<25\) these are \(3,7,11,19,23\)).
  • \(\mathbb Z_2[x]/(x^2+x+1)=\mathbb F_4\); \(\mathbb Z_2[x]/(x^3+x+1)=\mathbb F_8\); \(\mathbb Z_3[x]/(x^3-x+1)=\mathbb F_{27}\).
  • \(\mathbb Q[x]/(x^2-2)\cong\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\); \(\mathbb R[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb C\); \(\mathbb Q[x]/(x^3-2)\cong\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2)\).
  • \(\mathbb Z_2[x]/(x^2+1)\) is not a field: \((x+1)^2=0\).

Ideals of \(F[x]\)

Every ideal is principal. \((f)\subseteq(g)\iff g\mid f\). \((f)+(g)=(\gcd)\), \((f)\cap(g)=(\operatorname{lcm})\). Ideals of \(F[x]/(f)\) correspond to monic divisors of \(f\). The maximal ideals of \(F[x]\) are \((p)\) with \(p\) irreducible. For \(F\) algebraically closed they are exactly \((x-a)\).

Factorisation domains: definitions

In an integral domain \(D\):

  • \(a\) is irreducible if \(a\) is a nonzero nonunit and \(a=bc\Rightarrow b\) or \(c\) is a unit.
  • \(a\) is prime if \(a\) is a nonzero nonunit and \(a\mid bc\Rightarrow a\mid b\) or \(a\mid c\). (Equivalent: \((a)\) is a nonzero prime ideal.)
  • UFD: every nonzero nonunit is a product of irreducibles, uniquely up to order and units.
  • PID: every ideal is principal.
  • ED: there is \(d:D\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb N\) with division with remainder (\(a=qb+r\), \(r=0\) or \(d(r)<d(b)\)).

Theorems.

  1. In any domain, prime \(\Rightarrow\) irreducible. The converse holds in a UFD (and in a PID), where “irreducible \(\iff\) prime”.
  2. \(\text{field}\subset\text{ED}\subset\text{PID}\subset\text{UFD}\subset\text{domain}\), and all inclusions are strict.
  3. In a PID: every nonzero prime ideal is maximal; \((a)\) maximal iff \(a\) irreducible; gcds exist and are linear combinations; ACC holds on ideals (so factorisation into irreducibles always exists).
  4. \(D\) UFD \(\Rightarrow D[x]\) UFD (Gauss). Hence \(\mathbb Z[x]\) and \(F[x_1,\dots,x_n]\) are UFDs.
  5. \(D[x]\) is a PID \(\iff D\) is a field. \(D[x]\) is an ED \(\iff D\) is a field.
  6. In a UFD, any two nonzero elements have a gcd. In a Noetherian domain every nonzero nonunit is a product of irreducibles (existence), but uniqueness may fail.

Counterexamples (memorise these):

Ring UFD? PID? ED? Reason
\(\mathbb Z\), \(F[x]\) yes yes yes division algorithm
\(\mathbb Z[i]\), \(\mathbb Z[\omega]\) (\(\omega=e^{2\pi i/3}\)), \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-2}]\) yes yes yes norm is the Euclidean function
\(\mathbb Z[\frac{1+\sqrt{-19}}2]\) yes yes no PID which is not Euclidean (Motzkin)
\(\mathbb Z[x]\) yes no no \((2,x)\) is not principal
\(F[x,y]\) yes no no \((x,y)\) is not principal
\(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\) no no no \(6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})\)
\(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-3}]\) no no no \(4=2\cdot2=(1+\sqrt{-3})(1-\sqrt{-3})\)

Why \(\mathbb Z[x]\) is not a PID. Suppose \((2,x)=(g)\). Then \(g\mid2\) so \(g=\pm1,\pm2\); \(g\mid x\) forces \(g=\pm1\); but \((1)\ni1\) and \(1\notin(2,x)\) (every element has even constant term). This also follows from rule 5: \(\mathbb Z\) is not a field.

Why \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\) is not a UFD. Norm \(N(a+b\sqrt{-5})=a^2+5b^2\) is multiplicative; units are the elements of norm \(1\), namely \(\pm1\). Norms of \(2,3,1\pm\sqrt{-5}\) are \(4,9,6,6\). No element has norm \(2\) or \(3\) (\(a^2+5b^2=2\) or \(3\) is impossible: \(b=0\) gives \(a^2\in\{2,3\}\), and \(b\ne0\) makes \(a^2+5b^2\ge5\)), so each of the four is irreducible (a proper factorisation would need norms \(2\cdot2\) or \(3\cdot3\) or \(2\cdot3\)). Then \(2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})\) gives two different factorisations, since \(2\) is not an associate of \(1\pm\sqrt{-5}\). Also \(2\) is irreducible but not prime: \(2\mid(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})=6\) yet \(2\) divides neither factor.

Imaginary quadratic rings of integers. The ring of integers of \(\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-d})\) (\(d>0\) square-free) is norm-Euclidean for \(d=1,2,3,7,11\) and a PID (class number \(1\)) exactly for \(d=1,2,3,7,11,19,43,67,163\). For \(d=5\) the ring of integers is \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\) (class number \(2\)), not a UFD.

Real quadratic rings. For squarefree \(d>1\) the norm \(N(a+b\sqrt d)=a^2-db^2\) can be negative, and \(\lvert N\rvert\) is the Euclidean function when the ring is norm-Euclidean: \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt2]\) and \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt3]\) are Euclidean, hence PIDs and UFDs. Units are the elements of norm \(\pm1\), and for \(d>1\) there are infinitely many: \(1+\sqrt2\) has norm \(-1\) and all its powers are units (Pell’s equation). By contrast \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-d}]\) with \(d\ge2\) has only the units \(\pm1\). The ring \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt5]\) is not a UFD: \(4=2\cdot2=(\sqrt5+1)(\sqrt5-1)\), where \(a^2-5b^2=\pm2\) has no solution (squares mod \(5\) are \(0,1,4\)) so \(2\) and \(\sqrt5\pm1\) (norm \(-4\)) are irreducible, and \(\sqrt5+1=2u\) would need \(u=\frac{1+\sqrt5}2\notin\mathbb Z[\sqrt5]\). The larger ring \(\mathbb Z[\frac{1+\sqrt5}2]\) is a PID, so the choice of ring matters.

Gaussian integers \(\mathbb Z[i]\)

\(\mathbb Z[i]=\{a+bi:a,b\in\mathbb Z\}\), norm \(N(a+bi)=a^2+b^2\), multiplicative.

  • Euclidean with \(d=N\): for \(\alpha,\beta\neq0\) round \(\alpha/\beta\) to the nearest Gaussian integer; the remainder has \(N(r)\le N(\beta)/2\). Hence PID and UFD.
  • Units: \(\pm1,\pm i\) (norm \(1\)): four units.
  • Primes (up to unit multiples):
    1. \(1+i\) (and \(2=-i(1+i)^2\) ramifies);
    2. rational primes \(p\equiv3\pmod4\) stay prime (inert), e.g. \(3,7,11\);
    3. each rational prime \(p\equiv1\pmod4\) factors as \(p=(a+bi)(a-bi)\) with \(p=a^2+b^2\), e.g. \(5=(2+i)(2-i)\), \(13=(3+2i)(3-2i)\).
  • A Gaussian integer is prime iff its norm is a rational prime, or it is a unit times a rational prime \(\equiv3\pmod4\).
  • Fermat: an odd prime is a sum of two squares iff \(p\equiv1\pmod4\). A positive integer is a sum of two squares iff every prime \(\equiv3\pmod4\) divides it to an even power.
  • \(\lvert\mathbb Z[i]/(\alpha)\rvert=N(\alpha)\). For \(\alpha\) a Gaussian prime, \(\mathbb Z[i]/(\alpha)\) is a field; for \(\alpha=3\) it is \(\mathbb F_9\); for \(\alpha=2+i\) it is \(\mathbb Z_5\); \(\mathbb Z[i]/(p)\cong\mathbb Z_p\times\mathbb Z_p\) when \(p\equiv1\pmod4\); \(\mathbb Z[i]/(2)\) is not a domain.
  • Eisenstein integers \(\mathbb Z[\omega]\) are also Euclidean (norm \(a^2-ab+b^2\)) with \(6\) units.

Content, primitive polynomials, CRT

The content of \(f\in\mathbb Z[x]\) is the gcd of its coefficients; content is multiplicative (Gauss). \(f\) is primitive if content is \(1\). \(f\in\mathbb Q[x]\) factors as \(f=c\,f_0\) with \(c\in\mathbb Q\) and \(f_0\) primitive in \(\mathbb Z[x]\), uniquely up to sign. CRT in \(F[x]\): coprime moduli give \(F[x]/(fg)\cong F[x]/(f)\times F[x]/(g)\).

Worked examples

Example 1: irreducibility over \(\mathbb Q\)

(a) \(f=x^4+x^3+x^2+x+1=\Phi_5\). \(f(x+1)=x^4+5x^3+10x^2+10x+5\), Eisenstein at \(5\). Irreducible. Cross-check: \(f\) mod \(2\) has no roots and is not \((x^2+x+1)^2=x^4+x^2+1\), so it is irreducible over \(\mathbb Z_2\) (verified by search), hence over \(\mathbb Q\). (b) \(x^4+x^2+1\): factors as \((x^2+x+1)(x^2-x+1)\). Reducible. (c) \(2x^5-6x^3+9x-15\): the non-leading coefficients \(0,-6,0,9,-15\) are all divisible by \(3\), the leading coefficient \(2\) is not, and \(9\nmid15\). Eisenstein at \(p=3\): irreducible. (d) \(x^3+3x+1\) has possible rational roots \(\pm1\): values \(5,-3\). No root, degree \(3\), so irreducible.

Example 2: is \(\mathbb Z_3[x]/(x^3-x+1)\) a field? How many elements?

Roots in \(\mathbb Z_3\): \(f(0)=1\), \(f(1)=1\), \(f(2)=7\equiv1\). No root and degree \(3\), so irreducible. The quotient is a field with \(3^3=27\) elements. Here \(x^3-x-a\) (\(a\neq0\)) is irreducible over \(\mathbb F_3\) for both \(a=\pm1\).

Example 3: \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\) is not a UFD

See the core section: norms \(4,9,6,6\) are irreducible, \(6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})\) gives two inequivalent factorisations. So ED, PID, UFD all fail.

Example 4: Gaussian integer quotient

\(\mathbb Z[i]/(2+3i)\). \(N(2+3i)=13\), a rational prime, so \(2+3i\) is a Gaussian prime and the quotient is a field with \(13\) elements, \(\cong\mathbb Z_{13}\). Likewise \(\lvert\mathbb Z[i]/(3+4i)\rvert=25\); since \(3+4i=(2+i)^2\) (check: \(4+4i+i^2=3+4i\)) it is not a field; it is not a domain either.

Example 5: gcd in \(\mathbb Q[x]\)

\(x^4-1=(x^2-1)(x^2+1)\), \(x^6-1=(x^2-1)(x^4+x^2+1)\). \(x^4+x^2+1=x^2(x^2+1)+1\), so \(\gcd(x^2+1,x^4+x^2+1)=1\). Hence \(\gcd(x^4-1,x^6-1)=x^2-1\). By Bezout \(x^2-1=a(x^4-1)+b(x^6-1)\) with \(a,b\in\mathbb Q[x]\).

Example 6: factor over \(\mathbb Z_5\)

\(x^2+1\) at \(x=2\): \(5\equiv0\); at \(x=3\): \(10\equiv0\). So \(x^2+1=(x-2)(x-3)\) in \(\mathbb Z_5[x]\) and \(\mathbb Z_5[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb Z_5\times\mathbb Z_5\) (CRT): it has zero divisors. Contrast with \(x^2+2\): no root, field.

Common traps

  • No root \(\neq\) irreducible for degree \(\ge4\). \(x^4+4\) and \(x^4+x^2+1\) are reducible over \(\mathbb Q\) with no rational roots, and \(x^4+1\) is reducible over \(\mathbb R\) (\((x^2+\sqrt2x+1)(x^2-\sqrt2x+1)\)) with no real roots.
  • Reduction mod \(p\) goes one way. Irreducible mod \(p\) \(\Rightarrow\) irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\) (with \(p\nmid a_n\)). \(x^4+1\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\) but reducible mod every prime.
  • Eisenstein is sufficient, not necessary. Failure means “try a shift”, not “reducible”.
  • \(F[x]/(f)\) is a field only for irreducible \(f\). \(x^2+1\) over \(\mathbb Z_5\) and \(x^2-1\) over any field fail.
  • Order of \(F[x]/(f)\) is \(\lvert F\rvert^{\deg f}\), not \(\lvert F\rvert\cdot\deg f\).
  • ED \(\subset\) PID \(\subset\) UFD, not the reverse. \(\mathbb Z[x]\) is a UFD but not a PID. \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\) is none. Do not confuse “\(\mathbb Z[x]\) is not a PID” with “not a UFD”.
  • PID not ED exists (\(\mathbb Z[\frac{1+\sqrt{-19}}2]\)): the chain inclusions are strict.
  • Irreducible vs prime. Equal in a UFD; in \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\), \(2\) is irreducible but not prime.
  • Statements about \(R[x]\). “\(R[x]\) is a PID iff \(R\) is a field” is true; “\(R[x]\) is a PID iff \(R\) is a PID” is false (\(R=\mathbb Z\)); “\(R\) UFD \(\Rightarrow R[x]\) UFD” is true; “\(R\) PID \(\Rightarrow R[x]\) PID” is false.
  • Gaussian primes. \(5\) is not a Gaussian prime (\(=(2+i)(2-i)\)), while \(7\) is. \(2\) is not prime (\(=-i(1+i)^2\)).
  • Roots. “At most \(n\) roots” requires a domain; \(x^2-1\) has four roots in \(\mathbb Z_8\).

2026 Q36 (worked key: A)

Is \(\mathbb Z_5[x]/(x^2+2)\) a field, and what is its order? Answer: a field of order \(25\). \(x^2+2\) has no root mod \(5\) (squares mod \(5\) are \(0,1,4\); \(-2\equiv3\)), so it is irreducible of degree \(2\) over the field \(\mathbb Z_5\). Then \(\mathbb Z_5[x]/(x^2+2)\) is a field with \(5^2=25\) elements. (Unofficial worked key; the question asks both whether it is a field and for the order.)

Quick revision

  • \(F[x]/(f)\) is a field iff \(f\) is irreducible; it then has \(\lvert F\rvert^{\deg f}\) elements.
  • Degree \(2\) or \(3\) over a field: irreducible iff no root. Degree \(4\)+: not enough.
  • Eisenstein at \(p\): \(p\nmid a_n\), \(p\mid a_i\), \(p^2\nmid a_0\). Shift \(x\to x+1\) proves \(\Phi_p\) irreducible.
  • Irreducible mod \(p\) (\(p\nmid a_n\)) \(\Rightarrow\) irreducible over \(\mathbb Q\); \(x^4+1\) is a counterexample to the converse.
  • Gauss: \(R\) UFD \(\Rightarrow R[x]\) UFD. \(R[x]\) PID iff \(R\) field.
  • Field \(\subset\) ED \(\subset\) PID \(\subset\) UFD \(\subset\) domain, all strict.
  • \(\mathbb Z[x]\): UFD, not PID (\((2,x)\)). \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\): not UFD (\(6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})\)).
  • Prime \(\Rightarrow\) irreducible; equal in a UFD/PID. \(2\) is irreducible, not prime, in \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\).
  • \(\mathbb Z[i]\): ED with \(N=a^2+b^2\); units \(\pm1,\pm i\); \(p\equiv1\pmod4\) splits, \(p\equiv3\pmod4\) is inert.
  • \(\lvert\mathbb Z[i]/(\alpha)\rvert=N(\alpha)\).
  • Quartic over \(\mathbb F_p\): no root and not a product of two irreducible quadratics; \(x^4+x+1\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb F_2\).
  • Real quadratic \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt d]\) has infinitely many units; \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-d}]\), \(d\ge2\), has units \(\pm1\) only.
  • Number of monic irreducibles of degree \(n\) over \(\mathbb F_q\): \(\frac1n\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)q^{n/d}\).
