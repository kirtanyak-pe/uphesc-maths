Why it matters for the exam

Eigenvalue questions are the most reliable of the unit: one or two per paper (2025 Q48; 2026 Q83 and Q98; 2019 two). Together with the canonical-forms topic (2026 Q31, Q93) and quadratic forms they decide the bulk of the unit’s marks. They are mostly short computations: find eigenvalues from trace, determinant or a polynomial identity such as \(A^2=A\), then read off rank, trace or invertibility.

Core ideas

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

For a square matrix \(A\) (or an operator \(T\) on a finite-dimensional \(V\) over \(F\)), a scalar \(\lambda\in F\) is an eigenvalue if \(Av=\lambda v\) for some \(v\ne0\); \(v\) is an eigenvector. Equivalent: \(A-\lambda I\) is singular, \(\det(A-\lambda I)=0\).

The characteristic polynomial is \(\chi_A(x)=\det(xI-A)\). For \(A\) of order \(n\),

\(\chi_A(x)=x^n-(\operatorname{tr}A)x^{n-1}+c_{n-2}x^{n-2}-\cdots+(-1)^n\det A,\)

where \(c_k=(-1)^k\times\) (sum of all principal \(k\times k\) minors). For \(n=2\): \(x^2-(\operatorname{tr}A)x+\det A\). For \(n=3\): \(x^3-(\operatorname{tr}A)x^2+(\text{sum of the three principal }2\times2\text{ minors})x-\det A\).

Over \(\mathbb{C}\) the polynomial has \(n\) roots counted with multiplicity, and

\(\sum\lambda_i=\operatorname{tr}A,\qquad\prod\lambda_i=\det A,\qquad\operatorname{tr}(A^k)=\sum\lambda_i^k.\)

Over \(\mathbb{R}\) a matrix may have no real eigenvalue (rotation by \(90^\circ\) has \(\pm i\)); real matrices have non-real eigenvalues in conjugate pairs, and a real matrix of odd order always has a real eigenvalue.

How eigenvalues transform (with eigenvector unchanged):

Matrix Eigenvalues
\(A^T\) same as \(A\)
\(p(A)\), \(p\) a polynomial \(p(\lambda)\)
\(A^{-1}\) (\(A\) invertible) \(1/\lambda\)
\(\operatorname{adj}A\) \(\det A/\lambda\)
\(A+cI\) \(\lambda+c\)
\(cA\) \(c\lambda\)
Triangular or diagonal matrix the diagonal entries
\(AB\) and \(BA\) (\(A,B\) square) identical characteristic polynomials
Similar matrices identical eigenvalues

\(A\) is invertible iff \(0\) is not an eigenvalue. \(A\) is singular iff \(0\) is an eigenvalue.

Eigenspaces and multiplicities

The eigenspace of \(\lambda\) is \(E_\lambda=\ker(A-\lambda I)\).

  • Algebraic multiplicity \(a(\lambda)\): the multiplicity of \(\lambda\) as a root of \(\chi_A\).
  • Geometric multiplicity \(g(\lambda)=\dim E_\lambda=n-\operatorname{rank}(A-\lambda I)\).
  • Always \(1\le g(\lambda)\le a(\lambda)\).
  • Eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent; so \(E_{\lambda_1}+\cdots+E_{\lambda_k}\) is a direct sum.
  • If \(\operatorname{rank}A=r\) then \(g(0)=n-r\), so \(0\) has algebraic multiplicity at least \(n-r\).

A defective eigenvalue is one with \(g<a\). The matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}\) has \(a(2)=2\) but \(g(2)=1\).

Minimal polynomial

The minimal polynomial \(m_A\) is the monic polynomial of least degree with \(m_A(A)=0\).

  • \(m_A\) divides every polynomial \(p\) with \(p(A)=0\) (in particular divides \(\chi_A\)).
  • \(m_A\) and \(\chi_A\) have the same roots (the eigenvalues), though possibly different multiplicities; \(\deg m_A\le n\).
  • Similar matrices have the same minimal polynomial.
  • For a block diagonal matrix, \(m\) is the least common multiple of the minimal polynomials of the blocks.
  • The multiplicity of \(\lambda\) in \(m_A\) equals the size of the largest Jordan block of \(\lambda\) (see the next topic). So \(m_A=\chi_A\) iff each eigenvalue has a single Jordan block (the matrix is “cyclic”), and in particular if all eigenvalues are distinct.
  • The companion matrix of a monic polynomial \(p\) has \(\chi=m=p\).

Cayley-Hamilton theorem

Every square matrix satisfies its characteristic polynomial: \(\chi_A(A)=0\). (Over any field; here \(\chi_A(A)\) means substitute \(A\) and read the constant term as a multiple of \(I\).)

Uses:

  • Reduce high powers: for \(2\times2\), \(A^2=(\operatorname{tr}A)A-(\det A)I\), so \(A^k=\alpha_kA+\beta_kI\).
  • Inverse: if \(\det A\ne0\), \(A^{-1}\) is a polynomial in \(A\). For \(2\times2\), \(A^{-1}=\dfrac{(\operatorname{tr}A)I-A}{\det A}\).
  • If \(p(A)=0\) for some polynomial \(p\), every eigenvalue of \(A\) is a root of \(p\) (but not every root of \(p\) need be an eigenvalue).

Warning: the “proof” \(\det(A-AI)=0\) is wrong, because \(\det(xI-A)\) with \(x\) replaced by a matrix is not the determinant of a scalar matrix.

Diagonalisability

\(A\) is diagonalisable over \(F\) if \(A=PDP^{-1}\) with \(D\) diagonal, i.e. \(F^n\) has a basis of eigenvectors. The following are equivalent:

  1. \(F^n\) has a basis of eigenvectors of \(A\).
  2. \(\sum_\lambda g(\lambda)=n\) (sum over the eigenvalues in \(F\)).
  3. \(\chi_A\) splits into linear factors over \(F\) and \(g(\lambda)=a(\lambda)\) for every \(\lambda\).
  4. The minimal polynomial is a product of distinct linear factors over \(F\).

Sufficient but not necessary: \(n\) distinct eigenvalues in \(F\). Not sufficient: being invertible, having non-zero determinant, or having a full set of real eigenvalues (the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\) is invertible with eigenvalue \(1\) twice, but \(g=1\)).

More:

  • A matrix that satisfies a polynomial with distinct roots (such as \(A^2=A\), \(A^2=I\), \(A^k=I\)) is diagonalisable over \(\mathbb{C}\).
  • Real symmetric, Hermitian, skew-Hermitian, unitary and real orthogonal matrices (in fact all normal matrices) are diagonalisable over \(\mathbb{C}\); real symmetric ones over \(\mathbb{R}\) by an orthogonal matrix.
  • A diagonalisable matrix with eigenvalue \(0\) (rank \(r\)) has exactly \(n-r\) zero eigenvalues.
  • Commuting diagonalisable matrices are simultaneously diagonalisable.
  • A non-zero nilpotent matrix is never diagonalisable.
  • Diagonalisability depends on the field: a rotation matrix is diagonalisable over \(\mathbb{C}\) but not over \(\mathbb{R}\).

Powers. If \(A=PDP^{-1}\) then \(A^k=PD^kP^{-1}\) and \(p(A)=Pp(D)P^{-1}\).

Special matrices

Condition Consequences
\(A^2=A\) (idempotent) eigenvalues in \(\{0,1\}\); diagonalisable; \(\operatorname{tr}A=\operatorname{rank}A\); \(\det A=1\) if \(A=I\) else \(0\)
\(A^2=I\) (involution) eigenvalues \(\pm1\); diagonalisable; \(\operatorname{tr}A=a_+-a_-\); \(\det A=(-1)^{a_-}\); \(A=2P-I\) with \(P\) idempotent
\(A^k=I\) eigenvalues are \(k\)-th roots of unity; diagonalisable over \(\mathbb{C}\)
\(A^k=0\) (nilpotent) only eigenvalue \(0\); \(\chi=x^n\); \(\operatorname{tr}A=0\); not diagonalisable unless \(A=0\)
\(\operatorname{rank}A=1\), \(A=uv^T\) eigenvalues \(v^Tu\) (once) and \(0\) (\(n-1\) times); \(\chi=x^{n-1}(x-v^Tu)\)
\(A\) real skew-symmetric eigenvalues \(0\) or purely imaginary; \(\det=0\) if \(n\) odd
\(A\) real symmetric or Hermitian all eigenvalues real; eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues orthogonal
\(A\) unitary or real orthogonal \(\lvert\lambda\rvert=1\); \(\det\) of orthogonal is \(\pm1\)
\(A\) stochastic (rows sum to \(1\)) \(1\) is an eigenvalue (eigenvector \((1,\dots,1)^T\))
all-ones matrix \(J_n\) eigenvalues \(n\) and \(0\) (\(n-1\) times)
\(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}\) eigenvalues \(a+b\) and \(a-b\)

Rank-one matrices. For \(A=uv^T\): \(A^2=(v^Tu)A\). If \(v^Tu\ne0\) then \(m_A=x(x-v^Tu)\) and \(A\) is diagonalisable (a scaled projection). If \(v^Tu=0\) then \(A^2=0\) and \(A\) is nilpotent, hence not diagonalisable.

Idempotent and involution. Both are diagonalisable because their minimal polynomials divide \(x(x-1)\) and \((x-1)(x+1)\), which have distinct roots. For a Hermitian \(A\) with \(A^2=I\) in dimension \(4\) with equal multiplicities, the eigenvalues are \(1,1,-1,-1\) and the trace is \(0\).

Polynomial identities. If \(A\) satisfies \(A^3-3A^2+2A=0\), i.e. \(x(x-1)(x-2)\) annihilates \(A\), the eigenvalues lie in \(\{0,1,2\}\), and \(A\) is diagonalisable (distinct roots).

Trace and determinant facts

  • \(\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)\); \(\operatorname{tr}\) and \(\det\) are similarity invariants; \(\operatorname{tr}(A+B)=\operatorname{tr}A+\operatorname{tr}B\), but \(\operatorname{tr}(AB)\ne\operatorname{tr}A\operatorname{tr}B\) in general.
  • \(\det(AB)=\det A\det B\); \(\det A^T=\det A\); \(\det(cA)=c^n\det A\); \(\det(A^{-1})=1/\det A\).
  • \(\det(\operatorname{adj}A)=(\det A)^{n-1}\).
  • \(\det(I+uv^T)=1+v^Tu\).
  • There are no matrices with \(AB-BA=I\) over fields of characteristic \(0\) (trace of the left side is \(0\), of the right side \(n\)).
  • Tridiagonal \(n\times n\) matrix with \(2\) on the diagonal and \(-1\) on the two neighbouring diagonals: \(D_n=2D_{n-1}-D_{n-2}\) with \(D_1=2\), \(D_2=3\), so \(D_n=n+1\). Its eigenvalues are \(2-2\cos\dfrac{k\pi}{n+1}\), \(k=1,\dots,n\).
  • For an invertible \(n\times n\) matrix: rows form a basis, columns form a basis, null space is \(\{0\}\), \(0\) is not an eigenvalue. Nothing forces the eigenvalues to be distinct.

Formulas and results

  • \(\chi_A(x)=\det(xI-A)=x^n-(\operatorname{tr}A)x^{n-1}+\cdots+(-1)^n\det A\).
  • \(\sum\lambda_i=\operatorname{tr}A\), \(\prod\lambda_i=\det A\), \(\operatorname{tr}A^k=\sum\lambda_i^k\).
  • \(g(\lambda)=n-\operatorname{rank}(A-\lambda I)\) and \(1\le g\le a\).
  • \(m_A\mid\chi_A\); same roots; \(\chi_A(A)=0\).
  • \(2\times2\): \(A^2=(\operatorname{tr}A)A-(\det A)I\); \(A^{-1}=\frac{(\operatorname{tr}A)I-A}{\det A}\).
  • Diagonalisable \(\iff m_A\) has distinct linear factors \(\iff\sum g=n\).
  • Eigenvalues of \(p(A)\) are \(p(\lambda)\); of \(A^{-1}\) are \(1/\lambda\); of \(\operatorname{adj}A\) are \(\det A/\lambda\).
  • Idempotent: eigenvalues \(0,1\), \(\operatorname{tr}=\operatorname{rank}\). Involution: eigenvalues \(\pm1\).
  • Rank-one \(uv^T\): eigenvalues \(v^Tu,0,\dots,0\).
  • Tridiagonal\((-1,2,-1)\) determinant \(=n+1\).

Worked examples

Example 1: a defective matrix

\(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix}\). \(\chi_A=(x-2)^2(x-3)\). For \(\lambda=2\): \(A-2I\) has rank \(2\), so \(g(2)=1<a(2)=2\). Hence \(A\) is not diagonalisable. The minimal polynomial is \((x-2)^2(x-3)\) (the Jordan block for \(2\) has size \(2\)); it is verified by checking that no polynomial of degree \(2\) annihilates \(A\).

Example 2: diagonalise and take powers

\(A=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix}\). \(\chi=x^2-7x+10=(x-5)(x-2)\). Eigenvectors: \((1,1)\) for \(5\), \((1,-2)\) for \(2\). Then \(A^n=P D^nP^{-1}\) gives \(A^n=\frac{5^n}{3}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix}+\frac{2^n}{3}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-2&2\end{pmatrix}.\) For \(n=1\) this returns \(A\). For \(n=5\): \(A^5=\begin{pmatrix}2094&1031\\2062&1063\end{pmatrix}\) (agrees with direct multiplication).

Example 3: Cayley-Hamilton

\(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}\) has \(\chi=x^2-5x-2\), so \(A^2=5A+2I\). Then:

  • \(A^3=5A^2+2A=27A+10I\) and \(A^4=27A^2+10A=145A+54I=\begin{pmatrix}199&290\\435&634\end{pmatrix}\).
  • \(A^{-1}=\tfrac12(A-5I)=\begin{pmatrix}-2&1\\\tfrac32&-\tfrac12\end{pmatrix}\) (checked: \(AA^{-1}=I\)).

Example 4: rank-one matrices

\(A=uv^T\) with \(u=(1,2,3)^T\), \(v=(1,1,1)^T\): \(v^Tu=6\), so the eigenvalues are \(6,0,0\), \(m_A=x(x-6)\) and \(A\) is diagonalisable. For \(u=(1,-1,0)^T\), \(v=(1,1,5)^T\): \(v^Tu=0\), so \(A^2=0\), all eigenvalues \(0\) and \(A\) is not diagonalisable. Both checked by script.

Example 5: involution with given trace

\(A\) is a real \(4\times4\) matrix with \(A^2=I\) and \(\operatorname{tr}A=2\). Eigenvalues are \(\pm1\) with multiplicities \(a_+\), \(a_-\): \(a_++a_-=4\), \(a_+-a_-=2\), so \(a_+=3\), \(a_-=1\), and \(\det A=(-1)^1=-1\).

Example 6: functions of the spectrum

\(A\) is \(3\times3\) with eigenvalues \(1,2,3\). Then \(\operatorname{tr}A^2=1+4+9=14\); \(\det A=6\); the eigenvalues of \(\operatorname{adj}A\) are \(6,3,2\), so \(\det(\operatorname{adj}A)=36=6^2\); \(\operatorname{tr}(\operatorname{adj}A)=11\); \(A^{-1}\) has eigenvalues \(1,\tfrac12,\tfrac13\).

Example 7: a tridiagonal determinant

\(D_n=\det\bigl(2\delta_{ij}-\delta_{i+1,j}-\delta_{i,j+1}\bigr)\). Expanding along the first row, \(D_n=2D_{n-1}-D_{n-2}\) with \(D_1=2\) and \(D_2=3\). So \(D_n-D_{n-1}=1\) and \(D_n=n+1\) (checked for \(n\le11\)).

Example 8: idempotent operator, trace and nullity

\(T:\mathbb{R}^4\to\mathbb{R}^4\) with \(T^2=T\) and \(\operatorname{rank}T=2\) (the pattern of 2026 Q80, which is solved in the linear-transformations topic). The minimal polynomial divides \(x^2-x=x(x-1)\), and \(T\) is neither \(0\) nor \(I\), so \(m=x(x-1)\). Eigenvalue \(1\) has multiplicity \(\operatorname{rank}T=2\) and eigenvalue \(0\) has multiplicity \(4-2=2\). So \(\operatorname{tr}T=2\) and \(\operatorname{nullity}T=2\).

Common traps

  • Algebraic multiplicity \(\ne\) geometric multiplicity. “Repeated eigenvalue” does not mean non-diagonalisable, and “distinct eigenvalues” is sufficient but not necessary for diagonalisability. A statement “diagonalisable \(\Rightarrow\) distinct eigenvalues” is false (the identity matrix).
  • An invertible \(n\times n\) matrix need not have \(n\) distinct eigenvalues (2019 option (iii)). Invertible does not mean diagonalisable.
  • Same characteristic polynomial does not imply similar; compare minimal polynomials and ranks.
  • Roots of the minimal polynomial are exactly the eigenvalues; but a polynomial \(p\) with \(p(A)=0\) may have roots that are not eigenvalues.
  • \(\operatorname{tr}A=\operatorname{rank}A\) for idempotents only, not for general singular matrices.
  • Sign in the \(2\times2\) Cayley-Hamilton: \(A^2=(\operatorname{tr}A)A-(\det A)I\), so a negative determinant gives \(+\lvert\det A\rvert I\).
  • Eigenvalues of a sum or product are not sums or products of eigenvalues, unless the matrices commute and share eigenvectors.
  • \(\operatorname{adj}A\) has eigenvalues \(\det A/\lambda\), not \(\lambda^{-1}\).
  • The all-ones \(n\times n\) matrix has eigenvalues \(n,0,\dots,0\) (rank \(1\)), not \(1\).
  • Eigenvectors are never \(0\), but eigenvalues may be \(0\).
  • Over \(\mathbb{R}\) a matrix with complex eigenvalues is not diagonalisable over \(\mathbb{R}\) but is over \(\mathbb{C}\).
  • Computing \(\det\) for the three-term recurrence: starting values matter (\(D_1=2\), \(D_2=3\)).

Previous-year questions

2025 Q48 (answer B)

The eigenvalues of \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&2\\0&1&0\\1&2&1\end{pmatrix}\) are: (A) \(1,2,2\sqrt2\) (B) \(1,\ 1+\sqrt2,\ 1-\sqrt2\) (C) \(1,1,1\) (D) \(1,\ 2+\sqrt2,\ 2-\sqrt2\).

Solution: expand \(\det(A-\lambda I)\) along row 2: \((1-\lambda)\bigl[(1-\lambda)^2-2\bigr]=0\). So \(\lambda=1\) or \(1-\lambda=\pm\sqrt2\), giving \(1,\ 1\pm\sqrt2\). Check: trace \(=3\) matches \(1+(1+\sqrt2)+(1-\sqrt2)\). Official key: B.

2026 Q83 (answer A, worked key)

\(A\) is Hermitian with \(A^2=I\). Find the eigenvalues, decompose the space into orthogonal subspaces, and compute the trace if the dimension is \(4\) with equal multiplicities: (A) \(0\) (B) \(2\) (C) \(4\) (D) \(-2\).

Solution: \(A^2=I\) gives eigenvalues \(\pm1\); Hermitian gives real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenspaces \(E_1\perp E_{-1}\) with \(V=E_1\oplus E_{-1}\). Equal multiplicities in dimension \(4\) means \(2\) and \(2\), so \(\operatorname{tr}A=2(1)+2(-1)=0\). This answer comes from the unofficial worked key.

2026 Q98 (answer A, worked key; the first solve was medium confidence, an independent re-solve agreed)

A \(3\times3\) matrix \(A\) satisfies its characteristic equation \(A^3-3A^2+2A=0\). Find the eigenvalues, check invertibility and compute the rank of \(A(A-I)\): (A) \(1\) (B) \(2\) (C) \(3\) (D) \(0\).

Solution: the characteristic polynomial is \(x^3-3x^2+2x=x(x-1)(x-2)\), so the eigenvalues are \(0,1,2\) (distinct). \(A\) is singular. \(A(A-I)\) has eigenvalues \(0\cdot(-1)=0\), \(1\cdot0=0\), \(2\cdot1=2\), and it is diagonalisable (same eigenvectors as \(A\)), so its rank is the number of non-zero eigenvalues, \(1\). This answer comes from the unofficial worked key.

2019 (unnumbered, reconstructed from a video) invertible \(4\times4\) matrix (answer C, statement (iii))

For an invertible \(4\times4\) matrix, which statement is not necessarily true: (i) the rows form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^4\); (ii) the null space is \(\{0\}\); (iii) it has \(4\) distinct eigenvalues.

Solution: (i) and (ii) hold because the rank is \(4\). (iii) fails for \(I_4\) (eigenvalue \(1\) four times). The presenter crosses (iii). The option letter comes from the re-typed copy.

2019 (unnumbered, reconstructed) tridiagonal determinant (answer B, \(n+1\))

The determinant of the \(n\times n\) matrix \(A_n\) with entries \(2\delta_{ij}-\delta_{i+1,j}-\delta_{i,j+1}\) (that is, \(2\) on the diagonal, \(-1\) beside it): option (b) is \(n+1\).

Quick revision

  • \(\chi_A=x^n-(\operatorname{tr}A)x^{n-1}+\cdots+(-1)^n\det A\); \(\sum\lambda=\operatorname{tr}\), \(\prod\lambda=\det\).
  • \(1\le g(\lambda)\le a(\lambda)\); \(g(\lambda)=n-\operatorname{rank}(A-\lambda I)\).
  • Cayley-Hamilton: \(\chi_A(A)=0\); \(m_A\mid\chi_A\), same roots.
  • Diagonalisable \(\iff m_A\) has distinct linear factors \(\iff\sum g=n\).
  • \(n\) distinct eigenvalues \(\Rightarrow\) diagonalisable (converse false); invertible does not mean distinct eigenvalues.
  • \(A^2=A\): eigenvalues \(0,1\), \(\operatorname{tr}=\operatorname{rank}\). \(A^2=I\): eigenvalues \(\pm1\).
  • Nilpotent: eigenvalue \(0\) only, trace \(0\), not diagonalisable unless \(0\).
  • Rank-one \(uv^T\): eigenvalues \(v^Tu,0,\dots,0\); diagonalisable iff \(v^Tu\ne0\).
  • \(2\times2\): \(A^{-1}=\frac{(\operatorname{tr}A)I-A}{\det A}\) and \(A^2=(\operatorname{tr}A)A-(\det A)I\).
  • Eigenvalues of \(p(A)\): \(p(\lambda)\); of \(A^{-1}\): \(1/\lambda\); of \(\operatorname{adj}A\): \(\det A/\lambda\).
  • Tridiagonal\((-1,2,-1)\): \(\det=n+1\).
