Eigenvalues, Cayley-Hamilton and diagonalisation

15 questions. Pick an answer to see if it is right and why.

0 of 15 answered · 0 correct

  1. Question 1Easy
    A \(3\times3\) real matrix \(A\) has eigenvalues \(1,2,3\). Then \(\operatorname{tr}(A^2)\) equals

    Why

    The eigenvalues of \(A^2\) are \(1,4,9\), so \(\operatorname{tr}(A^2)=14\). The option \(36\) is \((\operatorname{tr}A)^2\), which is not the same thing.
  2. Question 2Easy
    \(A\) is a \(3\times3\) matrix with eigenvalues \(1,2,3\). Then \(\det(\operatorname{adj}A)\) equals

    Why

    \(\det A=6\) and \(\det(\operatorname{adj}A)=(\det A)^{n-1}=6^2=36\). Equivalently, the eigenvalues of \(\operatorname{adj}A\) are \(6/1,6/2,6/3=6,3,2\), with product \(36\).
  3. Question 3Easy
    \(A\) is a \(5\times5\) real matrix with \(A^2=A\) and \(\operatorname{rank}A=2\). Then \(\operatorname{tr}A\) equals

    Why

    \(A\) is idempotent, so it is diagonalisable with eigenvalues \(0,1\). The multiplicity of \(1\) equals the rank, so \(\operatorname{tr}A=\operatorname{rank}A=2\).
  4. Question 4Medium
    \(A\) is a real \(4\times4\) matrix with \(A^2=I\) and \(\operatorname{tr}A=2\). Then \(\det A\) equals

    Why

    Eigenvalues are \(\pm1\) with \(a_++a_-=4\) and \(a_+-a_-=2\), so \(a_+=3\), \(a_-=1\). Then \(\det A=(-1)^{a_-}=-1\).
  5. Question 5Medium
    The non-zero eigenvalue of the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\2&4&6\\3&6&9\end{pmatrix}\) is

    Why

    The matrix has rank \(1\), so two eigenvalues are \(0\) and the third equals the trace, \(1+4+9=14\). (It is \(uv^T\) with \(u=v=(1,2,3)^T\) and \(v^Tu=14\).)
  6. Question 6Easy
    Which of the following real matrices is diagonalisable over \(\mathbb{R}\)?

    Why

    \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}\) has distinct eigenvalues \(1,2\). The first has \(a(1)=2\) but \(g(1)=1\); the second is nilpotent and non-zero; the fourth has \(\chi=x^2+1\) with no real roots.
  7. Question 7Medium
    For \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}\) the Cayley-Hamilton theorem gives \(A^2=\)

    Why

    \(\chi_A=x^2-5x+(4-6)=x^2-5x-2\), so \(A^2-5A-2I=0\) and \(A^2=5A+2I\). Direct check: \(A^2=\begin{pmatrix}7&10\\15&22\end{pmatrix}\) and \(5A+2I\) is the same matrix. Taking the determinant with the wrong sign gives the option \(5A-2I\).
  8. Question 8Medium
    The minimal polynomial of \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix}\) is

    Why

    \(\chi_A=(x-2)^2(x-3)\). The eigenvalue \(2\) has one Jordan block of size \(2\) (since \(g(2)=1\)), so \((x-2)\) appears squared in \(m_A\); the eigenvalue \(3\) is simple. Checked: \((A-2I)(A-3I)\neq0\) but \((A-2I)^2(A-3I)=0\).
  9. Question 9Medium
    The determinant of the \(10\times10\) matrix with \(2\) on the diagonal and \(-1\) on the sub- and super-diagonal (zero elsewhere) is

    Why

    \(D_n=2D_{n-1}-D_{n-2}\) with \(D_1=2\), \(D_2=3\), so \(D_n=n+1\) and \(D_{10}=11\) (checked by exact arithmetic for \(n=1,\dots,11\)).
  10. Question 10Hard
    \(A\) is a \(4\times4\) matrix with \(\chi_A(x)=(x-1)^3(x-2)\) and \(\dim\ker(A-I)=2\). The minimal polynomial of \(A\) is

    Why

    \(g(1)=2\) means two Jordan blocks for \(1\), with sizes summing to \(3\), so the sizes are \(2\) and \(1\). The largest block has size \(2\), so \((x-1)^2\) appears in \(m_A\). The eigenvalue \(2\) is simple. Checked on \(\operatorname{diag}(J_2(1),1,2)\), where \(m=(x-1)^2(x-2)\).
  11. Question 11Medium
    \(A\) is a real \(3\times3\) matrix with \(A^2-3A+2I=0\) and \(\operatorname{tr}A=4\). Then \(\det A\) equals

    Why

    Eigenvalues lie in the roots \(\{1,2\}\) of \(x^2-3x+2\). If \(1\) occurs \(a\) times and \(2\) occurs \(b\) times, \(a+b=3\) and \(a+2b=4\), so \(b=1\), \(a=2\). Then \(\det A=1\cdot1\cdot2=2\). (Checked on \(\operatorname{diag}(1,1,2)\).)
  12. Question 12Hard
    Consider the following statements for real \(n\times n\) matrices. I. If \(A\) has \(n\) distinct real eigenvalues, then \(A\) is diagonalisable over \(\mathbb{R}\). II. If \(A\) is invertible and diagonalisable, then its eigenvalues are distinct. III. If \(\chi_A\) has a repeated root, then \(A\) is not diagonalisable. IV. If \(A^2=A\), then \(A\) is diagonalisable. Which statements are true?

    Why

    I is true (eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are independent). II is false: \(I_n\) is invertible and diagonalisable with a single eigenvalue. III is false: \(I_n\) has \(\chi=(x-1)^n\) yet is diagonalisable; a repeated root only allows non-diagonalisability. IV is true: \(x^2-x\) has distinct roots.
  13. Question 13Medium
    Assertion (A): every nilpotent matrix has trace \(0\). Reason (R): every eigenvalue of a nilpotent matrix is \(0\).

    Why

    If \(N^k=0\) and \(Nv=\lambda v\) with \(v\neq0\), then \(0=N^kv=\lambda^kv\), so \(\lambda=0\). The trace is the sum of the eigenvalues (over \(\mathbb{C}\), with multiplicity), hence \(0\). So R is true and explains A.
  14. Question 14Hard
    \(A\) is a real \(3\times3\) matrix with \(A^3=I\) and \(A\neq I\). Then \(\operatorname{tr}A\) equals

    Why

    Eigenvalues are cube roots of unity. \(A\) is diagonalisable over \(\mathbb{C}\) (distinct roots). Non-real eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs, so the possibilities are \(1,1,1\) (which forces \(A=I\), excluded) or \(1,\omega,\bar\omega\). Then \(\operatorname{tr}A=1+\omega+\bar\omega=1+2\cos\tfrac{2\pi}{3}=0\). Example: the cyclic permutation matrix.
  15. Question 15Medium
    \(A\) is a \(4\times4\) idempotent matrix of rank \(3\). Then \(\det(I+A)\) equals

    Why

    The eigenvalues of \(A\) are \(1,1,1,0\), so those of \(I+A\) are \(2,2,2,1\) and \(\det(I+A)=8\). The option \(16\) comes from treating every eigenvalue as \(1\) (the case \(A=I\)).
