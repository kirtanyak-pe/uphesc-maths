Inner product spaces, adjoint, special matrices and spectral theorem

15 questions. Pick an answer to see if it is right and why.

  1. Question 1Easy
    Which of the following defines an inner product on \(\mathbb R^2\)?

    Why

    Write the form as \(x^TGy\). It is an inner product iff \(G\) is symmetric positive definite. A: \(\det G=1-4<0\). D: \(\det G=2-1=1>0\) and \(g_{11}=1>0\), positive definite. B and C are indefinite (\(\langle x,x\rangle\) is \(2x_1x_2\) and \(x_1^2-x_2^2\), which take negative values).
  2. Question 2Easy
    In an inner product space, \(\lVert u\rVert=3\) and \(\lVert v\rVert=4\). Then \(\lVert u+v\rVert^2+\lVert u-v\rVert^2\) equals

    Why

    Parallelogram law: \(2(9+16)=50\). Option A is what Pythagoras gives for \(\lVert u+v\rVert^2\) only if \(u\perp v\).
  3. Question 3Easy
    Which of the following cannot be an eigenvalue of a \(3\times3\) real skew-symmetric matrix?

    Why

    Eigenvalues of a real skew-symmetric matrix are purely imaginary or \(0\), and they come in conjugate pairs. A non-zero real number such as \(2\) is impossible.
  4. Question 4Medium
    Gram-Schmidt is applied to \(v_1=(1,0,1)\), \(v_2=(1,1,0)\) in \(\mathbb R^3\) with the usual dot product, giving orthogonal \(u_1=v_1\) and \(u_2\). Then \(\lVert u_2\rVert^2\) equals

    Why

    \(u_2=v_2-\frac{\langle v_2,v_1\rangle}{\langle v_1,v_1\rangle}v_1=(1,1,0)-\tfrac12(1,0,1)=(\tfrac12,1,-\tfrac12)\), so \(\lVert u_2\rVert^2=\tfrac14+1+\tfrac14=\tfrac32\). Forgetting the factor \(\tfrac12\) gives \((0,1,-1)\) and the value \(2\).
  5. Question 5Easy
    The \((2,3)\) entry of the matrix of orthogonal projection of \(\mathbb R^3\) onto the line spanned by \((1,2,2)\) is

    Why

    \(P=\dfrac{vv^T}{v^Tv}\) with \(v^Tv=9\). The \((2,3)\) entry is \(\dfrac{2\cdot2}{9}=\dfrac49\). Without dividing by \(v^Tv\) one gets \(4\); with \(\lVert v\rVert=3\) instead of \(9\) one gets \(\tfrac43\).
  6. Question 6Medium
    Which of the following \(2\times2\) complex matrices is unitary?

    Why

    A matrix is unitary iff \(U^*U=I\), i.e. its columns are orthonormal. For \(\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1+i&1-i\\1-i&1+i\end{pmatrix}\) each column has squared norm \(\tfrac{\lvert1+i\rvert^2+\lvert1-i\rvert^2}{4}=1\), and \(\langle c_1,c_2\rangle=\tfrac{(1+i)\overline{(1-i)}+(1-i)\overline{(1+i)}}{4}=\tfrac{(1+i)^2+(1-i)^2}{4}=0\). In each of the other three matrices the columns have squared norm \(2\), so \(U^*U\) is a multiple of \(2\) on the diagonal and none of them is unitary without rescaling.
  7. Question 7Medium
    The distance from the point \((1,2,3)\) to the plane \(x+y+z=0\) in \(\mathbb R^3\) is

    Why

    Distance \(=\dfrac{\lvert1+2+3\rvert}{\sqrt{1+1+1}}=\dfrac6{\sqrt3}=2\sqrt3\). Option C forgets to divide by the length of the normal.
  8. Question 8Medium
    \(S\) is any subset of a finite-dimensional inner product space \(V\) and \(W=\operatorname{span}S\). Which statement is NOT always true?

    Why

    A vector is orthogonal to \(S\) iff it is orthogonal to \(\operatorname{span}S\), so \(S^\perp=W^\perp\) and \(\dim S^\perp=\dim V-\dim W\). If \(x\in S\cap S^\perp\) then \(\langle x,x\rangle=0\), so \(x=0\). But \(S\cup S^\perp\) need not be closed under addition: for \(S=\{(1,0)\}\) in \(\mathbb R^2\) the union is \(\{(1,0)\}\cup\{(0,t)\}\), which contains \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) but not their sum.
  9. Question 9Medium
    Assertion (A): the real matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}1&-2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}\) is normal but is not orthogonally diagonalizable over \(\mathbb R\). Reason (R): a real square matrix is orthogonally diagonalizable if and only if it is symmetric. Which is correct?

    Why

    The matrix \(M\) satisfies \(MM^T=M^TM=5I\), so it is normal, but its eigenvalues are \(1\pm2i\), which are not real, so it cannot be diagonalized over \(\mathbb R\). R is the real spectral theorem; since \(M\) is not symmetric, R shows exactly why it cannot be orthogonally diagonalized, so R explains A.
  10. Question 10Medium
    A real orthogonal \(3\times3\) matrix has \(\det=1\) and trace \(0\). Its rotation angle \(\theta\) (the eigenvalues are \(1,e^{\pm i\theta}\) with \(0<\theta<\pi\)) is

    Why

    \(\operatorname{tr}=1+2\cos\theta=0\) gives \(\cos\theta=-\tfrac12\), so \(\theta=2\pi/3\). (\(\theta=\pi/2\) would give trace \(1\).)
  11. Question 11Hard
    Which of the following \(2\times2\) complex matrices is NOT diagonalizable?

    Why

    A is complex symmetric but not Hermitian: its square is \(\begin{pmatrix}1+i^2&i-i\\i-i&i^2+1\end{pmatrix}=0\) while it is not \(0\), so it is a non-zero nilpotent and cannot be diagonalized. B is real symmetric (eigenvalues \(\pm1\)), C is Hermitian (eigenvalues \(0,2\)), D is normal with distinct eigenvalues \(\pm i\).
  12. Question 12Hard
    \(K\) is a real skew-symmetric \(n\times n\) matrix. Which statement is true?

    Why

    The non-zero eigenvalues of \(K\) are \(\pm ib_j\), so \(\det(I+K)=\prod(1+b_j^2)\ge1>0\). B is false: \(\det K=\prod b_j^2\ge0\) for even \(n\). C is false: odd order skew-symmetric matrices are singular. D is false: real eigenvalues can only be \(0\).
  13. Question 13Hard
    \(P\) is the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbb R^5\) onto a \(2\)-dimensional subspace. Then \(\det(I+P)\) equals

    Why

    \(P\) has eigenvalues \(1,1,0,0,0\), so \(I+P\) has eigenvalues \(2,2,1,1,1\) and \(\det(I+P)=2\cdot2=4\). Option D would be \(\det(2I)\).
  14. Question 14Easy
    Which of the following can be the rank of a non-zero real \(5\times5\) skew-symmetric matrix?

    Why

    A real skew-symmetric matrix is normal, and its non-zero eigenvalues come in pairs \(\pm ib\), so its rank (the number of non-zero eigenvalues) is even. Odd rank is impossible, and rank \(5\) would make a skew-symmetric matrix of odd order invertible. Only \(4\) is possible among the options (the rank may be \(2\) or \(4\)).
  15. Question 15Hard
    On \(\mathbb{R}^2\) take the inner product \(\langle x,y\rangle=x_1y_1+2x_2y_2\) and let \(T\) have standard matrix \(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\). The matrix of the adjoint \(T^*\) with respect to this inner product, in the standard basis, is

    Why

    Write \(\langle x,y\rangle=x^TGy\) with \(G=\operatorname{diag}(1,2)\). Then \(\langle Ax,y\rangle=x^TA^TGy=x^TG(G^{-1}A^TG)y\), so the adjoint has matrix \(G^{-1}A^TG\). Here \(A^TG=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\) and \(G^{-1}A^TG=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\\tfrac12&1\end{pmatrix}\). The plain transpose \(A^T\) is the adjoint only for the standard inner product, that is, in an orthonormal basis.
