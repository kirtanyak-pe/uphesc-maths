Why it matters for the exam

Canonical forms appear almost every year in the linear algebra block. In 2026 two questions (Q31, Q93) were pure canonical-form questions and Q98 needed the minimal polynomial. The usual forms are: read the Jordan structure from the minimal polynomial and ranks, count the possible Jordan forms, and read dimension, minimal polynomial and characteristic polynomial from a rational canonical form. Everything is a short calculation once you know the rules in this note.

Core ideas

Throughout, \(T\) is a linear operator on an \(n\)-dimensional vector space \(V\) over a field \(F\), and \(A\) is its matrix. \(\chi_A(x)=\det(xI-A)\) is the characteristic polynomial and \(m_A(x)\) the minimal polynomial (the monic polynomial of least degree with \(m_A(A)=0\)).

Minimal polynomial: the facts you need

  • \(m_A\) divides every polynomial \(p\) with \(p(A)=0\). In particular \(m_A\mid\chi_A\) (Cayley-Hamilton: \(\chi_A(A)=0\)).
  • \(m_A\) and \(\chi_A\) have the same roots (the eigenvalues), but possibly different multiplicities. Always \(1\le\deg m_A\le n\).
  • Similar matrices have the same \(\chi\) and the same \(m\).
  • For a block diagonal matrix, \(m=\operatorname{lcm}\) of the \(m\) of the blocks.
  • \(A\) is diagonalizable over \(F\) iff \(m_A\) splits into distinct linear factors over \(F\).
  • If \(A^k=I\) or \(A^2=A\) or \(A^3-3A^2+2A=0\), then \(m_A\) divides that polynomial, so the possible eigenvalues are its roots.

Jordan block and Jordan form

The Jordan block of size \(k\) and eigenvalue \(\lambda\) is the \(k\times k\) matrix with \(\lambda\) on the diagonal, \(1\) on the superdiagonal and \(0\) elsewhere:

\[J_k(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1&&\\&\lambda&\ddots&\\&&\ddots&1\\&&&\lambda\end{pmatrix}.\]

Theorem (Jordan form). If \(\chi_A\) splits into linear factors over \(F\) (always true over \(\mathbb C\)), then \(A\) is similar to a block diagonal matrix of Jordan blocks. The blocks are unique up to their order.

If \(\chi_A\) does not split (for example over \(\mathbb R\) with complex eigenvalues) the Jordan form does not exist over \(F\). The rational canonical form below always exists.

Reading the Jordan form of one eigenvalue \(\lambda\)

Let \(a\) = algebraic multiplicity (power of \(x-\lambda\) in \(\chi\)), \(N=A-\lambda I\) and \(r_k=\operatorname{rank}(N^k)\), with \(r_0=n\).

Quantity Value
Sum of the sizes of the blocks for \(\lambda\) \(a\)
Number of blocks for \(\lambda\) \(\dim\ker N=n-r_1\) (geometric multiplicity)
Size of the largest block exponent of \((x-\lambda)\) in \(m_A\)
Number of blocks of size \(\ge k\) \(r_{k-1}-r_k\)
Number of blocks of size exactly \(k\) \(r_{k-1}-2r_k+r_{k+1}\)
Dimension of generalized eigenspace \(\ker N^a\) \(a\)
\(\operatorname{rank}N^k\) restricted to the \(\lambda\)-part \(\sum_{\text{blocks}}\max(s-k,0)\)

The ranks \(r_k\) keep falling by the number of blocks of size \(\ge k\) and stabilise at \(n-a\) (the rest of the space, where \(N\) is invertible).

Eigenvalue part vs other eigenvalues. On the generalized eigenspace of a different eigenvalue \(\mu\ne\lambda\), the operator \(A-\lambda I\) is invertible. So when you compute \(\operatorname{rank}(A-\lambda I)^k\) you must add \(\dim\)(the other generalized eigenspaces). This is the step that 2026-Q31 tests.

Generalized eigenspaces and chains

The generalized eigenspace of \(\lambda\) is \(V_\lambda=\ker(A-\lambda I)^{a}=\ker(A-\lambda I)^{e}\), where \(e\) is the exponent of \(x-\lambda\) in \(m_A\). Then \(V=\bigoplus_\lambda V_\lambda\) (primary decomposition). A Jordan chain \(v_k,\,(A-\lambda)v_k,\dots\) spans one block: \(v_k\in\ker N^k\setminus\ker N^{k-1}\) gives a block of size at least \(k\).

Counting Jordan forms

For one eigenvalue of algebraic multiplicity \(a\), the possible block structures are the partitions of \(a\). The number of partitions \(p(a)\) is

\(a\) 1 2 3 4 5 6 7
\(p(a)\) 1 2 3 5 7 11 15
  • Given only \(\chi_A=\prod(x-\lambda_i)^{a_i}\): number of Jordan forms \(=\prod p(a_i)\).
  • Given also \(m_A=\prod(x-\lambda_i)^{e_i}\): for each \(i\) count the partitions of \(a_i\) whose largest part is exactly \(e_i\), then multiply.

Partitions with largest part \(e\) of \(a\): e.g. \(a=4,e=2\) gives \((2,2),(2,1,1)\), so 2.

When do \(\chi\) and \(m\) decide the similarity class?

The pair \((\chi,m)\) determines the Jordan form when every eigenvalue has \(a\le3\), since partitions of \(1,2,3\) are determined by their largest part: \((1)\); \((2),(1,1)\); \((3),(2,1),(1,1,1)\). Hence for \(n\le3\) (over \(\mathbb C\)) two matrices with the same \(\chi\) and \(m\) are similar.

For \(n=4\) this fails: \(J_2(\lambda)\oplus J_2(\lambda)\) and \(J_2(\lambda)\oplus J_1(\lambda)\oplus J_1(\lambda)\) have \(\chi=(x-\lambda)^4\) and \(m=(x-\lambda)^2\), but ranks of \(A-\lambda I\) are \(2\) and \(1\), so they are not similar. A third invariant (the ranks of \((A-\lambda I)^k\), equivalently the number of blocks) is needed.

Nilpotent operators

\(N^k=0\) for some \(k\) iff all eigenvalues are \(0\) iff \(\chi=x^n\). Jordan form of a nilpotent matrix: blocks \(J_s(0)\). The number of blocks \(=\dim\ker N\), the index of nilpotency (least \(k\) with \(N^k=0\)) \(=\) largest block, and the similarity classes of nilpotent \(n\times n\) matrices correspond to partitions of \(n\), so there are \(p(n)\) of them. \(N\) is similar to \(J_n(0)\) iff \(N^{n-1}\ne0\).

Powers and functions of a Jordan block

\(J_k(\lambda)=\lambda I+N\) with \(N^k=0\) and \(\lambda I,N\) commuting, so by the binomial theorem

\[J_2(\lambda)^m=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^m&m\lambda^{m-1}\\0&\lambda^m\end{pmatrix},\qquad f(J_k(\lambda))=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{f^{(j)}(\lambda)}{j!}N^j .\]

So \(A^m\to0\) iff all \(\lvert\lambda\rvert<1\), even with non-trivial blocks.

Rational canonical form

View \(V\) as an \(F[x]\)-module via \(x\cdot v=Tv\). The structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID gives two equivalent descriptions.

Invariant factors. There are unique monic non-constant polynomials \(d_1\mid d_2\mid\dots\mid d_k\) with \(V\cong\bigoplus F[x]/(d_i)\). Then

\[\chi_A=d_1d_2\cdots d_k,\qquad m_A=d_k,\qquad n=\sum\deg d_i .\]

Elementary divisors. Factor each \(d_i\) into powers of irreducibles. The list of all prime powers \(p_j^{e}\) that occur (with repeats) are the elementary divisors. Over \(\mathbb C\) the elementary divisors are exactly \((x-\lambda)^s\), one for each Jordan block \(J_s(\lambda)\).

Companion matrix. For monic \(f=x^k+a_{k-1}x^{k-1}+\dots+a_1x+a_0\),

\[C(f)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&-a_0\\1&0&\cdots&0&-a_1\\0&1&\cdots&0&-a_2\\ \vdots&&\ddots&&\vdots\\0&0&\cdots&1&-a_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}.\]

(Some books use its transpose; nothing changes.) Key facts: \(\chi_{C(f)}=m_{C(f)}=f\); \(C(f)\) is cyclic (non-derogatory): \(\operatorname{rank}(C(f)-\lambda I)=k-1\) for every eigenvalue, so each eigenvalue has a single Jordan block. In particular \(C((x-\lambda)^k)\sim J_k(\lambda)\).

Theorem (rational canonical form). Every square matrix over any field \(F\) is similar to \(\operatorname{diag}(C(d_1),\dots,C(d_k))\), and this block matrix is unique.

Elementary divisors to invariant factors. For each irreducible \(p\), sort its exponents in decreasing order. \(d_k\) is the product of the largest power of every prime, \(d_{k-1}\) the product of the second-largest powers, and so on. The number of invariant factors is the largest number of times any one prime appears.

Smith form. The invariant factors are the non-1 diagonal entries of the Smith normal form of \(xI-A\) over \(F[x]\). The \(i\)-th diagonal entry is \(\Delta_{i}/\Delta_{i-1}\), where \(\Delta_i\) is the gcd of the \(i\times i\) minors.

Similarity classes

  • \(A\sim B\) over \(F\) \(\iff\) same invariant factors \(\iff\) same elementary divisors \(\iff\) \(xI-A\) and \(xI-B\) are equivalent over \(F[x]\).
  • Over \(\mathbb C\) (or any algebraically closed field): \(A\sim B\) \(\iff\) same Jordan form.
  • Similarity does not change with field extension: if \(A,B\) over \(\mathbb Q\) are similar over \(\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\), they are similar over \(\mathbb Q\).
  • Every \(A\) is similar to \(A^{T}\).
  • The number of similarity classes with given \(\chi=\prod f_i^{a_i}\) (\(f_i\) distinct irreducible) is \(\prod p(a_i)\). If \(\chi\) has \(n\) distinct roots there is exactly one class: the matrix is diagonalizable and cyclic.
  • \(A\) is cyclic (non-derogatory) iff \(m_A=\chi_A\) iff only one invariant factor iff each eigenvalue has exactly one block.

Examples of counting: \(4\times4\) complex matrices with \(A^2=I\) are diagonalizable with eigenvalues \(\pm1\), so classes correspond to the number \(k=0,\dots,4\) of eigenvalues \(+1\): 5 classes. Over \(\mathbb F_2\) the \(2\times2\) matrices fall into 6 classes (for \(\chi=x^2\): \(0\) and \(J_2\); for \(\chi=(x+1)^2\): \(I\) and \(J_2(1)\); for \(\chi=x(x+1)\): one; for \(\chi=x^2+x+1\): one) and the invertible ones into 3, which is the number of conjugacy classes of \(GL_2(\mathbb F_2)\cong S_3\).

Centralizer dimension (bonus)

If a Jordan structure for each eigenvalue is the partition \(\mu=(\mu_1\ge\mu_2\ge\dots)\) with conjugate partition \(\mu'\), then the space of matrices commuting with \(A\) has dimension \(\sum_{\lambda}\sum_j(\mu'_j)^2\). Checks: one block \(J_n\) gives \(n\); \(A=\lambda I\) gives \(n^2\).

Worked examples

Example 1: read the structure from ranks

A \(6\times6\) matrix has \(\chi=(x-2)^6\) and \(\operatorname{rank}(A-2I)=3\), \(\operatorname{rank}(A-2I)^2=1\), \(\operatorname{rank}(A-2I)^3=0\). Find the Jordan form and \(m_A\).

With \(r_0=6,r_1=3,r_2=1,r_3=0\): number of blocks \(=6-3=3\). Blocks of size \(\ge2\): \(r_1-r_2=2\). Blocks of size \(\ge3\): \(r_2-r_3=1\). So sizes are \(3,2,1\) (check: \(3+2+1=6\)). \(m_A=(x-2)^3\). The matrix is \(J_3(2)\oplus J_2(2)\oplus J_1(2)\). (Verified by computing the ranks of this matrix.)

Example 2: count the possible Jordan forms

Matrix with \(\chi=(x-1)^3(x-2)^4\) and \(m=(x-1)^2(x-2)^2\).

For \(\lambda=1\): partitions of 3 with largest part 2: \((2,1)\), one choice. For \(\lambda=2\): partitions of 4 with largest part 2: \((2,2),(2,1,1)\), two choices. Total \(1\times2=2\) forms. Without \(m\) the count is \(p(3)p(4)=3\cdot5=15\).

Example 3: Jordan form of an explicit matrix

\[A=\begin{pmatrix}3&-1&0\\1&1&0\\1&-1&2\end{pmatrix}.\]

\(\chi_A=x^3-6x^2+12x-8=(x-2)^3\). \(N=A-2I\) has rank \(1\), so there are \(3-1=2\) blocks, and \(N^2=0\) so the largest block is \(2\). Hence \(A\sim J_2(2)\oplus J_1(2)\) and \(m_A=(x-2)^2\). \(A\) is not diagonalizable.

Example 4: elementary divisors to invariant factors

Over \(\mathbb Q\) the elementary divisors are \(x-1,\ (x-1)^2,\ x-2,\ (x-2)^3,\ x^2+1,\ x^2+1\).

Exponents by prime: \(x-1:\{2,1\}\), \(x-2:\{3,1\}\), \(x^2+1:\{1,1\}\). Each prime occurs at most twice, so there are 2 invariant factors.

  • \(d_2=(x-1)^2(x-2)^3(x^2+1)\), degree \(2+3+2=7\).
  • \(d_1=(x-1)(x-2)(x^2+1)\), degree \(4\).

Check \(d_1\mid d_2\). The space has dimension \(7+4=11=1+2+1+3+2+2\), \(m=d_2\), \(\chi=d_1d_2\).

Example 5: companion matrix

Take \(f=x^3-3x^2+4=(x+1)(x-2)^2\), so \(a_0=4,a_1=0,a_2=-3\) and

\[C(f)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-4\\1&0&0\\0&1&3\end{pmatrix}.\]

Its characteristic polynomial computes to \(f\). \(\operatorname{rank}(C+I)=2\) and \(\operatorname{rank}(C-2I)=2\), so each eigenvalue has exactly one block, as for any cyclic matrix. Also \(\operatorname{rank}(C-2I)^2=1\) so the 2-part is a single block of size 2. Hence \(C(f)\sim J_2(2)\oplus J_1(-1)\) and \(m=f\).

Example 6: not similar although \(\chi\) and \(m\) agree

\(A=J_2(1)\oplus J_2(1)\) and \(B=J_2(1)\oplus J_1(1)\oplus J_1(1)\). Both have \(\chi=(x-1)^4\) and \(m=(x-1)^2\). But \(\operatorname{rank}(A-I)=2\) and \(\operatorname{rank}(B-I)=1\). Not similar.

Common traps

  • “Same \(\chi\) and same \(m\) implies similar.” True for \(n\le3\) only. The smallest counterexample is \(4\times4\) (Example 6).
  • Largest block is the exponent in \(m\), not in \(\chi\). The exponent in \(\chi\) is the sum of all block sizes.
  • Number of blocks = geometric multiplicity, i.e. \(\dim\ker(A-\lambda I)\). Do not confuse it with the algebraic multiplicity.
  • Ranks of \((A-\lambda I)^k\) include the other eigenvalues. On the generalized eigenspace of \(\mu\ne\lambda\) the operator \(A-\lambda I\) is invertible, so those dimensions add to the rank. Forgetting them gives a wrong answer (the trap in 2026-Q31).
  • Minimal polynomial has to include every eigenvalue. If \(m=(x-2)^3(x-1)\) then \(1\) is an eigenvalue; the 1-part cannot be empty.
  • Companion matrix size. \(C(f)\) is \(\deg f\times\deg f\). In a question “companion matrices of degrees 2 and 3” the space has dimension \(2+3\), not \(\max\) or product.
  • Invariant factors must divide. \(d_1\mid d_2\mid\dots\); \(m\) is the last one, not the product.
  • Jordan form over \(\mathbb R\). A real matrix with non-real eigenvalues has no real Jordan form, but it does have a rational canonical form over \(\mathbb R\) and \(\mathbb Q\).
  • Diagonalizable needs distinct roots of \(m\). \(\chi\) with repeated roots can still be diagonalizable (e.g. \(I\), with \(m=x-1\)).
  • “\(A\) is not similar to \(A^T\).” Every square matrix over a field is similar to its transpose (each Jordan block \(J_k\) is conjugated to \(J_k^T\) by the reversal permutation, e.g. \(P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\) for \(k=2\)). So \(A\) and \(A^T\) have the same \(\chi\), \(m\), rank and Jordan form, but not the same eigenvectors.
  • Invariant subspace \(\ne\) direct summand. For one Jordan block \(J_n(\lambda)\) the invariant subspaces are exactly the \(n+1\) subspaces \(\ker(J-\lambda I)^k\), \(k=0,\dots,n\), a single chain; the line \(\ker(J-\lambda I)\) has no invariant complement for \(n\ge2\).
  • \(p(a)\) vs \(a\). Number of forms for one eigenvalue is the partition number (\(p(4)=5\)), not \(4\).

Previous-year questions

2026, Q31 (Jordan structure from the minimal polynomial)

Let \(T\) be a linear operator on a 5-dimensional (complex) space whose minimal polynomial is \((x-2)^3(x-1)\). Determine the possible Jordan block structure, then compute the dimension of the generalized eigenspace for eigenvalue \(2\) and \(\operatorname{rank}(T-2I)^2\). Options: (A) \((3,3)\) (B) \((2,3)\) (C) \((3,2)\) (D) \((4,1)\).

Answer: A, \((3,3)\) (worked key, unofficial).

The exponent \(3\) forces a block \(J_3(2)\); the factor \(x-1\) forces eigenvalue \(1\) with all its blocks of size 1. Dimension \(5\) leaves two cases. (i) 2-part is \(J_3(2)\), 1-part is \(J_1(1)\oplus J_1(1)\): generalized eigenspace dim \(3\), rank of \((T-2I)^2\) is \(1\) (from \(J_3(0)^2\)) plus \(2\) (the 1-part, where \(T-2I=-I\)) \(=3\). (ii) 2-part is \(J_3(2)\oplus J_1(2)\): dim \(4\), rank \(1+1=2\), i.e. \((4,2)\), which is not an option. Only \((3,3)\) appears.

2026, Q93 (rational canonical form)

A linear operator has a rational canonical form with companion matrices of degrees \(2\) and \(3\). Find the dimension of the space and the minimal and characteristic polynomials. Options for the dimension: (A) 3 (B) 6 (C) 4 (D) 5.

Answer: D, dimension 5 (worked key, unofficial).

The blocks are \(C(d_1)\) of size \(2\) and \(C(d_2)\) of size \(3\) with \(d_1\mid d_2\). Dimension \(=2+3=5\). \(m=d_2\) (degree 3) and \(\chi=d_1d_2\) (degree 5).

2026, Q98 (minimal polynomial from an annihilating polynomial)

A matrix \(A\) satisfies \(A^3-3A^2+2A=0\). Find the eigenvalues, decide invertibility, and compute \(\operatorname{rank}\,A(A-I)\). Options: (A) 1 (B) 2 (C) 3 (D) 0.

Answer: A, \(1\) (worked key). The question calls the equation the characteristic equation, so \(A\) is \(3\times3\).

\(x^3-3x^2+2x=x(x-1)(x-2)\) has distinct roots, so \(A\) is \(3\times3\) with eigenvalues \(0,1,2\), diagonalizable, and singular (not invertible). \(A(A-I)\) has eigenvalues \(0\cdot(-1)=0\), \(1\cdot0=0\), \(2\cdot1=2\) and is diagonalizable, so its rank is the number of non-zero eigenvalues, \(1\).

Quick revision

  • \(m_A\mid\chi_A\), same roots; \(A\) diagonalizable iff \(m_A\) has distinct linear factors.
  • Jordan blocks for \(\lambda\): sizes sum to algebraic multiplicity, number of blocks \(=\dim\ker(A-\lambda I)\), largest block \(=\) exponent in \(m_A\).
  • Blocks of size exactly \(k\): \(r_{k-1}-2r_k+r_{k+1}\) with \(r_k=\operatorname{rank}(A-\lambda I)^k\).
  • Number of Jordan forms for \(\chi\): \(\prod p(a_i)\); with \(m\) fixed: partitions of \(a_i\) with largest part \(e_i\).
  • \(\chi\) and \(m\) decide the class only for \(n\le3\); \(n=4\): \((2,2)\) vs \((2,1,1)\).
  • Nilpotent classes of \(n\times n\): \(p(n)\); \(N\sim J_n(0)\) iff \(N^{n-1}\ne0\).
  • Invariant factors \(d_1\mid\dots\mid d_k\): \(\chi=\prod d_i\), \(m=d_k\), \(n=\sum\deg d_i\).
  • Companion matrix \(C(f)\): \(\chi=m=f\), one Jordan block per eigenvalue.
  • Elementary divisors are prime powers of the invariant factors; over \(\mathbb C\) they are the Jordan blocks.
  • Similar over \(F\) iff same invariant factors; Jordan form needs \(\chi\) to split, rational form always exists.
  • \(J_2(\lambda)^m\) has \(m\lambda^{m-1}\) in the corner.
  • Every square matrix is similar to its transpose; invariant subspaces of \(J_n(\lambda)\) are the chain \(\ker(J-\lambda I)^k\).
