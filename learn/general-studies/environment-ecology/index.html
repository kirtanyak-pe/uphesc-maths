Why it matters for the exam

Environment is the biggest static-knowledge block after current affairs: 4 questions in 2025 (Q16-Q19) and 6 in 2026 (Q3, Q4, Q21, Q25, Q28, Q30). The 2025 questions were definitions and conventions (phenology, Cartagena Protocol, carbon trading, composition of air). The 2026 questions moved to applied reasoning: what an urban heat island is driven by, what happens to a coastal ecosystem under tourism pressure, what intergenerational equity means. Learn the definitions, the international treaties with their years, and the cause-effect logic of pollution.

Core ideas

Ecosystem basics

An ecosystem is a community of living organisms interacting with the non-living environment.

  • Food chain: a linear transfer of energy (grass, grasshopper, frog, snake, hawk). Food web: interlinked chains.
  • Trophic levels: producers, primary consumers (herbivores), secondary consumers, tertiary consumers; decomposers recycle matter.
  • Energy flow: only about 10 per cent of the energy at one trophic level passes to the next (Lindeman’s 10 per cent law). Energy flows one way; nutrients cycle.
  • Ecological pyramids: of number, biomass and energy. The pyramid of energy is always upright.
  • Biogeochemical cycles: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, water. Converting a natural ecosystem into an urban-industrial landscape disturbs these cycles; one consistent result is less evapotranspiration (fewer plants, sealed surfaces) and so altered regional hydrology (more runoff, less infiltration).
  • Ecology is the study of the relation between organisms and environment. Phenology is the study of the timing of seasonal activities of plants and animals (flowering, leaf fall, migration) in relation to changing environmental conditions, and it is used to track climate change. Climatology studies climate; forestry deals with forests.

Atmosphere

Layers from the ground up: troposphere (weather, most gases), stratosphere (ozone layer), mesosphere, thermosphere, exosphere.

Composition of dry air by volume:

Gas Share
Nitrogen about 78 per cent
Oxygen about 21 per cent
Argon about 0.93 per cent
Carbon dioxide about 0.04 per cent

In descending order of volume: nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide.

Pollution

Type Key terms
Air SO2, NOx, CO, ozone, particulate matter PM2.5 and PM10; smog
Water BOD, COD, dissolved oxygen, eutrophication, heavy metals
Soil Pesticides, plastics, industrial waste, salinisation
Noise Measured in decibels
  • Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) is the oxygen microbes need to break down organic matter in water. Higher BOD means more organic pollution. Dissolved oxygen moves the other way.
  • Eutrophication is the over-enrichment of water by nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus), causing algal blooms, oxygen loss and death of aquatic life. Rapid coastal tourism typically adds nutrient and sewage load, breaks habitats with construction and stirs up sediment: the combined outcome is coastal eutrophication, habitat fragmentation and sediment disturbance, not stable food webs or oligotrophic (nutrient-poor) conditions.
  • Air Quality Index (AQI) measures the concentration of atmospheric pollutants. CPCB categories: Good (0-50), Satisfactory (51-100), Moderate (101-200), Poor (201-300), Very Poor (301-400), Severe (401-500).
  • Urban heat island: cities are warmer than their surroundings. The intensity is most strongly driven by impervious surfaces (concrete, asphalt) and reduced evapotranspiration, plus heat released from vehicles and air-conditioning. More vegetation cover and permeable soil reduce it.

Climate change and ozone

  • Greenhouse gases: water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, ozone, CFCs. They trap outgoing infrared radiation. Methane and nitrous oxide are far stronger per molecule than carbon dioxide.
  • Ozone depletion: chlorine and bromine from CFCs and halons break down stratospheric ozone, producing the Antarctic ozone hole. The Vienna Convention (1985) and Montreal Protocol (1987) address it; the Kigali Amendment (2016) phases down HFCs.
  • Carbon footprint: total greenhouse gas emissions caused by a person, product or organisation, in carbon dioxide equivalent. Ecological footprint: the biologically productive land and sea area needed to support a population’s consumption and absorb its waste.
  • Carbon terms: a carbon sink absorbs more carbon than it emits (forests, oceans); carbon sequestration is capturing and storing carbon; carbon trading is a market-based system in which companies buy and sell emission permits or credits. One credit stands for one tonne of carbon dioxide equivalent.

The Cartagena Protocol is a protocol under the Convention on Biological Diversity; it is about living modified organisms (genetically modified organisms that can reproduce), not heritage sites, wetlands or ozone. In the Kunming-Montreal framework the “30 by 30” target means 30 per cent of terrestrial, inland water, coastal and marine areas under effective conservation by 2030, not 20, 40 or 50.

Biodiversity and conservation

  • Levels of biodiversity: genetic, species, ecosystem.
  • Biodiversity hotspot (Norman Myers): at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species and loss of at least 70 per cent of original habitat. India has four hotspots: the Himalaya, Indo-Burma, the Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, and Sundaland (Nicobar Islands).
  • In-situ conservation: national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves, sacred groves. Ex-situ: zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks, gene banks.
  • IUCN Red List categories: Extinct, Extinct in the Wild, Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, Near Threatened, Least Concern.
  • Indian laws and programmes: Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972, Water Act 1974, Forest (Conservation) Act 1980 (renamed Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam by the 2023 amendment), Air Act 1981, Environment (Protection) Act 1986, Biological Diversity Act 2002, National Green Tribunal Act 2010. Project Tiger began in 1973. The Constitution has Article 48A (State to protect the environment) and Article 51A(g) (citizen’s duty). India has announced a net-zero target for 2070, announced at COP26 (Glasgow, 2021).

Sustainable development and equity

Sustainable development, as defined in the Brundtland report Our Common Future (1987), meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. Intergenerational equity is the principle that the present generation should conserve resources so that future generations’ needs can be met. It rules out unlimited exploitation for present growth. Intra-generational equity concerns fairness within the present generation (equal distribution of benefits across nations or groups). The Sustainable Development Goals (17 goals, adopted in 2015) run to 2030.

Formulas and results

  • Energy transfer between trophic levels: \(E_{n+1} \approx 0.1\, E_n\).
  • Air by volume: N2 > O2 > Ar > CO2.
  • AQI bands: 0-50, 51-100, 101-200, 201-300, 301-400, 401-500.
  • Cartagena Protocol: living modified organisms. Ramsar: wetlands. Montreal: ozone. Basel: hazardous waste. CITES: endangered species trade.
  • Carbon trading: market for emission permits.
  • 30 by 30: Kunming-Montreal framework.
  • BOD: organic pollution in water. AQI: air pollutant concentration. Carbon footprint: total greenhouse gas emissions. Ecological footprint: biologically productive land demand.

Worked examples

Example 1: Order air gases

Arrange carbon dioxide, nitrogen, oxygen, argon in descending volume. Shares are about 0.04, 78, 21 and 0.93 per cent. Descending: nitrogen, oxygen, argon, carbon dioxide.

Example 2: Energy loss

A producer level stores 10,000 kJ. How much reaches tertiary consumers (third consumer level) under the 10 per cent law? Producers 10,000, primary consumers 1,000, secondary consumers 100, tertiary consumers 10 kJ.

Example 3: Read an AQI value

A city reports an AQI of 250. It lies in 201-300, so the category is Poor. A value of 90 would be Satisfactory, and 410 Severe.

Example 4: Match indicator to measure

BOD measures organic pollution in water. AQI measures atmospheric pollutant concentration. Carbon footprint measures total greenhouse gas emissions. Ecological footprint measures the biologically productive land demand. Each indicator has one medium (water, air) or one quantity (emissions, land), and the pairs are unique.

Example 5: Treaty recall by subject

“Which treaty deals with living modified organisms?” Biosafety is the keyword, so Cartagena Protocol. “Which phases out ozone-depleting substances?” Montreal Protocol. “Which governs wetlands?” Ramsar. “Which governs trade in endangered species?” CITES.

Example 6: Urban heat island

A city replaces a wooded area with concrete. Surface evapotranspiration falls (no leaf water loss), concrete stores heat and sheds rainfall rapidly. Both effects raise local temperatures: the UHI intensity rises. The option “reduced vegetation cover and reduced evapotranspiration with impervious surfaces” is correct; “increased soil permeability” would reduce it.

Common traps

  • Cartagena versus Nagoya. Cartagena is biosafety (LMOs); Nagoya is access and benefit sharing. Both are protocols under the CBD.
  • Carbon sink versus sequestration versus trading. A sink is the reservoir, sequestration is the process, trading is the market. “Carbon space” is not a standard term.
  • Phenology versus ecology. Phenology is about timing of seasonal events.
  • 30 per cent. Not 20, 40 or 50; the framework says 30.
  • Footprints. Carbon footprint counts emissions; ecological footprint counts land and sea area.
  • BOD. High BOD means polluted water, not clean water.
  • Eutrophication versus oligotrophic. Eutrophic water is nutrient-rich; oligotrophic is nutrient-poor.
  • Intergenerational and intragenerational equity. One looks forward in time, the other across nations or groups today.
  • Order of gases. Argon is third by volume and carbon dioxide is last.
