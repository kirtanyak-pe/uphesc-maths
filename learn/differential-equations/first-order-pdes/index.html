Why it matters for the exam

First-order PDEs give one question in most papers (2025-Q60 was a Clairaut equation, the simplest Charpit case). The examiner picks one of: forming a PDE, a Lagrange equation \(Pp+Qq=R\), a standard form I-IV, a Clairaut equation, or the meaning of complete/general/singular integral. Every one of these is a 2-minute question if the method is automatic.

Core ideas

Notation throughout: \(z=z(x,y)\), \(p=z_x=\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial x}\), \(q=z_y=\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial y}\).

Order, linearity

A first-order PDE is \(F(x,y,z,p,q)=0\). Classification used in questions:

Type Form Example
Linear \(P(x,y)p+Q(x,y)q+R(x,y)z=S(x,y)\) \(xp+yq+z=x\)
Semilinear \(P(x,y)p+Q(x,y)q=R(x,y,z)\) \(p+q=z^2\)
Quasilinear \(P(x,y,z)p+Q(x,y,z)q=R(x,y,z)\) \(zp+q=0\)
Nonlinear any other (p or q appears non-linearly) \(pq=z\), \(p^2+q^2=1\)

“Lagrange’s linear equation” in Indian books means the quasilinear form \(Pp+Qq=R\) with \(P,Q,R\) functions of \(x,y,z\). It is linear in \(p,q\), not in \(z\).

Formation of a PDE by eliminating constants

If a family \(f(x,y,z,a,b)=0\) has two arbitrary constants \(a,b\), differentiate with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) to get \(f_x+f_zp=0\) and \(f_y+f_zq=0\). Eliminating \(a,b\) from these three equations gives a first-order PDE.

Rule on numbers: with \(n\) independent variables and \(n\) constants you get a first-order PDE. With more constants than independent variables the result is of higher order (the extra constants need second derivatives to eliminate).

  • \(z=(x+a)(y+b)\): \(p=y+b\), \(q=x+a\), so \(pq=z\).
  • \(z=ax+by+ab\): \(p=a\), \(q=b\), so \(z=px+qy+pq\) (Clairaut form).
  • \(z=ax^2+by^2\): \(xp=2ax^2\), \(yq=2by^2\), so \(xp+yq=2z\).
  • \(x^2+y^2+(z-c)^2=r^2\) (spheres with centre on the \(z\)-axis, constants \(c,r\)): \(x+(z-c)p=0\) and \(y+(z-c)q=0\), so \(z-c=-x/p=-y/q\) and \(yp=xq\).
  • \(z=ax^2+by^2+c\) has three constants, so eliminating gives a second-order equation (\(z_{xy}=0\) and a relation between \(z_{xx}\), \(z_{yy}\) and \(p,q\)); it cannot be first order.

Formation by eliminating arbitrary functions

  • One arbitrary function of one expression \(u(x,y,z)\): \(\phi(u)=0\) or \(z=f(u)\) gives a first-order PDE.
  • \(F(u,v)=0\) with \(u,v\) functions of \(x,y,z\) gives the Lagrange equation \(Pp+Qq=R\) where \(P=\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(y,z)},\quad Q=\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(z,x)},\quad R=\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}.\)
  • Two arbitrary functions, as in \(z=f(x+y)+g(x-y)\), need second derivatives: \(z_{xx}=z_{yy}\).

Examples:

  • \(z=f(x^2+y^2)\): \(p=2xf'\), \(q=2yf'\), so \(yp-xq=0\).
  • \(z=f(y/x)\) or \(z=xf(y/x)\): \(z=xf(y/x)\) gives \(xp+yq=z\).
  • \(\phi(x^2+y^2+z^2,\,lx+my+nz)=0\) gives \((ny-mz)p+(lz-nx)q=mx-ly\) (surfaces of revolution about the line through the origin with direction \((l,m,n)\)).

Integrals

For \(F(x,y,z,p,q)=0\):

Integral Meaning
Complete integral Solution \(f(x,y,z,a,b)=0\) with two arbitrary constants \(a,b\) (and \(\partial(p,q)/\partial(a,b)\ne0\), so \(a,b\) are genuinely independent)
General integral Put \(b=\varphi(a)\) in the complete integral, differentiate \(f=0\) with respect to \(a\) (where \(b\) depends on \(a\)): \(f_a+f_b\varphi'(a)=0\). Eliminate \(a\). It contains one arbitrary function \(\varphi\)
Singular integral Envelope of the two-parameter family: eliminate \(a,b\) from \(f=0,\ f_a=0,\ f_b=0\). It contains no arbitrary constant or function
Special integral A solution contained in neither of the above

Facts examiners test:

  • The singular integral is a solution of the PDE but cannot be got from the general integral by choosing \(\varphi\).
  • A second route to the singular integral: eliminate \(p,q\) from \(F=0,\ F_p=0,\ F_q=0\). The result is a candidate; check it satisfies the PDE (it may include extra curves that are not solutions).
  • Each member of the general integral is the envelope of a one-parameter subfamily.
  • Do not confuse: the complete integral has constants, the general integral has an arbitrary function.

Lagrange’s equation \(Pp+Qq=R\)

Geometry. The normal to the surface \(z=z(x,y)\) is \((p,q,-1)\). The equation says \((P,Q,R)\cdot(p,q,-1)=0\), so the vector field \((P,Q,R)\) is tangent to every integral surface. Integral surfaces are therefore unions of integral curves of the field.

Method.

  1. Write the auxiliary (Lagrange) equations \(\frac{dx}{P}=\frac{dy}{Q}=\frac{dz}{R}.\)
  2. Find two independent integrals \(u(x,y,z)=c_1\) and \(v(x,y,z)=c_2\).
  3. The general integral is \(F(u,v)=0\) (\(F\) arbitrary), equivalently \(u=\phi(v)\).

Why. \(u=c_1\) and \(v=c_2\) satisfy \(Pu_x+Qu_y+Ru_z=0\) and \(Pv_x+Qv_y+Rv_z=0\). Differentiating \(F(u,v)=0\) in \(x\) and in \(y\) and eliminating \(F_u,F_v\) gives exactly \(Pp+Qq=R\).

Ways to get the two integrals.

  • Direct: two of the ratios involve only two variables (e.g. \(dx/P=dy/Q\) with \(P,Q\) free of \(z\)).
  • Use a solved integral to reduce the remaining ratio: substitute \(u=c_1\) to eliminate a variable.
  • Multipliers: for any \(l,m,n\) (constants or functions) \(\frac{dx}{P}=\frac{dy}{Q}=\frac{dz}{R}=\frac{l\,dx+m\,dy+n\,dz}{lP+mQ+nR}.\) If \(lP+mQ+nR=0\) then \(l\,dx+m\,dy+n\,dz=0\), and if this is exact you get an integral at once.
  • Common multiplier choices: \((1,1,1)\); \((x,y,z)\); \((1/x,1/y,1/z)\); \((1,-1,0)\) (gives \(dx-dy\)); constants \((l,m,n)\) taken from the coefficients.

Standard pairs to recognise.

Equation Auxiliary equations Integrals General integral
\(xp+yq=z\) \(\frac{dx}{x}=\frac{dy}{y}=\frac{dz}{z}\) \(\frac yx=c_1,\ \frac zx=c_2\) \(F\!\left(\frac yx,\frac zx\right)=0\), i.e. \(z=x\,\phi(y/x)\)
\((y-z)p+(z-x)q=x-y\) multipliers \((1,1,1)\) and \((x,y,z)\) \(x+y+z=c_1,\ x^2+y^2+z^2=c_2\) \(F(x+y+z,\ x^2+y^2+z^2)=0\)
\((mz-ny)p+(nx-lz)q=ly-mx\) multipliers \((l,m,n)\) and \((x,y,z)\) \(lx+my+nz=c_1,\ x^2+y^2+z^2=c_2\) \(F(lx+my+nz,\ x^2+y^2+z^2)=0\)
\(p\tan x+q\tan y=\tan z\) \(\frac{dx}{\tan x}=\frac{dy}{\tan y}=\frac{dz}{\tan z}\), integrate \(\cot\) \(\frac{\sin y}{\sin x}=c_1,\ \frac{\sin z}{\sin x}=c_2\) \(F\!\left(\frac{\sin y}{\sin x},\frac{\sin z}{\sin x}\right)=0\)

Standard forms of nonlinear first-order PDEs

Form I: \(f(p,q)=0\) (no \(x,y,z\)). Try \(z=ax+by+c\). Then \(p=a,q=b\), so \(f(a,b)=0\). Solve \(b=\varphi(a)\) (or choose \(a\) and solve for \(b\)): \(z=ax+\varphi(a)\,y+c\quad(a,c\text{ arbitrary}).\) Example: \(p^2+q^2=4\): \(z=ax+\sqrt{4-a^2}\,y+c\). The constant \(c\) appears additively, so there is no singular integral (the \(c\)-derivative is \(1\ne0\)).

Form II: Clairaut, \(z=px+qy+f(p,q)\). Complete integral: replace \(p\to a,\ q\to b\): \(z=ax+by+f(a,b).\) Singular integral: eliminate \(a,b\) from \(x+f_a=0,\ y+f_b=0\) and the complete integral. Example (2025-Q60): \(z=px+qy+p^2+q^2\) has complete integral \(z=ax+by+a^2+b^2\) and singular integral \(z=-\tfrac14(x^2+y^2)\) (from \(a=-x/2,\ b=-y/2\)). Example: \(z=px+qy+pq\) has complete integral \(z=ax+by+ab\) and singular integral \(z=-xy\).

Form III: \(f(z,p,q)=0\) (no \(x,y\) explicitly). Put \(u=x+ay\) and \(z=z(u)\). Then \(p=\dfrac{dz}{du},\ q=a\dfrac{dz}{du}\). Solve for \(dz/du\) and integrate. Example: \(p(1+q)=qz\). With \(q=ap\): \(1+ap=az\), so \(\dfrac{dz}{du}=z-\dfrac1a\), giving \(z=\dfrac1a+b\,e^{x+ay}\).

Form IV: separable, \(f_1(x,p)=f_2(y,q)\). Set both sides equal to \(a\), solve \(p=P(x,a)\) and \(q=Q(y,a)\), then \(z=\int P(x,a)\,dx+\int Q(y,a)\,dy+b.\) Example: \(p-x^2=q+y^2\): \(p=x^2+a,\ q=a-y^2\), so \(z=\dfrac{x^3}{3}+ax+ay-\dfrac{y^3}{3}+b\). Example: \(p^2y(1+x^2)=qx^2\): dividing, \(\dfrac{p^2(1+x^2)}{x^2}=\dfrac qy=a\), so \(z=\sqrt a\,\sqrt{1+x^2}+\dfrac a2y^2+b\).

Reductions to these forms. If \(x,y\) appear only through \(x^mp\) and \(y^nq\) (e.g. \(x^2p^2+y^2q^2=z\)), put \(X=x^{1-m}/(1-m)\), \(Y=y^{1-n}/(1-n)\) (use \(\ln x\) when \(m=1\)) so that \(x^mp=P\), \(y^nq=Q\), giving a Form III equation in \(P,Q,z\).

Charpit’s method (general \(F(x,y,z,p,q)=0\))

Idea: find a second equation \(G(x,y,z,p,q)=a\) compatible with \(F=0\). Then solve for \(p,q\) and integrate \(dz=p\,dx+q\,dy\).

Compatibility of \(F=0\) and \(G=a\) means \(G\) solves the Charpit equations, whose characteristic system is \(\frac{dp}{F_x+pF_z}=\frac{dq}{F_y+qF_z}=\frac{dz}{-pF_p-qF_q}=\frac{dx}{-F_p}=\frac{dy}{-F_q}.\)

Steps:

  1. Write the Charpit equations. Choose any two ratios that give an integral involving \(p\) or \(q\) (the easiest: \(\dfrac{dp}{F_x+pF_z}=\dfrac{dq}{F_y+qF_z}\) or one with \(dx/(-F_p)\)).
  2. Call that integral \(G=a\), solve \(F=0,\ G=a\) for \(p,q\).
  3. Integrate \(dz=p\,dx+q\,dy\) (it is exact by compatibility). The result with constants \(a,b\) is a complete integral.

Example. \(px+qy=pq\). Here \(F_x=p,\ F_y=q,\ F_z=0,\ F_p=x-q,\ F_q=y-p\). \(\dfrac{dp}{p}=\dfrac{dq}{q}\) gives \(q=ap\). Substituting: \(p(x+ay)=ap^2\), so \(p=\dfrac{x+ay}{a},\ q=x+ay\). Then \(dz=\frac{(x+ay)}{a}dx+(x+ay)dy=\frac1a(x+ay)\,d(x+ay),\) so \(z=\dfrac{(x+ay)^2}{2a}+b\).

Charpit contains forms I-IV as special cases: the auxiliary integral \(G=a\) is read off at once (\(p=a\) for forms I and II, \(q=ap\) for form III, \(f_1(x,p)=a\) for form IV).

Jacobi’s method (basic)

Jacobi’s method handles \(F(x_1,\dots,x_n,p_1,\dots,p_n)=0\) where \(z\) does not appear (any equation can be brought to this form by treating \(z\) as an extra variable). Define the Jacobi (Poisson) bracket \([F,G]=\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial G}{\partial x_i}-\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_i}\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_i}\right).\) Procedure: find \(n-1\) more functions \(F_2=a_2,\dots,F_n=a_n\) which are in involution with \(F\) and each other (\([F,F_j]=0\), \([F_i,F_j]=0\)). Solve the \(n\) equations for \(p_1,\dots,p_n\), then \(dz=\sum p_i\,dx_i\) integrates to a complete integral with \(n\) constants (the \(n\)th is additive). For \(n=2\) the condition \([F,G]=0\) is the same as Charpit’s characteristic system, so Jacobi reduces to Charpit.

Example. \(p^2+q^2=x+y\). \([F,G]=0\) is solved by \(G=p-q=a\). Then \(p+q=\sqrt{2(x+y)-a^2}\), so \(p=\tfrac12\big(\sqrt{2(x+y)-a^2}+a\big),\quad q=\tfrac12\big(\sqrt{2(x+y)-a^2}-a\big),\) \(z=\tfrac16\,(2(x+y)-a^2)^{3/2}+\tfrac a2(x-y)+b.\)

The Cauchy problem

Find the integral surface through a given initial curve \(\Gamma:\ x=x_0(s),\ y=y_0(s),\ z=z_0(s)\).

Quasilinear case \(Pp+Qq=R\).

  1. Integrals \(u=c_1,\ v=c_2\) of the auxiliary equations.
  2. Substitute the curve \(\Gamma\) into \(u\) and \(v\) and eliminate the parameter \(s\) to get a relation \(\Phi(c_1,c_2)=0\).
  3. The required surface is \(\Phi(u,v)=0\).

Condition for a unique solution near \(\Gamma\): the projection of \(\Gamma\) in the \(xy\)-plane must not be tangent to the characteristic direction: \(Q\,x_0'(s)-P\,y_0'(s)\ne0\ \text{ along }\Gamma.\)

  • If \(\Gamma\) is itself an integral curve of the auxiliary equations (a characteristic), then either there is no solution or infinitely many.
  • Example: \(p+q=0\) (so \(P=Q=1,\ R=0\)) has integrals \(x-y=c_1,\ z=c_2\), so \(z=f(x-y)\). The curve \(y=x,\ z=1\) is a characteristic: \(z=1\) and \(z=1+(x-y)^2\) both pass through it (infinitely many solutions). The curve \(y=x,\ z=x\) projects onto the same direction but \(z\) varies along it, while every solution is constant on \(y=x\) (there \(x-y=0\)): no solution exists. The curve \(y=0,\ z=g(x)\) is transversal (\(Qx_0'-Py_0'=1\ne0\)) and gives the unique solution \(z=g(x-y)\).

General nonlinear \(F(x,y,z,p,q)=0\). Characteristic strip equations: \(\frac{dx}{F_p}=\frac{dy}{F_q}=\frac{dz}{pF_p+qF_q}=\frac{dp}{-(F_x+pF_z)}=\frac{dq}{-(F_y+qF_z)}=dt.\) Procedure: from the data find \(p_0(s),q_0(s)\) by solving \(F(x_0,y_0,z_0,p_0,q_0)=0\) together with the strip condition \(z_0'=p_0x_0'+q_0y_0'\). Integrate the system with these initial values at \(t=0\). Eliminate \(s,t\) to get \(z(x,y)\). A unique local solution exists if \(x_0'F_q-y_0'F_p\ne0\) on \(\Gamma\) (the data curve is non-characteristic).

Example. \(z\,p+q=0\) with \(z(x,0)=x\). Auxiliary equations: \(\dfrac{dx}{z}=\dfrac{dy}{1}=\dfrac{dz}{0}\), so \(z=c_1\) and \(x-zy=c_2\). Data: at \(y=0\), \(z=x\) gives \(c_1=x\), \(c_2=x\), so \(c_2=c_1\). Hence \(x-zy=z\) and \(z=\frac{x}{1+y}.\)

Formulas and results

  • Eliminating \(n\) constants (with \(n\) independent variables) gives a first-order PDE; more constants give higher order.
  • \(F(u,v)=0\) gives \(Pp+Qq=R\) with \(P=\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(y,z)},\ Q=\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(z,x)},\ R=\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\).
  • Lagrange: \(\dfrac{dx}{P}=\dfrac{dy}{Q}=\dfrac{dz}{R}=\dfrac{l\,dx+m\,dy+n\,dz}{lP+mQ+nR}\); general integral \(F(u,v)=0\).
  • Complete integral: two arbitrary constants. General: \(b=\varphi(a)\) and \(f_a+f_b\varphi'=0\). Singular: \(f=f_a=f_b=0\), or \(F=F_p=F_q=0\).
  • Form I: \(f(p,q)=0\Rightarrow z=ax+\varphi(a)y+c\).
  • Form II (Clairaut): \(z=px+qy+f(p,q)\Rightarrow z=ax+by+f(a,b)\); singular from \(x+f_a=0,\ y+f_b=0\).
  • Form III: \(f(z,p,q)=0\): put \(u=x+ay\), \(p=z'\), \(q=az'\).
  • Form IV: \(f_1(x,p)=f_2(y,q)=a\).
  • Charpit: \(\dfrac{dp}{F_x+pF_z}=\dfrac{dq}{F_y+qF_z}=\dfrac{dz}{-pF_p-qF_q}=\dfrac{dx}{-F_p}=\dfrac{dy}{-F_q}\).
  • Jacobi bracket: \([F,G]=\sum(F_{p_i}G_{x_i}-F_{x_i}G_{p_i})\); need \(n\) functions in involution.
  • Cauchy data: quasilinear transversality \(Qx_0'-Py_0'\ne0\); nonlinear \(x_0'F_q-y_0'F_p\ne0\).

Worked examples

Example 1: forming a PDE (constants)

Eliminate \(a,b\) from \(z=(x+a)(y+b)\).

\(p=y+b,\ q=x+a\), hence \(pq=(x+a)(y+b)=z\). The PDE is \(pq=z\).

Example 2: forming a PDE (arbitrary function)

Eliminate \(f\) from \(z=f(x^2+y^2)\).

\(p=2xf'(x^2+y^2)\) and \(q=2yf'(x^2+y^2)\). Dividing, \(p/q=x/y\), so \(yp-xq=0\).

Example 3: Lagrange with multipliers

Solve \(x(y-z)p+y(z-x)q=z(x-y)\).

Auxiliary equations: \(\dfrac{dx}{x(y-z)}=\dfrac{dy}{y(z-x)}=\dfrac{dz}{z(x-y)}\).

  • Multipliers \((1,1,1)\): \(x(y-z)+y(z-x)+z(x-y)=0\), so \(dx+dy+dz=0\) and \(x+y+z=c_1\).
  • Multipliers \((1/x,1/y,1/z)\): \((y-z)+(z-x)+(x-y)=0\), so \(\dfrac{dx}{x}+\dfrac{dy}{y}+\dfrac{dz}{z}=0\) and \(xyz=c_2\).

General integral: \(F(x+y+z,\ xyz)=0\).

Example 4: Lagrange by direct integration

Solve \(x^2p+y^2q=(x+y)z\).

\(\dfrac{dx}{x^2}=\dfrac{dy}{y^2}\) gives \(\dfrac1x-\dfrac1y=c_1\). Using \(\dfrac{dx-dy}{x^2-y^2}=\dfrac{dz}{(x+y)z}\), i.e. \(\dfrac{d(x-y)}{(x-y)(x+y)}=\dfrac{dz}{(x+y)z}\), we get \(\ln(x-y)=\ln z+\text{const}\), so \(\dfrac{x-y}{z}=c_2\). General integral: \(F\!\left(\dfrac1x-\dfrac1y,\ \dfrac{x-y}{z}\right)=0\).

Example 5: Cauchy problem for a Lagrange equation

Find the surface satisfying \(xp+yq=z\) through the circle \(x^2+y^2=1,\ z=1\).

Integrals: \(\dfrac yx=c_1,\ \dfrac zx=c_2\). Parametrise the circle as \(x=\cos t,\ y=\sin t,\ z=1\): \(c_1=\tan t,\ c_2=\sec t\), so \(c_2^2=1+c_1^2\). Hence \(\dfrac{z^2}{x^2}=1+\dfrac{y^2}{x^2}\), i.e. \(z^2=x^2+y^2\) (the cone). Check: \(z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) gives \(xp+yq=\dfrac{x^2+y^2}{z}=z\).

Example 6: Charpit

Find a complete integral of \(px+qy=pq\). (Worked in the Core ideas section.)

\(z=\dfrac{(x+ay)^2}{2a}+b\). Check: \(p=\dfrac{x+ay}{a},\ q=x+ay\), so \(px+qy=\dfrac{(x+ay)^2}{a}=pq\).

Example 7: Standard form and singular integral

Find the complete and singular integrals of \(z=px+qy+pq\).

Clairaut form with \(f(p,q)=pq\). Complete integral: \(z=ax+by+ab\). Singular: differentiate with respect to \(a\): \(x+b=0\); with respect to \(b\): \(y+a=0\). So \(a=-y,\ b=-x\), and \(z=-xy-xy+xy=-xy\). Check: \(z=-xy\) gives \(p=-y,\ q=-x\), and \(px+qy+pq=-xy-xy+xy=-xy=z\). It is a solution not obtainable from the complete integral for any fixed \(a,b\).

Common traps

  • Mixing up \(z=f(u)\) with \(F(u,v)=0\). One arbitrary function of one combination gives \(z=f(\cdot)\); two combinations in the auxiliary integrals give \(F(u,v)=0\).
  • Giving constants where the general integral needs an arbitrary function. “\(z=ax+by+ab\)” is the complete integral, not the general one.
  • Calling a complete integral “the” solution. A first-order PDE has a family of solutions; the general integral is a family built from the complete one.
  • Assuming the singular integral comes from the general integral. It does not; it is the envelope and generally lies outside the general family.
  • In Clairaut, forgetting that the complete integral is obtained by substituting constants \(a,b\) for \(p,q\), including inside \(f\).
  • Form I: not solving for the second constant. \(f(a,b)=0\) ties \(b\) to \(a\), so there are only two constants \(a,c\) afterwards.
  • Charpit signs: the denominators for \(dx,dy,dz\) carry \(-F_p,\ -F_q,\ -(pF_p+qF_q)\) against \(F_x+pF_z,\ F_y+qF_z\) for \(dp,dq\). Writing all plus or swapping \(p\) and \(q\) gives wrong integrals.
  • Multipliers must satisfy \(lP+mQ+nR=0\) before you can set \(l\,dx+m\,dy+n\,dz=0\).
  • Cauchy problem: if the data curve is a characteristic, a solution need not exist or be unique. Always check the transversality condition.
  • Applying the Lagrange method to a nonlinear equation such as \(pq=z\) (not of the form \(Pp+Qq=R\)).

Previous-year questions

2025 Q60

Question. The complete solution of \(z=xp+yq+p^2+q^2\), where \(p=\partial z/\partial x,\ q=\partial z/\partial y\), is: (A) \(z=ax+by+a+b\) (B) \(z=ax+by+a^2+b^2\) (C) \(z=x+y+a^2+b^2\) (D) \(z=x+y+a+b\).

Answer. B (official key).

Solution. Clairaut form \(z=px+qy+f(p,q)\) with \(f=p^2+q^2\); replace \(p,q\) by constants \(a,b\) to get \(z=ax+by+a^2+b^2\). Option A has \(f(a,b)=a+b\) instead of \(a^2+b^2\); options C and D are not linear in the constants \(a,b\) multiplying \(x,y\), so \(p=a,\ q=b\) fails.

Quick revision

  • \(F(u,v)=0\) with \(u,v\) in \(x,y,z\) gives \(Pp+Qq=R\); \(z=f(u)\) one function gives a PDE of order 1.
  • Eliminate \(n\) constants (n variables): first order; more: higher order.
  • Lagrange: \(\dfrac{dx}{P}=\dfrac{dy}{Q}=\dfrac{dz}{R}\), two integrals \(u=c_1,\ v=c_2\), answer \(F(u,v)=0\).
  • Multiplier rule: \(\dfrac{l\,dx+m\,dy+n\,dz}{lP+mQ+nR}\); take \(lP+mQ+nR=0\) for an exact integral.
  • Complete integral: 2 constants; general: arbitrary function; singular: envelope (\(f=f_a=f_b=0\)).
  • Clairaut \(z=px+qy+f(p,q)\): complete integral \(z=ax+by+f(a,b)\); singular from \(x+f_a=0,\ y+f_b=0\).
  • \(f(p,q)=0\): \(z=ax+\varphi(a)y+c\). \(f(z,p,q)=0\): \(u=x+ay\). \(f_1(x,p)=f_2(y,q)\): separate with \(a\).
  • Charpit: \(\dfrac{dp}{F_x+pF_z}=\dfrac{dq}{F_y+qF_z}=\dfrac{dz}{-pF_p-qF_q}=\dfrac{dx}{-F_p}=\dfrac{dy}{-F_q}\).
  • Jacobi: for \(F(x_i,p_i)=0\) find involutive \(F_j=a_j\); for two variables it equals Charpit.
  • Cauchy: data curve must not be a characteristic (\(Qx_0'-Py_0'\ne0\)).
