Why it matters for the exam

Two of the three ODE questions in the 2025 paper and two of the four in the 2026 paper came from this topic. They are theory-only: an initial value problem that has no, one or infinitely many solutions (2025 Q56, 2026 Q66), and singular solutions of Clairaut-type equations (2025 Q57, 2026 Q41). Nobody asks for a long integration. The solving techniques below are needed only because they feed these two themes.

Core ideas

Basic language

A first-order ODE is \(F(x,y,y')=0\), written in normal form as \(y'=f(x,y)\). A general solution is a one-parameter family \(\phi(x,y,c)=0\). A particular solution is one member of the family. A singular solution is a solution that cannot be obtained from the general solution by any choice of \(c\). An initial value problem (IVP) is \(y'=f(x,y)\), \(y(x_0)=y_0\). Throughout, \(p=y'=dy/dx\).

Exact equations

\(M\,dx+N\,dy=0\) is exact if there is \(F\) with \(F_x=M\), \(F_y=N\); the solution is \(F(x,y)=c\).

Test. If \(M,N,M_y,N_x\) are continuous on a simply connected region, the equation is exact if and only if \(M_y=N_x\).

Method. Integrate \(M\) with respect to \(x\) (treating \(y\) as constant) to get \(F=\int M\,dx+g(y)\); choose \(g\) so that \(F_y=N\).

Integrating factors

If \(M_y\neq N_x\), look for \(\mu(x,y)\) such that \(\mu M\,dx+\mu N\,dy=0\) is exact.

Condition Integrating factor
\(\dfrac{M_y-N_x}{N}=g(x)\) depends on \(x\) only \(\mu=e^{\int g\,dx}\)
\(\dfrac{N_x-M_y}{M}=h(y)\) depends on \(y\) only \(\mu=e^{\int h\,dy}\)
\(M,N\) homogeneous of the same degree, \(Mx+Ny\ne0\) \(\mu=\dfrac{1}{Mx+Ny}\)
\(M=y\,f(xy)\), \(N=x\,g(xy)\), \(Mx-Ny\ne0\) \(\mu=\dfrac{1}{Mx-Ny}\)

Memory aid: in the first row the sign is \(M_y-N_x\) over \(N\); in the second it flips to \(N_x-M_y\) over \(M\).

Linear equations

\(y'+P(x)y=Q(x)\) has integrating factor \(\mu=e^{\int P\,dx}\), so \(y=e^{-\int P\,dx}\left(\int Q\,e^{\int P\,dx}\,dx+c\right).\)

Bernoulli equations

\(y'+P(x)y=Q(x)y^n\) with \(n\ne0,1\). Divide by \(y^n\) and put \(v=y^{1-n}\); this gives the linear equation \(v'+(1-n)Pv=(1-n)Q\). For \(n>0\) the function \(y=0\) is also a solution and is lost in the division.

Homogeneous equations

\(y'=f(y/x)\): put \(y=vx\), so \(v+xv'=f(v)\) and \(\dfrac{dv}{f(v)-v}=\dfrac{dx}{x}\). An equation \(y'=\dfrac{a_1x+b_1y+c_1}{a_2x+b_2y+c_2}\) is reduced to this form by shifting the origin to the intersection of the two lines (or by \(v=a_1x+b_1y\) when the lines are parallel).

Equations solvable for \(p\): Lagrange and Clairaut

Lagrange (d’Alembert) equation: \(y=x\,\varphi(p)+\psi(p)\).

Differentiate with respect to \(x\) and write \(p'=dp/dx\): \(p=\varphi(p)+\big(x\varphi'(p)+\psi'(p)\big)p'\ \Longrightarrow\ \big(p-\varphi(p)\big)\frac{dx}{dp}=x\,\varphi'(p)+\psi'(p).\) This is linear in \(x\) as a function of \(p\). Solve it, then use the parametric pair \(x=x(p,c)\), \(y=x\varphi(p)+\psi(p)\) as the general solution. Dividing by \(p-\varphi(p)\) loses the roots of \(p=\varphi(p)\): if \(p=p_0\) is a root, then \(y=p_0x+\psi(p_0)\) is an extra solution, usually not in the general family.

Clairaut equation: the special case \(\varphi(p)=p\): \(y=px+f(p).\) Differentiating gives \(\big(x+f'(p)\big)p'=0\).

  • \(p'=0\) gives \(p=c\) and the general solution \(y=cx+f(c)\) (a family of straight lines).
  • \(x+f'(p)=0\) gives the singular solution \(x=-f'(p)\), \(y=px+f(p)\) with \(p\) as parameter. It is the envelope of the family of lines.

Singular solutions: envelope, \(p\)-discriminant, \(c\)-discriminant

A singular solution of a first-order equation is the envelope of the one-parameter family of general solutions (a curve touching every member, with the same tangent there), and it is itself a solution of the ODE.

\(c\)-discriminant. For the family \(\phi(x,y,c)=0\), eliminate \(c\) from \(\phi=0\) and \(\partial\phi/\partial c=0\).

\(p\)-discriminant. For the ODE \(f(x,y,p)=0\), eliminate \(p\) from \(f=0\) and \(\partial f/\partial p=0\).

Both discriminants contain the envelope, but they also contain other loci. The standard table (a locus counted once means its equation appears as a simple factor):

Locus In \(c\)-discriminant In \(p\)-discriminant Solution of the ODE?
Envelope \(E\) once once Yes (singular solution)
Node locus \(N\) twice not present No
Cusp locus \(C\) three times once No
Tac-locus \(T\) not present twice No

Procedure. Compute the discriminant, split into factors, and test each factor: it is a singular solution only if (i) it satisfies the ODE and (ii) it is not a member of the general solution. A factor that is a member for a special \(c\) is a particular solution, not singular. The discriminant is a necessary condition; the check is mandatory.

Orthogonal trajectories

For a family \(F(x,y,c)=0\):

  1. Differentiate and eliminate \(c\) to get \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=f(x,y)\).
  2. Replace \(f\) by \(-1/f\): the orthogonal family satisfies \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=-\dfrac{1}{f(x,y)}\).
  3. Solve.

In polar form, eliminate \(a\) to get an equation in \(r,\theta,dr/d\theta\) and replace \(\dfrac{dr}{d\theta}\) by \(-r^2\dfrac{d\theta}{dr}\).

A family is self-orthogonal if it is its own orthogonal family (confocal parabolas \(y^2=4a(x+a)\) are the standard example).

Existence and uniqueness theorems

IVP: \(y'=f(x,y)\), \(y(x_0)=y_0\). Let \(R=\{\lvert x-x_0\rvert\le a,\ \lvert y-y_0\rvert\le b\}\) be a closed rectangle on which \(f\) is continuous, let \(M=\max_R\lvert f\rvert\) and \(h=\min\{a,\,b/M\}\).

Peano’s theorem. If \(f\) is continuous on \(R\), the IVP has at least one solution on \(\lvert x-x_0\rvert\le h\). Nothing is said about uniqueness.

Picard–Lindelöf theorem. If \(f\) is continuous on \(R\) and satisfies a Lipschitz condition in \(y\), \(\lvert f(x,y_1)-f(x,y_2)\rvert\le L\,\lvert y_1-y_2\rvert\quad\text{for all }(x,y_1),(x,y_2)\in R,\) then the IVP has a unique solution on \(\lvert x-x_0\rvert\le h\). The solution is the uniform limit of the Picard iterates.

Facts about the Lipschitz condition:

  • Sufficient: \(f_y\) exists and is bounded on \(R\) (mean value theorem). Continuity of \(f_y\) on the compact \(R\) is enough.
  • Lipschitz does not need differentiability: \(f=\lvert y\rvert\) is Lipschitz with \(L=1\) although \(f_y\) fails at \(y=0\).
  • Lipschitz implies continuity in \(y\); continuity in \(y\) does not imply Lipschitz. \(y^{1/3}\) and \(\sqrt{\lvert y\rvert}\) are continuous but not Lipschitz near \(y=0\).
  • The conditions are sufficient, not necessary. \(y'=y\ln\lvert y\rvert\), \(y(0)=0\) (with \(f(x,0)=0\)) is not Lipschitz at \(0\) and still has a unique solution, \(y\equiv0\).

Global statements.

  • If \(f\) is continuous and Lipschitz in \(y\) on the whole strip \(a\le x\le b\), \(\lvert y\rvert<\infty\), the unique solution exists on all of \([a,b]\).
  • A linear equation \(y'+P(x)y=Q(x)\) with \(P,Q\) continuous on an interval \(I\) has a unique solution through each point on all of \(I\).
  • If the Lipschitz condition holds only locally, the solution can blow up: \(y'=y^2\), \(y(0)=1\) gives \(y=1/(1-x)\), which exists only for \(x<1\).
  • The number \(h=\min\{a,b/M\}\) is a guaranteed lower bound on the interval of existence, not the maximal interval.

Uniqueness failure: \(y'=y^{1/3}\)

\(f(x,y)=y^{1/3}\) is continuous everywhere, so by Peano a solution through \((0,0)\) exists. It is not Lipschitz at \(y=0\): \(\lvert f(x,y)-f(x,0)\rvert/\lvert y\rvert=\lvert y\rvert^{-2/3}\to\infty\). And uniqueness really fails:

  • \(y\equiv0\) is a solution.
  • Separating variables, \(y^{-1/3}dy=dx\) gives \(\tfrac32y^{2/3}=x-c\), so \(y=\pm\left(\tfrac{2}{3}(x-c)\right)^{3/2}\) for \(x\ge c\).
  • For every \(c\ge0\) the function \(y=0\) for \(x\le c\), \(y=\pm\left(\tfrac23(x-c)\right)^{3/2}\) for \(x>c\) is \(C^1\) and solves the IVP.

So there are infinitely many solutions (not two, not three). The same happens for \(y'=3y^{2/3}\), \(y(0)=0\): \(y=0\), \(y=x^3\) and every glued function \(y=0\) (\(x\le c\)), \(y=(x-c)^3\) (\(x>c\)).

When \(f\) is discontinuous at the initial point

\(x\,y'=4y\), \(y(0)=2\). Here \(y'=4y/x\) is not continuous at \(x=0\), so neither theorem applies; solve directly. \(x\,y'=4y\) gives \(y=Cx^4\) (also on each side of \(0\) separately), hence \(y(0)=0\) always.

IVP Result
\(x y'=4y\), \(y(0)=2\) no solution (\(y(0)=0\ne2\))
\(x y'=4y\), \(y(0)=0\) infinitely many (\(y=Cx^4\), any \(C\); \(C\) may differ on the two sides)
\(x y'=4y\), \(y(1)=2\) unique, \(y=2x^4\) on \(x>0\)

Picard iterates

The IVP is equivalent to the integral equation \(y(x)=y_0+\int_{x_0}^{x}f(t,y(t))\,dt\). The iterates are \(y_0(x)\equiv y_0,\qquad y_{n+1}(x)=y_0+\int_{x_0}^{x}f\big(t,y_n(t)\big)\,dt.\) Under the Picard–Lindelöf hypotheses they converge uniformly on \(\lvert x-x_0\rvert\le h\) to the unique solution. The proof uses \(\lvert y_{n+1}-y_n\rvert\le \frac{M L^{n}\,\lvert x-x_0\rvert^{n+1}}{(n+1)!},\) so \(\sum(y_{n+1}-y_n)\) converges by comparison with \(e^{L h}\). Uniqueness follows from Gronwall’s inequality.

Worked examples

Example 1: integrating factor depending on \(x\)

Solve \((x^2+y^2+x)\,dx+xy\,dy=0\).

\(M=x^2+y^2+x\), \(N=xy\), \(M_y=2y\), \(N_x=y\), not exact. \(\dfrac{M_y-N_x}{N}=\dfrac{y}{xy}=\dfrac1x\), so \(\mu=e^{\int dx/x}=x\).

The equation becomes \((x^3+xy^2+x^2)dx+x^2y\,dy=0\). Integrating the \(dx\) part gives \(F=\dfrac{x^4}{4}+\dfrac{x^2y^2}{2}+\dfrac{x^3}{3}+g(y)\), and \(F_y=x^2y\) forces \(g'=0\). Solution: \(\frac{x^4}{4}+\frac{x^2y^2}{2}+\frac{x^3}{3}=c.\)

Example 2: Bernoulli equation

Solve \(y'+\dfrac yx=xy^2\) for \(x>0\).

Put \(v=y^{-1}\), \(v'=-y^{-2}y'\). Dividing by \(y^2\): \(-v'+\dfrac vx=x\), i.e. \(v'-\dfrac vx=-x\). With \(\mu=1/x\): \((v/x)'=-1\), so \(v/x=-x+c\), \(v=cx-x^2\). Hence \(y=\frac{1}{cx-x^2}\quad(\text{and }y=0).\)

Example 3: Clairaut equation with singular solution

Solve \(y=px+p^2\).

General solution: \(y=cx+c^2\). For the singular solution use \(x=-f'(p)=-2p\), so \(p=-x/2\) and \(y=px+p^2=-\dfrac{x^2}{2}+\dfrac{x^2}{4}\): \(y=-\frac{x^2}{4}.\) Check: \(p=-x/2\) gives \(px+p^2=-x^2/2+x^2/4=-x^2/4=y\). It is not in the family (each line \(y=cx+c^2\) touches the parabola at \(x=-2c\)). Variant: \(y=px-p^2\) has singular solution \(y=x^2/4\) (sign matters).

Example 4: Lagrange equation

Solve \(y=2px+p^2\).

Here \(\varphi(p)=2p\), \(\psi(p)=p^2\). Differentiating: \(p=2p+(2x+2p)p'\), so \(-p=2(x+p)\dfrac{dp}{dx}\), i.e. \(\dfrac{dx}{dp}+\dfrac{2}{p}x=-2\). With \(\mu=p^2\): \((xp^2)'=-2p^2\), \(xp^2=-\dfrac{2p^3}{3}+c\). General solution (parametric): \(x=-\frac{2p}{3}+\frac{c}{p^2},\qquad y=2px+p^2=\frac{2c}{p}-\frac{p^2}{3}.\) Also \(p=\varphi(p)\) means \(p=0\), giving the extra solution \(y=0\), which is not obtained for finite \(c\).

Example 5: singular solution from the \(p\)-discriminant (2025 pattern)

Find the singular solution of \(p^3-4xyp+8y^2=0\).

The \(p\)-discriminant of \(p^3+Bp+C\) is \(-4B^3-27C^2\), with \(B=-4xy\), \(C=8y^2\): \(256x^3y^3-1728y^4=64y^3(4x^3-27y)\). Candidates: \(y=0\) and \(27y=4x^3\).

Direct check of the general solution: \(y=c(x-c)^2\) satisfies the ODE (substitute \(p=2c(x-c)\): \(p^3-4xyp+8y^2=8c^2(x-c)^3\,[\,c-x+(x-c)\,]=0\)). Its \(c\)-discriminant: \(\phi_c=(x-c)(3c-x)\), so \(c=x\) gives \(y=0\) and \(c=x/3\) gives \(y=\dfrac{4x^3}{27}\).

  • \(y=0\) is the member \(c=0\) of the family, so it is a particular solution, not singular.
  • \(y=4x^3/27\) satisfies the ODE (\(p=4x^2/9\): \(\frac{64}{729}-\frac{192}{729}+\frac{128}{729}=0\)) and is not in the family. It is the envelope.

Singular solution: \(27y=4x^3\).

Example 6: orthogonal trajectories

(a) Family \(y=cx^2\): \(y'=2cx=2y/x\). Orthogonal: \(y'=-\dfrac{x}{2y}\), so \(2y\,dy=-x\,dx\) and \(x^2+2y^2=k\) (ellipses).

(b) Cardioids \(r=a(1+\cos\theta)\): \(\dfrac1r\dfrac{dr}{d\theta}=-\dfrac{\sin\theta}{1+\cos\theta}=-\tan\dfrac\theta2\). Replace \(dr/d\theta\) by \(-r^2d\theta/dr\): \(r\dfrac{d\theta}{dr}=\tan\dfrac\theta2\), so \(\dfrac{dr}{r}=\cot\dfrac\theta2\,d\theta\), giving \(r=k\sin^2\dfrac\theta2=\dfrac k2(1-\cos\theta)\).

Example 7: Picard iterates

\(y'=1+y^2\), \(y(0)=0\). \(y_0=0\), \(y_1=x\), \(y_2=x+\dfrac{x^3}{3}\), \(y_3=\int_0^x\left(1+\left(t+\dfrac{t^3}{3}\right)^2\right)dt=x+\dfrac{x^3}{3}+\dfrac{2x^5}{15}+\dfrac{x^7}{63}\). The exact solution is \(\tan x=x+\dfrac{x^3}{3}+\dfrac{2x^5}{15}+\dfrac{17x^7}{315}+\cdots\); \(y_3\) agrees with \(\tan x\) through the \(x^5\) term.

Also: for \(y'=x+y\), \(y(0)=0\) the iterates are \(\dfrac{x^2}{2},\ \dfrac{x^2}{2}+\dfrac{x^3}{6},\ \dfrac{x^2}{2}+\dfrac{x^3}{6}+\dfrac{x^4}{24},\dots\to e^x-1-x\).

Common traps

  • Peano versus Picard. Continuity gives existence only. Uniqueness needs the Lipschitz condition in \(y\). An option saying “continuity of \(f\) guarantees a unique solution” is false.
  • Lipschitz does not mean differentiable. \(f=\lvert y\rvert\) is Lipschitz, so \(y'=\lvert y\rvert\), \(y(0)=0\) has a unique solution.
  • The theorems are sufficient only. Failure of the Lipschitz condition does not prove non-uniqueness; check directly (\(y'=y\ln\lvert y\rvert\)).
  • Discontinuity at the initial point. In \(x\,y'=4y\), \(y(0)=2\) the hypotheses fail; the answer (“no solution”) comes from solving, not from a theorem.
  • Counting solutions of \(y'=y^{1/3}\). The answer is infinitely many, not “exactly two” (\(y=0\) and \(y=(2x/3)^{3/2}\)) because the solutions can be glued at any \(c\ge0\).
  • Discriminant is only a candidate. The discriminants can contain node, cusp and tac loci that are not solutions; always test that the factor satisfies the ODE and is not a member of the family. A factor like \(y=0\) above is a member (\(c=0\)).
  • Sign slips in Clairaut. \(y=px+f(p)\) gives \(x=-f'(p)\). For \(f=p^2\) the singular solution is \(y=-x^2/4\); for \(y=px-p^2\) it is \(y=x^2/4\).
  • Lagrange equation. Dividing by \(p-\varphi(p)\) drops the solutions \(y=p_0x+\psi(p_0)\) with \(p_0=\varphi(p_0)\).
  • Integrating factor sign. Use \((M_y-N_x)/N\) for \(\mu(x)\) and \((N_x-M_y)/M\) for \(\mu(y)\).
  • Bernoulli. The substitution is \(v=y^{1-n}\), not \(y^{n}\) or \(y^{-n}\).
  • Local versus global. \(h=\min(a,b/M)\) is only a guaranteed interval; \(y'=y^2\) shows solutions can blow up in finite \(x\).

Previous-year questions

2025 Q56

Question. The initial value problem \(x\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}=4y\), \(y(0)=2\) has: (A) a unique solution (B) no solution (C) two solutions (D) infinite solutions.

Answer: B.

Solving \(x\,y'=4y\) gives \(y=Cx^4\), so \(y(0)=0\) for every \(C\), never \(2\). No solution exists. (The theorems do not apply: \(4y/x\) is discontinuous at \(x=0\).)

2025 Q57

Question. A singular solution of \(\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)^3-4xy\dfrac{dy}{dx}+8y^2=0\) is: (A) \(27y=4x^2\) (B) \(9y^2=4x^3\) (C) \(3y=4x^2\) (D) \(27y=4x^3\).

Answer: D.

\(p\)-discriminant \(=256x^3y^3-1728y^4=64y^3(4x^3-27y)\). The factor \(4x^3-27y\) gives \(27y=4x^3\). It satisfies the ODE and is the envelope of the general solution \(y=c(x-c)^2\). Worked in Example 5. The factor \(y=0\) is the particular solution \(c=0\).

2026 Q41

Question (restated). For the equation \((y-px)^2=p^2(1+p^2)\), what is the nature of the singular solution relative to the general solution? (A) does not exist (B) envelope of the general solution (C) same as the general solution (D) constant solution.

Answer: B (envelope of the general solution).

Taking the square root, \(y=px\pm p\sqrt{1+p^2}\), which is a Clairaut equation \(y=px+f(p)\). Its general solution is the family of straight lines \(y=cx\pm c\sqrt{1+c^2}\). The singular solution comes from \(x=-f'(p)\) and is the envelope of those lines; it is not a member of the family. (Source note: no official key exists for 2026; B is the worked-key answer and agrees with the mathematics. The exam photo carries a candidate’s pen mark near C, which is not a key.)

2026 Q66

Question (restated). For the IVP \(y'=y^{1/3}\), \(y(0)=0\), the number of solutions is: unique, none, infinitely many, or exactly two.

Answer: C (infinitely many).

\(f=y^{1/3}\) is continuous (so a solution exists by Peano) but not Lipschitz at \(y=0\). The solution \(y\equiv0\) and \(y=\pm(2x/3)^{3/2}\) (\(x\ge0\)) can be glued at any \(c\ge0\), giving infinitely many solutions.

Quick revision

  • Exact: \(M_y=N_x\); integrating factor \(e^{\int(M_y-N_x)/N\,dx}\) if that ratio depends on \(x\) alone.
  • Linear \(y'+Py=Q\): \(\mu=e^{\int P}\). Bernoulli: \(v=y^{1-n}\).
  • Clairaut \(y=px+f(p)\): general \(y=cx+f(c)\), singular from \(x=-f'(p)\).
  • Lagrange \(y=x\varphi(p)+\psi(p)\): linear in \(x\) over \(p\); roots of \(p=\varphi(p)\) give extra lines.
  • Singular solution: in the discriminants, must satisfy the ODE and not belong to the family.
  • Envelope shows once in both discriminants; node twice in \(c\); cusp three times in \(c\); tac-locus twice in \(p\).
  • Orthogonal trajectories: replace \(y'\) by \(-1/y'\).
  • Peano: continuity gives existence only. Picard–Lindelöf: continuity plus Lipschitz in \(y\) gives existence and uniqueness.
  • \(f_y\) bounded implies Lipschitz; Lipschitz does not need \(f_y\) to exist (\(\lvert y\rvert\)).
  • \(y'=y^{1/3}\), \(y(0)=0\): infinitely many solutions. \(x y'=4y\): \(y(0)=2\) none, \(y(0)=0\) infinitely many.
  • Picard iterates \(y_{n+1}=y_0+\int f(t,y_n)\,dt\) converge uniformly to the unique solution.
  • Guaranteed interval \(h=\min(a,b/M)\) is only a lower bound.
