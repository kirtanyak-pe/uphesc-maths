Why it matters for the exam

Both papers carry one computational question and one theory question from here: a particular integral of a constant-coefficient equation (2025 Q54) and the Wronskian through Abel’s formula (2026 Q91). Expect the operator shortcuts \(\frac{1}{f(D)}\), Wronskian facts, Cauchy-Euler equations, variation of parameters and the form of series solutions. Linear systems and power series have not been asked in these two papers; they sit under the syllabus phrase “general theory” and are standard MSc-level material.

Core ideas

Structure of the solution set

The linear equation of order \(n\) is \(L[y]=y^{(n)}+p_1(x)y^{(n-1)}+\dots+p_n(x)y=r(x).\)

Existence and uniqueness. If \(p_1,\dots,p_n,r\) are continuous on an interval \(I\) and \(x_0\in I\), then for any initial data \(y(x_0),\dots,y^{(n-1)}(x_0)\) there is a unique solution, and it exists on all of \(I\).

Consequences.

  • The solutions of \(L[y]=0\) form an \(n\)-dimensional vector space.
  • Every solution of \(L[y]=r\) is \(y=y_h+y_p\) (\(y_h\) the complementary function, \(y_p\) one particular integral).
  • If \(y_p\) and \(\tilde y_p\) both solve \(L[y]=r\), their difference solves \(L[y]=0\).
  • Superposition: if \(L[y_1]=r_1\) and \(L[y_2]=r_2\) then \(L[y_1+y_2]=r_1+r_2\).

Linear dependence and the Wronskian

The Wronskian of \(y_1,\dots,y_n\) is the determinant \(W(x)=\begin{vmatrix}y_1&\cdots&y_n\\ y_1'&\cdots&y_n'\\ \vdots&&\vdots\\ y_1^{(n-1)}&\cdots&y_n^{(n-1)}\end{vmatrix},\qquad W(y_1,y_2)=y_1y_2'-y_1'y_2.\)

  • If \(y_1,\dots,y_n\) are linearly dependent on \(I\), then \(W\equiv0\) on \(I\).
  • The converse is false for arbitrary functions. On \(\mathbb R\), \(y_1=x^2\) and \(y_2=x\lvert x\rvert\) are linearly independent but \(W\equiv0\).
  • The converse is true for solutions of one linear homogeneous equation with continuous coefficients: \(W\equiv0\) \(\iff\) \(W(x_0)=0\) at one point \(\iff\) dependent. For such solutions \(W\) is either never zero or identically zero.
  • A fundamental set is \(n\) linearly independent solutions of \(L[y]=0\); equivalently \(W\neq0\).

Abel’s formula. For solutions of \(y^{(n)}+p_1y^{(n-1)}+\dots=0\), \(W(x)=W(x_0)\exp\!\left(-\int_{x_0}^{x}p_1(t)\,dt\right).\) For \(y''+p\,y'+q\,y=0\): \(W=C\,e^{-\int p\,dx}\). For \(a_0y''+a_1y'+a_2y=0\): \(W=C\exp\left(-\int\frac{a_1}{a_0}dx\right)\). The proof: \(W'=-p_1W\). For \(n=1\) the Wronskian of one solution is the solution itself, \(y_1=Ce^{-\int p_1}\).

Typical consequences:

  • \(y'+\frac1xy=0\) or \(y''+\frac1xy'+\dots=0\) (\(x>0\)): \(W=C/x\), so \(xW\) is constant. Bessel’s equation \(x^2y''+xy'+(x^2-\nu^2)y=0\) has \(W[J_\nu,Y_\nu]=\frac{2}{\pi x}\).
  • \(y''-2xy'+\dots=0\) (Hermite): \(W=Ce^{x^2}\).
  • \(y''+q(x)y=0\) (no \(y'\) term): \(W\) is constant.
  • \((1-x^2)y''-2xy'+\dots=0\) (Legendre): \(W=\dfrac{C}{1-x^2}\).

Second solution from the Wronskian. If \(y_1\) is known, \(y_2=y_1\displaystyle\int\frac{W}{y_1^2}\,dx\).

Constant-coefficient equations

For \(f(D)y=r(x)\) with \(D=d/dx\), \(f(D)=D^n+a_1D^{n-1}+\dots+a_n\): the auxiliary equation is \(f(m)=0\).

Roots of \(f(m)=0\) Contribution to the complementary function (CF)
real, simple \(m\) \(e^{mx}\)
real, multiplicity \(k\) \((c_0+c_1x+\dots+c_{k-1}x^{k-1})e^{mx}\)
\(\alpha\pm i\beta\), simple \(e^{\alpha x}(A\cos\beta x+B\sin\beta x)\)
\(\alpha\pm i\beta\), multiplicity \(k\) \(e^{\alpha x}\big[P_{k-1}(x)\cos\beta x+Q_{k-1}(x)\sin\beta x\big]\) with polynomials of degree at most \(k-1\)

Example: \((D^2+1)^2y=0\) has CF \((c_1+c_2x)\cos x+(c_3+c_4x)\sin x\).

Particular integral shortcuts

Write \(y_p=\dfrac{1}{f(D)}r\).

Right side \(r\) Rule
\(e^{ax}\) \(\dfrac{e^{ax}}{f(a)}\) if \(f(a)\ne0\). If \(a\) is a root of multiplicity \(k\): \(\dfrac{x^k e^{ax}}{f^{(k)}(a)}\)
\(\sin(ax+b)\) or \(\cos(ax+b)\), \(f(D)=\varphi(D^2)\) replace \(D^2\) by \(-a^2\): \(\dfrac{\sin(ax+b)}{\varphi(-a^2)}\) if \(\varphi(-a^2)\ne0\)
resonance, \(f=D^2+a^2\) \(\dfrac{1}{D^2+a^2}\sin ax=-\dfrac{x\cos ax}{2a}\), \(\ \dfrac{1}{D^2+a^2}\cos ax=\dfrac{x\sin ax}{2a}\)
polynomial \(x^m\) expand \(\dfrac{1}{f(D)}\) as a power series in \(D\) (lowest terms first) and apply to \(x^m\)
\(e^{ax}V(x)\) \(e^{ax}\dfrac{1}{f(D+a)}V(x)\) (shift)
\(xV(x)\) \(x\dfrac{1}{f(D)}V-\dfrac{f'(D)}{[f(D)]^2}V\)
\(e^{ax}\) and \((D-a)^k\) \(\dfrac{1}{(D-a)^k}e^{ax}=\dfrac{x^ke^{ax}}{k!}\)

When \(f(D)\) has a factor \(D^s\), use \(\dfrac1{D}=\int\), i.e. integrate \(s\) times after dealing with the remaining factor.

If \(r\) is a sum, take a particular integral for each term and add. The method of undetermined coefficients gives the same answer: try the form of \(r\), multiplied by \(x^k\) if it overlaps the CF.

Variation of parameters

For \(y''+p\,y'+q\,y=r\) with fundamental set \(y_1,y_2\) and Wronskian \(W=y_1y_2'-y_1'y_2\): \(y_p=-y_1\int\frac{y_2\,r}{W}\,dx+y_2\int\frac{y_1\,r}{W}\,dx.\) The equation must be in standard form (coefficient of \(y''\) equal to \(1\)). For order \(n\): \(y_p=\sum_k y_k\int\frac{W_k\,r}{W}dx\), where \(W_k\) is \(W\) with its \(k\)-th column replaced by \((0,\dots,0,1)^T\).

Cauchy-Euler equations

\(x^2y''+axy'+by=r(x)\) (\(x>0\); for \(x<0\) use \(\lvert x\rvert\)). Substituting \(x=e^t\) and \(\theta=\frac{d}{dt}\) gives \(x\,y'=\theta y\), \(x^2y''=\theta(\theta-1)y\), so the equation has constant coefficients in \(t\). Trial solution \(y=x^m\): \(m(m-1)+am+b=0.\)

Roots Solution
real distinct \(m_1,m_2\) \(c_1x^{m_1}+c_2x^{m_2}\)
repeated \(m\) \((c_1+c_2\ln x)\,x^m\)
\(\alpha\pm i\beta\) \(x^\alpha\big[A\cos(\beta\ln x)+B\sin(\beta\ln x)\big]\)

Legendre’s linear equation \((ax+b)^2y''+\dots\) is handled with \(ax+b=e^t\). For a non-homogeneous Cauchy-Euler equation with \(r=x^k\), in the variable \(t\) the right side is \(e^{kt}\) and the usual exponential rule applies.

Reduction of order

If \(y_1\ne0\) solves \(y''+py'+qy=0\), put \(y=v\,y_1\). Then \(v'=\dfrac{W}{y_1^2}=\dfrac{Ce^{-\int p}}{y_1^2}\) and \(y_2=y_1\int\frac{e^{-\int p\,dx}}{y_1^2}\,dx.\) The normal form \(y=u\,e^{-\frac12\int p}\) removes the first-derivative term: \(u''+\left(q-\frac{p^2}{4}-\frac{p'}{2}\right)u=0\). It has the same zeros as \(y\).

Linear systems \(x'=Ax\)

\(A\) is a constant \(n\times n\) matrix. The solution with \(x(0)=x_0\) is \(x=e^{At}x_0\). A fundamental matrix \(\Phi(t)\) has \(n\) independent solutions as columns; \(e^{At}=\Phi(t)\Phi(0)^{-1}\).

  • Real eigenvalue \(\lambda\), eigenvector \(v\): solution \(e^{\lambda t}v\). \(n\) distinct eigenvalues give a fundamental set.
  • Complex pair \(\lambda=\alpha\pm i\beta\), eigenvector \(v=a+ib\): two real solutions \(e^{\alpha t}(a\cos\beta t-b\sin\beta t)\) and \(e^{\alpha t}(a\sin\beta t+b\cos\beta t)\).
  • Repeated eigenvalue with a missing eigenvector (defective): \(x=e^{\lambda t}(tv+w)\) where \((A-\lambda I)w=v\).
  • Liouville (Abel) formula: \(\det\Phi(t)=\det\Phi(0)\,e^{t\operatorname{tr}A}\).
  • Non-homogeneous \(x'=Ax+g(t)\): \(x=\Phi(t)\left[\Phi(0)^{-1}x_0+\int_0^t\Phi(s)^{-1}g(s)\,ds\right]\).
  • An \(n\)-th order equation is the system with companion matrix; for \(y''+py'+qy=0\), \(A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-q&-p\end{pmatrix}\), \(\operatorname{tr}A=-p\), which recovers Abel’s formula.

Phase portrait of a \(2\times2\) system (eigenvalues \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\)):

Eigenvalues Type of origin
both real, both negative stable node
both real, both positive unstable node
real, opposite signs saddle (unstable)
\(\alpha\pm i\beta\), \(\alpha<0\) stable spiral
\(\alpha\pm i\beta\), \(\alpha>0\) unstable spiral
purely imaginary \(\pm i\beta\) centre (stable, not asymptotically)

Power series solutions

Consider \(y''+P(x)y'+Q(x)y=0\).

Ordinary point. \(x_0\) is ordinary if \(P\) and \(Q\) are analytic at \(x_0\). Then there are two independent solutions \(y=\sum_{n\ge0}a_n(x-x_0)^n\) (determined by \(a_0,a_1\)), and the radius of convergence is at least the distance from \(x_0\) to the nearest singular point of \(P,Q\) in the complex plane. Substitute the series, collect powers of \(x\), get a recurrence.

Regular singular point (Frobenius, statement only). \(x_0\) is a singular point; it is regular if \((x-x_0)P\) and \((x-x_0)^2Q\) are analytic at \(x_0\), otherwise irregular. Put \(p_0=\lim(x-x_0)P\), \(q_0=\lim(x-x_0)^2Q\). The indicial equation is \(r(r-1)+p_0r+q_0=0.\) With roots \(r_1\ge r_2\) (real):

  • \(r_1-r_2\) not an integer: two Frobenius series \(x^{r_1}\sum a_nx^n\) and \(x^{r_2}\sum b_nx^n\).
  • \(r_1=r_2\): one Frobenius series, the second solution contains \(\ln x\).
  • \(r_1-r_2\) a positive integer: the series for \(r_1\) always exists; the second solution may contain a \(\ln x\) term.

Examples: Bessel \(x^2y''+xy'+(x^2-\nu^2)y=0\) has indicial roots \(\pm\nu\). \(2xy''+y'+y=0\) has roots \(0,\frac12\).

Worked examples

Example 1: Wronskian by Abel’s formula

\((1-x^2)y''-2xy'+6y=0\) on \((-1,1)\). Two solutions have \(W(0)=3\). Find \(W(x)\).

\(p=\dfrac{-2x}{1-x^2}\), so \(-\int p\,dx=\int\dfrac{2x}{1-x^2}dx=-\ln(1-x^2)\). Hence \(W=\dfrac{C}{1-x^2}\), and \(W(0)=3\) gives \(C=3\): \(W(x)=\frac{3}{1-x^2}.\)

Example 2: particular integral (2025 type)

Find a particular integral of \(y''-2y'+5y=e^{2x}\sin x\).

\(f(D)=D^2-2D+5\). Shift: \(\dfrac{1}{f(D)}e^{2x}\sin x=e^{2x}\dfrac{1}{f(D+2)}\sin x\) with \(f(D+2)=(D+2)^2-2(D+2)+5=D^2+2D+5\). On \(\sin x\), \(D^2=-1\), so \(f(D+2)\to 4+2D\): \(\frac{1}{4+2D}\sin x=\frac{4-2D}{16-4D^2}\sin x=\frac{4-2D}{20}\sin x=\frac{4\sin x-2\cos x}{20}.\) \(y_p=\frac{e^{2x}}{10}\big(2\sin x-\cos x\big)=-\frac{1}{10}e^{2x}(\cos x-2\sin x).\) The CF is \(e^{x}(A\cos2x+B\sin2x)\) (roots \(1\pm2i\)).

Example 3: resonance

(a) \((D-2)^2y=e^{2x}\): \(2\) is a double root, so \(y_p=\dfrac{x^2e^{2x}}{2!}=\dfrac{x^2e^{2x}}{2}\).

(b) \(y''+4y=\sin2x\): \(\dfrac{1}{D^2+4}\sin2x=-\dfrac{x\cos2x}{4}\). General solution \(y=c_1\cos2x+c_2\sin2x-\dfrac{x\cos2x}{4}\).

Example 4: variation of parameters

\(y''+y=\sec x\). \(y_1=\cos x\), \(y_2=\sin x\), \(W=1\). \(y_p=-\cos x\int\sin x\sec x\,dx+\sin x\int\cos x\sec x\,dx=\cos x\,\ln\lvert\cos x\rvert+x\sin x.\)

Example 5: Cauchy-Euler, non-homogeneous

\(x^2y''-2xy'+2y=x^3\). With \(x=e^t\): \(\big(\theta(\theta-1)-2\theta+2\big)y=(\theta-1)(\theta-2)y=e^{3t}\). CF: \(c_1x+c_2x^2\). PI: \(\dfrac{e^{3t}}{(3-1)(3-2)}=\dfrac{x^3}{2}\). General solution \(y=c_1x+c_2x^2+\dfrac{x^3}{2}\).

Example 6: linear system and power series

(a) \(x'=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&2\end{pmatrix}x\). \(\lambda^2-3\lambda-4=0\), so \(\lambda=4,-1\). For \(\lambda=4\): \(v=(2,3)^T\). For \(\lambda=-1\): \(v=(1,-1)^T\). \(x=c_1e^{4t}\binom23+c_2e^{-t}\binom{1}{-1}\). Saddle, since eigenvalues have opposite signs. \(\det\Phi(t)/\det\Phi(0)=e^{3t}\) (trace \(3\)).

(b) Airy equation \(y''-xy=0\) about \(0\). With \(y=\sum a_nx^n\): \((n+2)(n+1)a_{n+2}=a_{n-1}\) for \(n\ge1\), \(a_2=0\). Hence \(y=a_0\Big(1+\frac{x^3}{6}+\frac{x^6}{180}+\cdots\Big)+a_1\Big(x+\frac{x^4}{12}+\frac{x^7}{504}+\cdots\Big).\) Since \(P,Q\) are entire, the series converge for all \(x\).

Common traps

  • Abel’s formula needs the leading coefficient \(1\). For \(a_0(x)y''+a_1(x)y'+\dots\) use \(\exp(-\int a_1/a_0)\), not \(\exp(-\int a_1)\). The sign in the exponent is minus.
  • \(W\equiv0\) does not imply dependence unless the functions are solutions of the same linear equation (counterexample \(x^2\), \(x\lvert x\rvert\)).
  • No \(y'\) term means constant Wronskian. \(y''+q(x)y=0\) has \(W=\text{const}\).
  • Shift rule. \(\frac{1}{f(D)}e^{ax}V=e^{ax}\frac{1}{f(D+a)}V\); forgetting to shift \(D\) to \(D+a\) gives a wrong answer.
  • Resonance. If \(f(a)=0\) the rule \(e^{ax}/f(a)\) is invalid; use the \(x^k\) rule. For \(\sin ax\) with \(D^2+a^2\), the answer has a factor \(x\) and the sign is \(-\frac{x\cos ax}{2a}\).
  • Variation of parameters requires \(y''\) with coefficient \(1\); with other leading coefficients divide first.
  • Cauchy-Euler repeated root gives \(x^m\ln x\), not \(x^{m+1}\); complex roots give \(\cos(\beta\ln x)\), not \(\cos\beta x\).
  • Radius of convergence uses the distance to the nearest complex singularity, not only real ones: \((x^2-2x+5)y''+\dots\) about \(0\) has radius at least \(\sqrt5\) (singularities \(1\pm2i\)).
  • Systems. For complex eigenvalues use real and imaginary parts of \(e^{\lambda t}v\). For a defective eigenvalue, do not write \(e^{\lambda t}v\) twice.
  • Existence is on the whole interval of continuity for linear equations, unlike non-linear ones.
  • Frobenius. Roots differing by an integer do not always give a log term; the larger root always gives a series.

Previous-year questions

2025 Q54

Question. The particular integral of \(\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}-2\dfrac{dy}{dx}+5y=e^{2x}\sin x\) is: (A) \(-\frac{1}{10}e^{2x}\cos x\) (B) \(-\frac{1}{10}e^{2x}\sin x\) (C) \(-\frac{1}{10}e^{2x}(\cos x-2\sin x)\) (D) \(-\frac{1}{10}e^{2x}(\sin x-2\cos x)\).

Answer: C.

By the shift rule, \(y_p=e^{2x}\dfrac{1}{D^2+2D+5}\sin x=e^{2x}\dfrac{4\sin x-2\cos x}{20}=\dfrac{e^{2x}}{10}(2\sin x-\cos x)\). This is Example 2 above.

2026 Q91

Question. Let \(y'+\dfrac1x\,y=x^2\) for \(x>0\). If \(W(x)\) is the Wronskian of the homogeneous part, evaluate \(W(x)\cdot x\): (A) constant (B) \(x\) (C) \(x^2\) (D) \(1/x\).

Answer: A (worked key, unofficial; the mathematics agrees).

The homogeneous part is the first-order equation \(y'+y/x=0\), so \(n=1\) and the “Wronskian” of one solution \(y_1\) is just \(y_1\). Abel’s formula gives \(W=C\exp\left(-\int\frac1x\,dx\right)=\dfrac Cx\), and indeed \(y_1=C/x\). Hence \(xW=C\) is constant. (Pen marks on the exam photo are a candidate’s, not a key.)

Quick revision

  • Linear \(n\)-th order with continuous coefficients on \(I\): unique solution on all of \(I\); solution space has dimension \(n\).
  • \(W\equiv0\) and dependence are equivalent only for solutions of the same linear ODE.
  • Abel: \(W=W(x_0)e^{-\int p_1}\); for \(y''+py'+qy=0\), \(W=Ce^{-\int p}\); no \(y'\) term gives constant \(W\).
  • \(\frac{1}{f(D)}e^{ax}=\frac{e^{ax}}{f(a)}\); if \(a\) is a \(k\)-fold root, \(\frac{x^ke^{ax}}{f^{(k)}(a)}\).
  • \(\frac{1}{D^2+a^2}\sin ax=-\frac{x\cos ax}{2a}\), \(\frac{1}{D^2+a^2}\cos ax=\frac{x\sin ax}{2a}\).
  • Shift: \(\frac{1}{f(D)}e^{ax}V=e^{ax}\frac{1}{f(D+a)}V\).
  • Variation of parameters: \(y_p=-y_1\int\frac{y_2r}{W}+y_2\int\frac{y_1r}{W}\).
  • Cauchy-Euler: \(x=e^t\); \(m(m-1)+am+b=0\); repeated root gives \(x^m\ln x\).
  • Reduction of order: \(y_2=y_1\int\frac{e^{-\int p}}{y_1^2}dx\).
  • \(x'=Ax\): solutions \(e^{\lambda t}v\); \(\det\Phi=\det\Phi(0)e^{t\operatorname{tr}A}\); saddle if eigenvalues are real with opposite signs.
  • Series at an ordinary point converge at least up to the nearest (complex) singularity.
  • Frobenius indicial equation \(r(r-1)+p_0r+q_0=0\); equal roots give a \(\ln\) in the second solution.
