Why it matters for the exam

Only one question so far (2026 Q57: eigenvalues of \(y''+\lambda y=0\), \(y(0)=y(\pi)=0\)), but the same machinery sits behind the separation-of-variables questions in the PDE topics and behind Green’s function questions in the integral-equations unit. Likely forms: the eigenvalues for a given boundary condition, which property holds for a Sturm-Liouville problem (real eigenvalues, orthogonality with weight), conversion to Sturm-Liouville form, zeros of solutions (Sturm theorems) and Green’s functions.

Core ideas

Boundary value problems

A boundary value problem (BVP) prescribes conditions at two points. Unlike an initial value problem, it may have no solution, one, or infinitely many, even for linear equations.

  • \(y''+y=0\), \(y(0)=0\), \(y(\pi)=1\): no solution (\(y=A\cos x+B\sin x\), \(y(0)=0\) forces \(A=0\), then \(y(\pi)=0\)).
  • \(y''+y=0\), \(y(0)=0\), \(y(\pi)=0\): infinitely many (\(y=B\sin x\)).
  • \(y''+y=0\), \(y(0)=0\), \(y(\pi/2)=1\): unique, \(y=\sin x\).

Common boundary conditions at \(x=a\) (and similarly at \(b\)): Dirichlet \(y=0\); Neumann \(y'=0\); Robin \(\alpha y+\beta y'=0\); periodic \(y(a)=y(b)\), \(y'(a)=y'(b)\).

Fredholm alternative for BVPs. For \(L[y]=f\) with homogeneous self-adjoint boundary conditions: if the homogeneous problem \(L[y]=0\) has only \(y=0\), then \(L[y]=f\) has a unique solution for every \(f\). If it has a non-trivial solution \(\phi\), then \(L[y]=f\) has a solution only if \(\int f\phi\,dx=0\) (and then it has infinitely many).

Example: \(y''+y=1\), \(y(0)=y(\pi)=0\). Here \(\phi=\sin x\) and \(\int_0^\pi\sin x\,dx=2\ne0\), so there is no solution.

The Sturm-Liouville form

The Sturm-Liouville (SL) equation is

\[\big(p(x)y'\big)'+q(x)y+\lambda\,w(x)y=0,\qquad a\le x\le b,\]

with boundary conditions

\[\alpha_1y(a)+\alpha_2y'(a)=0,\qquad \beta_1y(b)+\beta_2y'(b)=0\]

(real constants, \((\alpha_1,\alpha_2)\ne(0,0)\), \((\beta_1,\beta_2)\ne(0,0)\)).

It is regular if \(p,p',q,w\) are continuous on the closed interval \([a,b]\) and \(p>0\), \(w>0\) on \([a,b]\). It is singular if \(p\) or \(w\) vanishes at an endpoint, or the interval is infinite (Legendre, Bessel, Hermite and Laguerre problems are singular). The function \(w\) is the weight function.

Converting to SL form. Given \(a_0(x)y''+a_1(x)y'+\big(a_2(x)+\lambda a_3(x)\big)y=0\), multiply by \(\dfrac{1}{a_0}\exp\left(\int\frac{a_1}{a_0}dx\right)\):

\[p=\exp\!\left(\int\frac{a_1}{a_0}dx\right),\quad q=p\,\frac{a_2}{a_0},\quad w=p\,\frac{a_3}{a_0}.\]

Self-adjoint test. \(L=a_0y''+a_1y'+a_2y\) is already in SL form (self-adjoint) exactly when \(a_1=a_0'\). Then \(L=(a_0y')'+a_2y\). If \(a_1\ne a_0'\), multiply by \(\dfrac{1}{a_0}\exp\int\dfrac{a_1}{a_0}dx\) as above. Example: \(x^2y''+kxy'+y\) is self-adjoint iff \(kx=2x\), i.e. \(k=2\). Another: \(y''+4y'+\lambda y=0\) has \(p=e^{4x}\) and \(w=e^{4x}\), giving \((e^{4x}y')'+\lambda e^{4x}y=0\).

Bessel problem. For \(xy''+y'+\left(\lambda x-\dfrac{\nu^2}{x}\right)y=0\) on \([0,1]\) with \(y\) bounded and \(y(1)=0\), the eigenvalues are \(\lambda_n=\mu_n^2\) where \(J_\nu(\mu_n)=0\), and \(\phi_n=J_\nu(\mu_nx)\). Orthogonality has weight \(x\): \(\displaystyle\int_0^1xJ_\nu(\mu_mx)J_\nu(\mu_nx)\,dx=0\) for \(m\ne n\), and \(\displaystyle\int_0^1xJ_\nu(\mu_nx)^2dx=\tfrac12J_{\nu+1}(\mu_n)^2\). The Fourier-Bessel expansion is \(f=\sum c_nJ_\nu(\mu_nx)\) with \(c_n=\dfrac{2}{J_{\nu+1}(\mu_n)^2}\displaystyle\int_0^1xf(x)J_\nu(\mu_nx)\,dx\). These problems are singular, so the regular-problem theorems (simplicity, oscillation count) are used with care; the real eigenvalues and orthogonality still hold.

Properties of a regular SL problem (separated boundary conditions)

  1. Eigenvalues are real.
  2. Eigenfunctions for different eigenvalues are orthogonal with respect to the weight: \(\displaystyle\int_a^b w(x)\,\phi_m(x)\phi_n(x)\,dx=0\) for \(\lambda_m\ne\lambda_n\).
  3. Each eigenvalue is simple: its eigenspace is one-dimensional.
  4. The eigenvalues form a countable increasing sequence \(\lambda_1<\lambda_2<\cdots\to+\infty\), bounded below.
  5. Oscillation: the eigenfunction \(\phi_n\) has exactly \(n-1\) zeros in \((a,b)\).
  6. Completeness: \(\{\phi_n\}\) is a complete orthogonal set in \(L^2_w[a,b]\); \(f=\sum c_n\phi_n\) with \(c_n=\dfrac{\int w f\phi_n}{\int w\phi_n^2}\), converging in mean for \(f\in L^2_w\), and pointwise (to the average at a jump) for piecewise smooth \(f\).

Why real and orthogonal (Lagrange identity). For \(Lu=(pu')'+qu\) and \(u,v\) satisfying the separated boundary conditions,

\[\int_a^b(vLu-uLv)\,dx=\Big[p(vu'-uv')\Big]_a^b=0.\]

If \(L\phi_m=-\lambda_mw\phi_m\) and \(L\phi_n=-\lambda_nw\phi_n\), then \((\lambda_n-\lambda_m)\int w\phi_m\phi_n=0\). Taking \(\phi_n=\bar\phi_m\) gives \((\lambda-\bar\lambda)\int w\lvert\phi\rvert^2=0\), so \(\lambda\) is real.

Sign of eigenvalues (Rayleigh quotient). Multiplying by \(y\) and integrating,

\[\lambda=\frac{-\big[p\,yy'\big]_a^b+\int_a^b\left(p\,y'^2-q\,y^2\right)dx}{\int_a^bw\,y^2\,dx}.\]

With Dirichlet (or Neumann) boundary terms vanishing and \(q\le0\), all eigenvalues are \(\ge0\); for Dirichlet conditions with \(q\le0\) they are positive.

Periodic conditions (for example \(y(-L)=y(L)\), \(y'(-L)=y'(L)\)) are self-adjoint, so eigenvalues are real and eigenfunctions of different eigenvalues are orthogonal, but the problem is not regular (the conditions are not separated) and eigenvalues need not be simple: the periodic problem for \(y''+\lambda y=0\) has two eigenfunctions \(\cos\) and \(\sin\) for each \(\lambda=(n\pi/L)^2\), \(n\ge1\), while \(\lambda=0\) is simple with \(y=1\). On \([0,2\pi]\) with \(y(0)=y(2\pi)\), \(y'(0)=y'(2\pi)\) the eigenvalues are \(n^2\), \(n=0,1,2,\dots\), with eigenfunctions \(1\) and \(\cos nx\), \(\sin nx\).

Standard eigenvalue problems \(y''+\lambda y=0\) on \([0,L]\)

Method: split into \(\lambda<0\), \(\lambda=0\), \(\lambda>0\). For \(\lambda=\mu^2>0\), \(y=A\cos\mu x+B\sin\mu x\).

With \(L=\pi\) and \(y(0)=y(\pi)=0\): \(\lambda_n=n^2\), \(\phi_n=\sin nx\) (this is the 2026 question). For Dirichlet conditions \(\lambda=0\) gives \(y=Ax+B=0\) and \(\lambda<0\) gives \(\sinh\), \(\cosh\) combinations that cannot vanish at two points, so there are no eigenvalues \(\le0\). Orthogonality integrals: \(\displaystyle\int_0^L\sin\frac{m\pi x}{L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}dx=\frac L2\,\delta_{mn}\), and \(\int_0^L\cos^2\frac{n\pi x}{L}dx=\frac L2\) for \(n\ge1\), \(=L\) for \(n=0\).

Robin conditions give transcendental equations. For \(y(0)=0\), \(y(L)\cdot h+y'(L)=0\) with \(h>0\), the eigenvalues are \(\mu_n^2\) where \(\tan\mu L=-\mu/h\); there is no \(\lambda\le0\). Eigenvalues must then be found numerically. Pay attention to the sign: \(y(L)h+y'(L)=0\) with \(h>0\) gives \(\tan\mu L=-\mu/h\), whereas \(y'(L)-hy(L)=0\) gives \(\tan\mu L=\mu/h\) (and then a negative eigenvalue can appear when \(hL>1\)).

Sturm separation and comparison theorems

These concern zeros of solutions of \(y''+q(x)y=0\) with \(q\) continuous. (Any \(y''+py'+qy=0\) becomes this form via \(y=u\,e^{-\frac12\int p}\); the zeros of \(y\) and \(u\) coincide.)

Basic facts. Zeros of a non-trivial solution are simple (if \(y(x_0)=y'(x_0)=0\) then \(y\equiv0\)) and isolated, so there are finitely many in any closed bounded interval.

Sturm separation theorem. If \(y_1,y_2\) are linearly independent solutions of \(y''+p(x)y'+q(x)y=0\), their zeros interlace: between two consecutive zeros of one lies exactly one zero of the other. Example: \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\).

Sturm comparison theorem. Let \(y''+q_1(x)y=0\) and \(z''+q_2(x)z=0\) with \(q_2(x)>q_1(x)\) on an interval. If \(y\) is a non-trivial solution of the first with consecutive zeros \(a<b\), then every solution \(z\) of the second has at least one zero in the open interval \((a,b)\). (With only \(q_2\ge q_1\), \(z\) has a zero in \([a,b]\), inside \((a,b)\) unless \(q_1\equiv q_2\) on \([a,b]\) and \(z\) is a multiple of \(y\).)

Larger \(q\) means faster oscillation.

Consequences.

  • If \(q\ge m^2>0\) on an interval, consecutive zeros of any solution are at most \(\pi/m\) apart (compare with \(\sin m(x-x_0)\)).
  • If \(q\le M^2\) on an interval, consecutive zeros are at least \(\pi/M\) apart.
  • If \(q\le0\) on an interval, a non-trivial solution has at most one zero there.
  • Bessel zeros. For \(x^2y''+xy'+(x^2-\nu^2)y=0\), \(u=\sqrt x\,y\) satisfies \(u''+\left(1+\dfrac{1/4-\nu^2}{x^2}\right)u=0\). If \(\nu<\frac12\) then \(q>1\) and zeros of \(J_\nu\) are spaced less than \(\pi\) apart; if \(\nu>\frac12\), spacing is more than \(\pi\); if \(\nu=\frac12\), \(J_{1/2}\propto\sin x/\sqrt x\) and spacing is exactly \(\pi\).

Green’s function for a BVP

Consider \(Ly=(p\,y')'+q\,y=f(x)\) on \([a,b]\) with homogeneous separated boundary conditions, and assume the homogeneous problem has only the trivial solution. Let \(u_1\) solve \(Lu=0\) and satisfy the condition at \(a\); let \(u_2\) solve \(Lu=0\) and satisfy the condition at \(b\). Their Wronskian \(W=u_1u_2'-u_1'u_2\) satisfies \(pW=\text{constant}\). Then

\[y(x)=\int_a^bG(x,\xi)f(\xi)\,d\xi,\qquad G(x,\xi)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{u_1(x)u_2(\xi)}{p(\xi)W(\xi)},&x\le\xi\\\dfrac{u_1(\xi)u_2(x)}{p(\xi)W(\xi)},&x\ge\xi.\end{cases}\]

Defining properties of \(G(x,\xi)\) as a function of \(x\):

  1. \(LG=0\) for \(x\ne\xi\).
  2. \(G\) satisfies both boundary conditions.
  3. \(G\) is continuous at \(x=\xi\).
  4. \(\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial x}\) has a jump at \(x=\xi\): \(\ \partial_xG(\xi^+,\xi)-\partial_xG(\xi^-,\xi)=\dfrac{1}{p(\xi)}\).
  5. \(G\) is symmetric: \(G(x,\xi)=G(\xi,x)\) (for self-adjoint problems).

The eigenvalue problem \(Ly=-\lambda y\) becomes the Fredholm equation \(y=-\lambda\int G\,y\), and the eigenvalues of the SL problem are the reciprocals (up to sign) of the characteristic values of the symmetric kernel \(G\).

If the homogeneous problem has a non-trivial solution (as for \(y''+y=0\) on \([0,\pi]\) with Dirichlet conditions), \(u_1\) and \(u_2\) are dependent, \(W=0\), and no Green’s function exists.

Worked examples

Example 1: mixed boundary conditions

\(y''+\lambda y=0\), \(y(0)=0\), \(y'(L)=0\).

  • \(\lambda=0\): \(y=Ax+B\), \(y(0)=0\Rightarrow B=0\), \(y'(L)=A=0\). Only \(y=0\).
  • \(\lambda=-\mu^2<0\): \(y=A\sinh\mu x\) (from \(y(0)=0\)), \(y'(L)=A\mu\cosh\mu L=0\Rightarrow A=0\).
  • \(\lambda=\mu^2>0\): \(y=B\sin\mu x\), \(y'(L)=B\mu\cos\mu L=0\Rightarrow\cos\mu L=0\Rightarrow\mu L=\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{2}\).
\[\lambda_n=\left(\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{2L}\right)^2,\qquad\phi_n=\sin\frac{(2n-1)\pi x}{2L},\quad n=1,2,\dots\]

Example 2: reducing to SL form and finding the weight

(a) \(x^2y''+3xy'+\lambda y=0\) on \([1,2]\). \(a_0=x^2\), \(a_1=3x\): \(p=\exp\int\frac3x=x^3\). \(w=p\cdot\frac{1}{x^2}=x\). SL form: \((x^3y')'+\lambda\,x\,y=0\); orthogonality weight \(x\).

(b) Hermite \(y''-2xy'+\lambda y=0\): \(p=e^{-\int2x}=e^{-x^2}\), \(w=e^{-x^2}\), so \((e^{-x^2}y')'+\lambda e^{-x^2}y=0\).

Example 3: Euler-type eigenvalue problem

\(x^2y''+xy'+\lambda y=0\), \(y(1)=y(e^{\pi})=0\).

Put \(t=\ln x\in[0,\pi]\): \(y_{tt}+\lambda y=0\), \(y(0)=y(\pi)=0\). So \(\lambda_n=n^2\), \(\phi_n=\sin(n\ln x)\). SL form: \((xy')'+\frac{\lambda}{x}y=0\), weight \(1/x\):

\[\int_1^{e^\pi}\sin(m\ln x)\sin(n\ln x)\frac{dx}{x}=0\ (m\ne n).\]

If the interval were \([1,e]\) the eigenvalues would be \((n\pi)^2\).

Example 4: Robin condition

\(y''+\lambda y=0\), \(y(0)=0\), \(y(1)+y'(1)=0\).

  • \(\lambda=0\): \(y=Ax\), \(y(1)+y'(1)=2A=0\). No eigenvalue.
  • \(\lambda=-\mu^2\): \(y=\sinh\mu x\), \(y(1)+y'(1)=\sinh\mu+\mu\cosh\mu>0\). None.
  • \(\lambda=\mu^2>0\): \(y=\sin\mu x\) and \(\sin\mu+\mu\cos\mu=0\), i.e. \(\tan\mu=-\mu\). The first positive roots are \(\mu_1\approx2.0288\), \(\mu_2\approx4.9132\), so \(\lambda_1\approx4.116\), \(\lambda_2\approx24.14\).

Example 5: shifted equation

\(y''+2y'+(1+\lambda)y=0\), \(y(0)=y(\pi)=0\).

Put \(y=e^{-x}u\): \(y''+2y'+y=e^{-x}u''\), so \(u''+\lambda u=0\), \(u(0)=u(\pi)=0\). Hence \(\lambda_n=n^2\) and \(\phi_n=e^{-x}\sin nx\). SL form: \(p=e^{2x}\), \(w=e^{2x}\), i.e. \((e^{2x}y')'+(1+\lambda)e^{2x}y=0\). Orthogonality: \(\int_0^\pi e^{2x}\phi_m\phi_n\,dx=\int_0^\pi\sin mx\sin nx\,dx=0\).

Example 6: Green’s functions

(a) \(y''=f\), \(y(0)=y(1)=0\). \(p=1\), \(u_1=x\), \(u_2=x-1\), \(W=x\cdot1-1\cdot(x-1)=1\):

\[G(x,\xi)=\begin{cases}\xi(x-1),&\xi\le x\\x(\xi-1),&x\le\xi\end{cases}=-x_<(1-x_>).\]

Check: for \(f=1\), \(y=\int_0^1G\,d\xi=\frac{x(x-1)}{2}\), which satisfies \(y''=1\) and the boundary conditions. The derivative of \(G\) jumps from \(\xi-1\) to \(\xi\), a jump of \(1=1/p\). Also \(G\left(\tfrac12,\tfrac14\right)=-\tfrac18\).

(b) \(y''+y=f\) on \([0,\pi/2]\), \(y(0)=y(\pi/2)=0\). \(u_1=\sin x\), \(u_2=\cos x\), \(W=-1\): \(G(x,\xi)=-\sin x_<\cos x_>\). For \(f=1\): \(y=1-\cos x-\sin x\).

Common traps

  • Eigenvalue index and range. Dirichlet: \(n\ge1\) (\(\lambda=0\) gives only \(y=0\)). Neumann: \(\lambda=0\) is an eigenvalue.
  • Mixed boundary conditions give odd multiples. \(\left(\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{2L}\right)^2\), not \(\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2\).
  • Interval length. \([0,L]\) gives \((n\pi/L)^2\); \([0,\pi]\) gives \(n^2\); \([0,2]\) gives \(n^2\pi^2/4\). Euler equation with \(x\in[1,e]\) gives \((n\pi)^2\), not \(n^2\).
  • Weight function. Orthogonality is \(\int w\phi_m\phi_n\), not \(\int\phi_m\phi_n\), unless \(w=1\). Get \(w\) from the conversion formula \(w=p\,a_3/a_0\).
  • Simplicity fails for periodic conditions. Each \(\lambda=(n\pi/L)^2\) has two eigenfunctions.
  • Orthogonality needs self-adjoint conditions. For arbitrary mixed boundary conditions the Lagrange boundary term need not vanish.
  • Reality, not positivity. Self-adjointness gives real eigenvalues; positivity needs the Rayleigh-quotient argument and a sign condition on \(q\).
  • Oscillation count. \(\phi_n\) has \(n-1\) interior zeros, not \(n\).
  • Sturm separation versus comparison. Separation is for two solutions of the same equation (exactly one zero between consecutive zeros); comparison is for different \(q\) (at least one zero).
  • Green’s function jump. \(G\) is continuous but \(G_x\) is not; its jump is \(1/p(\xi)\) (the sign convention follows from \((py')'=f\)).
  • No Green’s function when the homogeneous BVP has a non-trivial solution; there the Fredholm alternative applies.
  • Eigenfunction normalisation. \(\int_0^\pi\sin^2nx\,dx=\pi/2\), so the orthonormal functions are \(\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\sin nx\).

2026 Q57

Question (restated). The eigenvalues of the boundary value problem \(y''+\lambda y=0\), \(y(0)=y(\pi)=0\) are given by: (A) \(n\) (B) \(\pi n\) (C) \(n^2\) (D) \(2n\) (with \(n=1,2,3,\dots\)).

Answer: C (\(\lambda_n=n^2\)). No official key has been published for 2026; this follows from the derivation below.

For \(\lambda\le0\) only \(y=0\) satisfies both conditions. For \(\lambda=\mu^2>0\), \(y=B\sin\mu x\) and \(y(\pi)=0\) requires \(\sin\mu\pi=0\), so \(\mu=n\). Hence \(\lambda_n=n^2\) with eigenfunctions \(\sin nx\). See the table above with \(L=\pi\).

Quick revision

  • BVPs can have no, one or infinitely many solutions; \(y''+y=1\), \(y(0)=y(\pi)=0\) has none.
  • SL equation \((py')'+qy+\lambda wy=0\), \(p>0\), \(w>0\) regular on \([a,b]\).
  • Conversion: \(p=e^{\int a_1/a_0}\), \(w=p\,a_3/a_0\).
  • Eigenvalues real, simple, increasing to \(\infty\); eigenfunctions orthogonal with weight \(w\); \(\phi_n\) has \(n-1\) interior zeros.
  • Dirichlet on \([0,L]\): \((n\pi/L)^2\), \(\sin\), \(n\ge1\). Neumann: same values, \(\cos\), \(n\ge0\).
  • Mixed conditions: \(\left((2n-1)\pi/2L\right)^2\).
  • \(y''+\lambda y=0\), \(y(0)=y(\pi)=0\): \(\lambda=n^2\).
  • Robin gives \(\tan\mu L=-\mu/h\)-type equations (no closed form).
  • Periodic conditions: eigenvalues not simple (two eigenfunctions each); the problem is not regular.
  • Self-adjoint iff \(a_1=a_0'\). Legendre eigenvalues \(n(n+1)\), Hermite \(2n\), Chebyshev \(n^2\), Laguerre \(n\). Bessel eigenfunctions \(J_\nu(\mu_nx)\) have weight \(x\).
  • Sturm separation: independent solutions interlace. Comparison: larger \(q\) oscillates faster.
  • \(q\ge m^2\) implies zeros at most \(\pi/m\) apart; \(q\le0\) implies at most one zero.
  • Green’s function: \(G=u_1(x_<)u_2(x_>)/(pW)\); continuous, symmetric, \(G_x\) jumps by \(1/p\).
