Why it matters for the exam

Direct questions on the Riemann integral are rare, but its ideas appear everywhere: which functions are Riemann integrable (the 2019 paper asked about the indicator of the rationals), convergence tests for series and integrals (2025 asked two such questions), and Beta and Gamma evaluations that are standard in Unit 3 and Unit 7. The 2025 questions were ordering-of-implications and assertion-reason items on series of constants, so this note includes the series tests too.

Core ideas

Riemann (Darboux) integral

Let \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) be bounded and let \(P=\{a=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_n=b\}\) be a partition. With \(m_i=\inf_{[x_{i-1},x_i]}f\) and \(M_i=\sup_{[x_{i-1},x_i]}f\), \(L(P,f)=\sum m_i\Delta x_i,\qquad U(P,f)=\sum M_i\Delta x_i.\)

  • Refining a partition raises \(L\) and lowers \(U\). Every lower sum is at most every upper sum.
  • Lower integral \(\underline{\int}f=\sup_P L(P,f)\), upper integral \(\overline{\int}f=\inf_P U(P,f)\), and \(\underline\int\le\overline\int\).
  • \(f\) is Riemann integrable iff \(\underline\int f=\overline\int f\); this common value is \(\int_a^bf\).
  • Riemann’s criterion. \(f\) is integrable iff for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is a partition \(P\) with \(U(P,f)-L(P,f)<\varepsilon\).
  • Riemann sums. \(f\) is integrable with integral \(I\) iff \(\sum f(t_i)\Delta x_i\to I\) as the mesh \(\to0\), for every choice of tags \(t_i\in[x_{i-1},x_i]\).

Which functions are integrable

Class Integrable?
Continuous on \([a,b]\) Yes
Monotone on \([a,b]\) Yes (even with infinitely many jumps)
Bounded, finitely many discontinuities Yes
Bounded, countably many discontinuities Yes (Thomae’s function: \(\frac1q\) at \(\frac pq\) in lowest terms, \(0\) at irrationals, integral \(0\))
Bounded variation Yes
Dirichlet function (\(1\) on \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(0\) on irrationals) No: every \(L=0\), every \(U=1\)
Unbounded No (not Riemann integrable by definition)

Lebesgue’s criterion. A bounded \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) is Riemann integrable iff the set of its discontinuities has Lebesgue measure zero. This explains the table: countable sets have measure zero; the Dirichlet function is discontinuous everywhere, a set of measure \(b-a>0\). (The Dirichlet function is Lebesgue integrable with integral \(0\), because \(\mathbb{Q}\) has measure \(0\).)

Closure properties: sums, scalar multiples, products, \(\lvert f\rvert\) and \(f^2\) of integrable functions are integrable; \(g\circ f\) is integrable if \(f\) is integrable and \(g\) is continuous. The converse of \(\lvert f\rvert\) fails: \(f=1\) on \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(-1\) elsewhere has \(\lvert f\rvert=1\) integrable but \(f\) not. Likewise \(f^2\) integrable does not give \(f\) integrable.

Properties

  • Linearity, additivity \(\int_a^b=\int_a^c+\int_c^b\), monotonicity, \(\bigl\lvert\int f\bigr\rvert\le\int\lvert f\rvert\).
  • First mean value theorem. If \(f\) is continuous and \(g\ge0\) integrable, then \(\int_a^bfg=f(\xi)\int_a^bg\) for some \(\xi\in[a,b]\). With \(g=1\): \(\int_a^bf=f(\xi)(b-a)\).
  • Bonnet’s (second) mean value theorem. If \(g\) is monotone decreasing and \(\ge0\), then \(\int_a^bfg=g(a)\int_a^\xi f\) for some \(\xi\in[a,b]\).
  • \(F(x)=\int_a^xf\) is continuous (indeed Lipschitz) for every integrable \(f\).

Fundamental theorem of calculus

  1. FTC I. If \(f\) is integrable and continuous at \(x_0\), then \(F(x)=\int_a^xf\) is differentiable at \(x_0\) with \(F'(x_0)=f(x_0)\). At a jump of \(f\), \(F\) is continuous but has a corner. Example: \(f=\mathbf 1_{[1/2,1]}\) gives \(F(x)=\max(0,x-\frac12)\), not differentiable at \(\frac12\).
  2. FTC II. If \(f\) is integrable and \(G'=f\) on \([a,b]\), then \(\int_a^bf=G(b)-G(a)\). Integrability of \(f\) is a hypothesis, not a consequence: a function that is differentiable everywhere may have a derivative that is not Riemann integrable (for example \(x^2\sin(1/x^2)\) has an unbounded derivative).

Evaluating limits by Riemann sums

If \(f\) is continuous on \([0,1]\), \(\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^nf\!\left(\frac kn\right)=\int_0^1f.\) Rewrite the sum as \(\frac1n\sum f(k/n)\). Products turn into sums by taking logs. Examples: \(\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{n}{n^2+k^2}=\frac1n\sum\frac{1}{1+(k/n)^2}\to\int_0^1\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\frac\pi4\); and \(\bigl(\frac{n!}{n^n}\bigr)^{1/n}\to e^{\int_0^1\ln x\,dx}=\frac1e\).

Improper integrals

Type I (infinite interval). \(\int_a^\infty f=\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_a^Rf\) if the limit exists (finite). \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty f\) converges iff both \(\int_{-\infty}^c f\) and \(\int_c^\infty f\) converge separately.

Type II (unbounded integrand at an end point \(c\)). If \(f\) is unbounded near \(b\), \(\int_a^bf=\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}\int_a^{b-\varepsilon}f\). If the singularity is inside, split at it and require both pieces to converge.

Standard comparison integrals.

Integral Converges iff
\(\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,dx\) \(p>1\)
\(\int_0^1x^{-p}\,dx\) \(p<1\)
\(\int_0^\infty x^{-p}\,dx\) never
\(\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{x(\ln x)^p}\) \(p>1\)
\(\int_0^\infty e^{-ax}dx\) \(a>0\)

Tests (for \(f,g\ge0\)).

  • Comparison. \(0\le f\le g\): \(\int g\) converges \(\Rightarrow\int f\) converges; \(\int f\) diverges \(\Rightarrow\int g\) diverges.
  • Limit comparison. \(\lim f/g=\ell\in(0,\infty)\): both converge or both diverge. At infinity compare with \(x^{-p}\); at a singularity \(x=0\) compare with \(x^{-p}\) too.
  • \(\mu\)-test. If \(\lim_{x\to\infty}x^\mu f(x)=\ell\) finite, then \(\int^\infty f\) converges if \(\mu>1\), and if \(\ell\ne0\) and \(\mu\le1\) it diverges. Near a finite singularity \(b\): \(\lim(b-x)^\mu f(x)\) finite with \(\mu<1\) gives convergence.
  • Absolute convergence \(\Rightarrow\) convergence. The converse fails.
  • Dirichlet test. If \(F(x)=\int_a^xf\) is bounded and \(g\) is monotone with \(g\to0\) as \(x\to\infty\), then \(\int_a^\infty fg\) converges.
  • Abel test. If \(\int_a^\infty f\) converges and \(g\) is monotone and bounded, then \(\int_a^\infty fg\) converges.

Key examples.

  • \(\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}dx=\frac\pi2\) converges conditionally (Dirichlet test with \(f=\sin x\), \(g=1/x\)) but \(\int_1^\infty\frac{\lvert\sin x\rvert}{x}dx\) diverges (it grows like \(\frac2\pi\ln R\)).
  • \(\int_0^\infty\sin(x^2)\,dx\) converges (substitute \(t=x^2\) to get \(\frac12\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin t}{\sqrt t}dt\) and use the Dirichlet test), even though the integrand does not tend to \(0\).
  • \(\int_0^1\ln x\,dx=-1\), and \(\int_0^1x^{-1/2}\ln x\,dx=-4\).
  • Convergence of \(\int_a^\infty f\) does not imply \(f\to0\) (a thin tall spike train), but if \(f\) is uniformly continuous or monotone, it does.

Cauchy principal value. \(\mathrm{PV}\int_{-1}^1\frac{dx}{x}=0\) and \(\mathrm{PV}\int_{-\infty}^\infty x\,dx=0\) although both improper integrals diverge. The PV may exist when the integral does not.

Gamma and Beta functions

\(\Gamma(n)=\int_0^\infty x^{n-1}e^{-x}dx\ (n>0),\qquad B(m,n)=\int_0^1x^{m-1}(1-x)^{n-1}dx\ (m,n>0).\)

Both converge exactly for the stated parameter ranges (Gamma: Type II at \(0\) needs \(n>0\); Type I at infinity is always fine because of \(e^{-x}\); Beta: Type II at both ends needs \(m>0\) and \(n>0\)).

Properties:

  • \(\Gamma(n+1)=n\,\Gamma(n)\), \(\Gamma(1)=1\), so \(\Gamma(n+1)=n!\) for integers; \(\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi\), \(\Gamma(\tfrac32)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\), \(\Gamma(\tfrac52)=\frac{3\sqrt\pi}{4}\).
  • \(\Gamma\) has no value at \(0,-1,-2,\dots\) (poles). For negative non-integers use \(\Gamma(n)=\Gamma(n+1)/n\).
  • Reflection. \(\Gamma(n)\Gamma(1-n)=\dfrac{\pi}{\sin n\pi}\), \(0<n<1\).
  • Duplication (Legendre). \(\Gamma(n)\,\Gamma(n+\tfrac12)=2^{1-2n}\sqrt\pi\,\Gamma(2n)\).
  • \(B(m,n)=B(n,m)=\dfrac{\Gamma(m)\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(m+n)}\).
  • \(B(m,n)=2\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{2m-1}\theta\cos^{2n-1}\theta\,d\theta=\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{m-1}}{(1+x)^{m+n}}dx\).
  • Consequence: \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^p\theta\cos^q\theta\,d\theta=\frac12B\!\left(\frac{p+1}2,\frac{q+1}2\right)\).
  • Gaussian: \(\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}dx=\frac{\sqrt\pi}2\); and \(\int_0^\infty x^ae^{-x^b}dx=\frac1b\Gamma\!\left(\frac{a+1}b\right)\).

Series of constants (convergence tests)

Let \(s_N=\sum_{n\le N}a_n\). The series converges iff \((s_N)\) converges.

Chain of implications. \((s_N)\) convergent \(\iff\sum a_n\) convergent \(\Rightarrow a_n\to0\Rightarrow(a_n)\) convergent \(\Rightarrow(a_n)\) Cauchy (in \(\mathbb{R}\)). None of the one-way arrows reverses: \(a_n=\frac1n\to0\) but \(\sum\frac1n\) diverges.

Tests for \(a_n\ge0\):

Test Statement
\(n\)th term (divergence) \(a_n\not\to0\Rightarrow\sum a_n\) diverges. Silent if \(a_n\to0\)
Comparison, limit comparison as for integrals
\(p\)-series \(\sum n^{-p}\) converges iff \(p>1\); \(\sum\frac{1}{n(\ln n)^p}\) converges iff \(p>1\)
Cauchy condensation \(a_n\downarrow\): \(\sum a_n\) and \(\sum2^ka_{2^k}\) behave alike
Ratio (D’Alembert) \(\lim\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\rho\): converges if \(\rho<1\), diverges if \(\rho>1\), silent if \(\rho=1\)
Root (Cauchy) \(\limsup a_n^{1/n}=\rho\): converges if \(<1\), diverges if \(>1\)
Raabe \(\lim n\bigl(\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}-1\bigr)=\ell\): converges if \(\ell>1\), diverges if \(\ell<1\), silent if \(\ell=1\)
Integral \(f\ge0\) decreasing: \(\sum f(n)\) and \(\int^\infty f\) behave alike

The root test is stronger than the ratio test: whenever the ratio test decides, the root test decides the same way.

General series: Leibniz (\(a_n\downarrow0\Rightarrow\sum(-1)^na_n\) converges), Dirichlet (bounded partial sums of \(b_n\), monotone \(a_n\to0\)), Abel (\(\sum b_n\) convergent, \(a_n\) monotone bounded). Absolutely convergent series can be rearranged freely and multiplied (Cauchy product); a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to any sum (Riemann), and Mertens’ theorem allows the Cauchy product if one factor converges absolutely.

Formulas and results

  • \(L(P,f)\le U(P,f)\); integrable iff \(\forall\varepsilon\ \exists P:\ U-L<\varepsilon\).
  • Bounded \(f\) is Riemann integrable iff its discontinuity set has measure zero.
  • \(\lim\frac1n\sum f(k/n)=\int_0^1f\); \(\lim\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{n}{n^2+k^2}=\frac\pi4\); \(\lim\frac{(n!)^{1/n}}{n}=\frac1e\).
  • \(\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\) converges iff \(p>1\); \(\int_0^1x^{-p}\) iff \(p<1\); \(\int_0^\infty x^{-p}\) never.
  • \(\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}x=\frac\pi2\) (conditional); \(\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\frac\pi2\); \(\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{p-1}}{1+x}dx=\frac\pi{\sin p\pi}\), \(0<p<1\).
  • \(\Gamma(n+1)=n\Gamma(n)\); \(\Gamma(\frac12)=\sqrt\pi\); \(\Gamma(n)\Gamma(1-n)=\pi/\sin n\pi\).
  • \(B(m,n)=\frac{\Gamma(m)\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(m+n)}\); \(\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^p\cos^q=\frac12B(\frac{p+1}2,\frac{q+1}2)\).
  • \(\int_0^\infty x^ae^{-x^b}dx=\frac1b\Gamma(\frac{a+1}b)\).
  • Series: ratio, root, Raabe; Leibniz; \(p\)-series; \(\sum\frac1{n(\ln n)^p}\) converges iff \(p>1\).

Worked examples

Example 1: limit as a Riemann sum

Find \(\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{n}{n^2+k^2}\).

Write the term as \(\frac1n\cdot\frac{1}{1+(k/n)^2}\). This is a Riemann sum of \(f(x)=\frac{1}{1+x^2}\) on \([0,1]\). The limit is \(\int_0^1\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\arctan1=\frac\pi4\).

Example 2: a Type II improper integral

Evaluate \(\int_0^1\frac{\ln x}{\sqrt x}dx\).

Put \(x=t^2\), \(dx=2t\,dt\): the integral is \(\int_0^1\frac{2\ln t}{t}\,2t\,dt=4\int_0^1\ln t\,dt\). Since \(\int_0^1\ln t\,dt=\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}[t\ln t-t]_\varepsilon^1=-1\), the answer is \(-4\). It converges because \(\sqrt x\ln x\to0\) at \(0\) (compare \(x^{-1/2+\delta}\)).

Example 3: Beta function

Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^6\theta\cos^4\theta\,d\theta\).

With \(p=6\), \(q=4\): \(\frac12B\left(\frac72,\frac52\right)=\frac12\frac{\Gamma(7/2)\Gamma(5/2)}{\Gamma(6)}\). Now \(\Gamma(\frac72)=\frac{15\sqrt\pi}8\), \(\Gamma(\frac52)=\frac{3\sqrt\pi}4\), \(\Gamma(6)=120\). So the value is \(\frac12\cdot\frac{45\pi/32}{120}=\frac{3\pi}{512}\).

Example 4: Gamma form

Evaluate \(\int_0^\infty x^4e^{-x^2}dx\).

With \(a=4\), \(b=2\): \(\frac12\Gamma\left(\frac52\right)=\frac12\cdot\frac{3\sqrt\pi}{4}=\frac{3\sqrt\pi}{8}\).

Example 5: conditional convergence

Does \(\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}dx\) converge absolutely?

At \(0\) the integrand tends to \(1\), so there is no singularity. At infinity, \(F(x)=\int_0^x\sin t\,dt=1-\cos x\) is bounded and \(\frac1x\downarrow0\), so the Dirichlet test gives convergence. For absolute convergence use \(\lvert\sin x\rvert\ge\sin^2x=\frac{1-\cos2x}{2}\): then \(\int_1^R\frac{\lvert\sin x\rvert}{x}\ge\frac12\ln R-\frac12\int_1^R\frac{\cos2x}{x}dx\to\infty\). So it converges conditionally but not absolutely.

Example 6: Raabe’s test

Test \(\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\binom{2n}{n}}{4^n}\). The terms are \(c_n=\frac{1\cdot3\cdots(2n-1)}{2\cdot4\cdots2n}\). Then \(\frac{c_n}{c_{n+1}}=\frac{2n+2}{2n+1}\), so the ratio test gives \(\rho=1\) and is silent. Raabe: \(n\left(\frac{2n+2}{2n+1}-1\right)=\frac{n}{2n+1}\to\frac12<1\). The series diverges. (Indeed \(c_n\sim\frac1{\sqrt{\pi n}}\).)

Common traps

  • \(\int_0^\infty x^{-p}dx\) never converges: it needs \(p<1\) at \(0\) and \(p>1\) at infinity. Questions offering “\(p>1\)” or “\(p<1\)” as the answer are traps.
  • Ratio test with limit \(1\) decides nothing; use Raabe, comparison, or the integral test.
  • \(a_n\to0\) is necessary, not sufficient. The \(n\)th-term test only proves divergence.
  • \(\lvert f\rvert\) integrable does not give \(f\) integrable. The converse (integrable \(\Rightarrow\) \(\lvert f\rvert\) integrable) is true.
  • Integrable needs boundedness. An unbounded \(f\) is not Riemann integrable; the improper integral is a different notion.
  • FTC II needs \(f\) integrable. A derivative that exists everywhere need not be integrable.
  • Principal value is not the integral. \(\int_{-1}^1\frac{dx}x\) diverges although the PV is \(0\).
  • Beta/Gamma ranges. \(B(m,n)\) needs \(m,n>0\), and \(\Gamma(0)\) is undefined. Check the exponents \(2m-1\) and \(2n-1\) when converting a trigonometric integral.
  • Dirichlet function. Not Riemann integrable, but Lebesgue integrable with integral \(0\) (do not confuse the two).
  • Comparison needs \(f\ge0\). For signed integrands use absolute convergence or Dirichlet/Abel.

Previous-year questions

2025, Q42

Arrange the following in a sequence so that each statement implies the next: I. \(\{a_n\}\) is a Cauchy sequence; II. \(\{a_1+\dots+a_n\}\) is convergent; III. \(\sum a_n\) is convergent; IV. \(\lim a_n=0\). Options: (A) I, II, IV, III; (B) I, IV, III, II; (C) II, III, IV, I; (D) I, III, II, IV. Answer: C (official key).

Solution: II and III say the same thing. A convergent series has \(a_n\to0\) (IV). A sequence that tends to \(0\) converges, hence is Cauchy (I). The reverse implications fail (harmonic series).

2025, Q44

Assertion (A): the series \(\sum u_n\) with \(u_n=\frac{3^n}{2^n+3^n}\) is divergent. Reason (R): \(\lim u_n\ne0\). Answer: A (both true, R explains A; official key).

Solution: \(u_n=\frac{1}{1+(2/3)^n}\to1\ne0\), so the \(n\)th-term test shows divergence. R is exactly the reason.

Quick revision

  • Riemann integrable iff \(\forall\varepsilon\ \exists P\) with \(U-L<\varepsilon\); bounded is part of the definition.
  • Bounded \(f\) is Riemann integrable iff its discontinuity set has measure zero (Lebesgue).
  • Continuous, monotone, countably-discontinuous bounded, BV: all integrable. Dirichlet function: not.
  • \(\lvert f\rvert\) integrable does not imply \(f\) integrable; \(f\) integrable implies \(\lvert f\rvert\) and \(f^2\) integrable.
  • FTC I: derivative of \(\int_a^xf\) is \(f\) at points of continuity; FTC II needs \(G'=f\) with \(f\) integrable.
  • \(\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\): \(p>1\); \(\int_0^1x^{-p}\): \(p<1\); \(\int_0^\infty x^{-p}\): never.
  • \(\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}x=\frac\pi2\), conditional only.
  • \(B(m,n)=\frac{\Gamma(m)\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(m+n)}\); \(\Gamma(\frac12)=\sqrt\pi\); \(\Gamma(n)\Gamma(1-n)=\frac\pi{\sin n\pi}\).
  • \(\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^p\cos^q=\frac12B(\frac{p+1}2,\frac{q+1}2)\).
  • Ratio test silent at \(1\): use Raabe. \(\sum\frac1{n(\ln n)^p}\) converges iff \(p>1\).
  • Series chain: partial sums converge \(\iff\) series converges \(\Rightarrow a_n\to0\Rightarrow\) Cauchy.
