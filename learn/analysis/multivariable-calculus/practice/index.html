  1. Question 1Easy
    \(\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\)

    Why

    Along \(y=mx\) the value is \(\frac{m}{1+m^2}\), which depends on \(m\) (it is \(\frac12\) along \(y=x\) and \(-\frac12\) along \(y=-x\)). So the limit does not exist.
  2. Question 2Easy
    The tangent plane to the surface \(z=xy\) at the point \((2,3,6)\) is

    Why

    For \(z=f(x,y)\) the plane is \(z-f(a,b)=f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b)\). Here \(f_x=y=3\) and \(f_y=x=2\), so \(z-6=3(x-2)+2(y-3)\), that is \(3x+2y-z=6\). The first wrong option swaps the two partial derivatives.
  3. Question 3Medium
    For \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2y}{x^4+y^2}\), \(\;f(0,0)=0\), along which path does \(f\) approach a non-zero limit at the origin?

    Why

    Along \(y=x^2\): \(\frac{x^4}{x^4+x^4}=\frac12\). Along every line \(y=mx\) the value is \(\frac{mx}{x^2+m^2}\to0\) and along \(x=0\) it is \(0\). Hence the limit at the origin does not exist.
  4. Question 4Easy
    Which implication is FALSE for \(f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}\) at a point?

    Why

    Existence of the two partial derivatives is much weaker than differentiability: \(\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\) (\(f(0,0)=0\)) has both partials at \(0\) but is not even continuous there.
  5. Question 5Medium
    Let \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^3}{x^2+y^2}\), \(f(0,0)=0\), and \(u=\left(\frac1{\sqrt2},\frac1{\sqrt2}\right)\). The directional derivative \(D_uf(0,0)\) equals

    Why

    By the definition, \(\frac{f(tu)-f(0,0)}t=\frac{t^3\cos^3\theta/t^2}{t}=\cos^3\theta\), which for \(\theta=\frac\pi4\) equals \(\frac1{2\sqrt2}\). The value \(\nabla f(0,0)\cdot u=(1,0)\cdot u=\frac1{\sqrt2}\) is different, because \(f\) is not differentiable at the origin and the gradient formula does not apply.
  6. Question 6Medium
    For \(u=x^2-y^2\) and \(v=2xy\), the Jacobian \(\dfrac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\) equals

    Why

    \(\det\begin{pmatrix}2x&-2y\\2y&2x\end{pmatrix}=4x^2+4y^2\). This is the Jacobian of \(z\mapsto z^2\) (here \(u+iv=(x+iy)^2\)), which equals \(\lvert2z\rvert^2\) and vanishes only at the origin.
  7. Question 7Medium
    If \(y=y(x)\) is defined near \((1,1)\) by \(xy+\ln y=1\), then \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) at \(x=1\) equals

    Why

    Put \(F=xy+\ln y-1\). Then \(F_x=y=1\) and \(F_y=x+\frac1y=2\) at \((1,1)\), and \(\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}=-\frac12\).
  8. Question 8Hard
    The origin is a critical point of \(f(x,y)=x^3-3xy^2\). It is

    Why

    \(f_x=3x^2-3y^2\) and \(f_y=-6xy\) vanish only at the origin, and there \(f_{xx}=f_{xy}=f_{yy}=0\), so \(D=0\) and the test is silent. Direct check: \(f(t,0)=t^3\) takes both signs near \(0\), so the origin is neither a minimum nor a maximum, hence a saddle (a monkey saddle).
  9. Question 9Easy
    The origin is a critical point of \(f(x,y)=x^2+4xy+y^2\). It is

    Why

    \(r=2\), \(s=4\), \(t=2\), so \(rt-s^2=4-16=-12<0\): a saddle point (along \(y=x\) the function is \(6x^2>0\), along \(y=-x\) it is \(-2x^2<0\)).
  10. Question 10Medium
    The maximum value of \(x+2y\) subject to \(x^2+y^2=5\) is

    Why

    \(\nabla f=(1,2)=\lambda(2x,2y)\) gives \(y=2x\); then \(5x^2=5\), \(x=1\), \(y=2\) and \(f=5\). (Also by Cauchy-Schwarz: \(x+2y\le\sqrt5\sqrt5\).)
  11. Question 11Medium
    The directional derivative of \(f(x,y)=x^2+xy\) at \((1,2)\) in the direction of \((3,4)\) is

    Why

    \(\nabla f=(2x+y,x)=(4,1)\) at \((1,2)\). The unit vector in the direction \((3,4)\) is \((\frac35,\frac45)\), so \(D_uf=\frac{12+4}5=\frac{16}5\). Using the vector \((3,4)\) without normalising would give \(16\), which is wrong.
  12. Question 12Medium
    The second-degree terms in the Taylor expansion of \(e^x\sin y\) about \((0,0)\) are

    Why

    \(e^x\sin y=\left(1+x+\frac{x^2}2+\dots\right)\left(y-\frac{y^3}6+\dots\right)=y+xy+\frac{x^2y}2-\frac{y^3}6+\dots\); the only degree-2 term is \(xy\). (Via derivatives: \(f_{xx}=0\), \(f_{xy}=1\), \(f_{yy}=0\) at the origin.)
  13. Question 13Medium
    For \(f(x,y)=x^3+3x^2y+y^3\) the value of \(xf_x+yf_y\) at \((1,1)\) is

    Why

    \(f\) is homogeneous of degree \(3\), so by Euler’s theorem \(xf_x+yf_y=3f\). Since \(f(1,1)=1+3+1=5\), the value is \(15\). The factor \(n=3\) must not be forgotten.
  14. Question 14Hard
    Assertion (A): \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\), \(f(0,0)=0\), has partial derivatives at the origin. Reason (R): if \(f_x\) and \(f_y\) exist at a point, then \(f\) is continuous there.

    Why

    \(f(h,0)=0=f(0,k)\) so \(f_x(0,0)=f_y(0,0)=0\): A is true. R is false: existence of partials does not give continuity, and this very \(f\) is discontinuous at \(0\) (limit \(\frac12\) along \(y=x\)).
  15. Question 15Hard
    Let \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{xy(x^2-y^2)}{x^2+y^2}\) for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\), \(f(0,0)=0\). With \(f_{xy}=\partial_y(\partial_xf)\) and \(f_{yx}=\partial_x(\partial_yf)\), the value of \(f_{xy}(0,0)-f_{yx}(0,0)\) is

    Why

    \(f_x(0,y)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,y)}h=\lim\frac{y(h^2-y^2)}{h^2+y^2}=-y\), so \(f_{xy}(0,0)=-1\). Similarly \(f_y(x,0)=x\) and \(f_{yx}(0,0)=1\). The difference is \(-2\). Schwarz’s theorem does not apply because the mixed partials are not continuous at \(0\).
