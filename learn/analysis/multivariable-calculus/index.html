Why it matters for the exam

Three of the nine Unit 3 questions in 2025 came from here: continuity and differentiability of \(\frac{xy}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\) at the origin, the maximum of \(\sin x\sin y\sin(x+y)\), and the maximum distance between two curves. The recurring skills are the path test for limits, the hierarchy “partials exist / differentiable / \(C^1\)”, and finding and classifying critical points. Questions are short, and the options usually differ by one word (“continuous”, “differentiable”, “partial derivatives exist”).

Core ideas

Limits and continuity in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)

\(\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f(x,y)=L\) means: for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is \(\delta>0\) with \(\lvert f(x,y)-L\rvert<\varepsilon\) whenever \(0<\sqrt{(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2}<\delta\). The value must be the same along every approach.

Disproving a limit (path test). If two paths give different limits, or one path gives no limit, the limit does not exist. Paths to try: \(y=mx\), \(x=0\), \(y=0\), \(y=x^2\), \(x=y^2\).

Proving a limit. Use the squeeze theorem or polar coordinates: put \(x=r\cos\theta,\ y=r\sin\theta\) and show \(\lvert f-L\rvert\le g(r)\to0\) with \(g\) independent of \(\theta\). Agreeing along all straight lines proves nothing.

Function at \((0,0)\) Limit
\(\dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\) Does not exist (\(\frac m{1+m^2}\) along \(y=mx\))
\(\dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}\) \(0\) (\(\lvert f\rvert\le\frac r2\))
\(\dfrac{xy}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\) \(0\) (\(\lvert f\rvert\le\frac r2\))
\(\dfrac{x^2y}{x^4+y^2}\) Does not exist: \(0\) along every line, \(\frac12\) along \(y=x^2\)
\(\dfrac{xy^2}{x^2+y^4}\) Does not exist: \(0\) along lines, \(\frac12\) along \(x=y^2\)
\(\dfrac{x^2y^2}{x^2+y^2}\) \(0\)

A function is continuous at a point if the limit exists and equals the value. Iterated limits \(\lim_x\lim_y f\) and \(\lim_y\lim_x f\) can both exist and differ, or exist while the double limit does not; the double limit existing does not guarantee the iterated limits exist.

Partial and directional derivatives

\(f_x(a,b)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h,b)-f(a,b)}h\), and similarly \(f_y\). The directional derivative in the direction of a unit vector \(u\) is

\[D_uf(a)=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{f(a+tu)-f(a)}t.\]

It always equals \(\nabla f(a)\cdot u\) when \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\), and then the maximum rate of increase is \(\lVert\nabla f\rVert\) in the direction of \(\nabla f\).

Partial derivatives at a point are limits along the two axes only. So all partial derivatives (even all directional derivatives) can exist at a point where \(f\) is not continuous. Example: \(f(x,y)=\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\), \(f(0,0)=0\), has \(f_x(0,0)=f_y(0,0)=0\) but is discontinuous at \(0\). Another: \(f=\frac{x^2y}{x^4+y^2}\), \(f(0,0)=0\), has a directional derivative at \(0\) in every direction (equal to \(\frac{\cos^2\theta}{\sin\theta}\) in the direction \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) if \(\sin\theta\ne0\), and \(0\) otherwise), yet it is discontinuous at \(0\) because of the path \(y=x^2\).

Worked case. For \(f(x,y)=x^2+3xy\) at \((1,2)\): \(\nabla f=(2x+3y,\,3x)=(8,3)\). In the direction \(v=(3,4)\), \(u=\frac v5\), so \(D_uf=\frac{24+12}5=\frac{36}5\). The largest rate is \(\lVert\nabla f\rVert=\sqrt{73}\) (along \((8,3)\)), the smallest is \(-\sqrt{73}\), and the rate is \(0\) along \(\pm(3,-8)\), the tangent direction to the level curve.

Tangent plane and normal. For \(z=f(x,y)\) differentiable at \((a,b)\): \(z-f(a,b)=f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b)\), with normal direction \((f_x,f_y,-1)\); here \(z-7=8(x-1)+3(y-2)\). For a level surface \(F(x,y,z)=c\) the normal is \(\nabla F\): for \(x^2+y^2+z^2=14\) at \((1,2,3)\) the tangent plane is \(x+2y+3z=14\).

The formula \(D_uf=\nabla f\cdot u\) needs differentiability. For \(f=\frac{x^3}{x^2+y^2}\), \(f(0,0)=0\): \(f_x(0,0)=1\), \(f_y(0,0)=0\), yet along \(u=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) the derivative is \(\frac{f(tu)}t=\cos^3\theta\ne\cos\theta=\nabla f\cdot u\). So \(f\) is continuous at \(0\), all directional derivatives exist, and \(f\) is not differentiable there (\(D_uf\) is not linear in \(u\)).

Differentiability (total derivative)

\(f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}\) is differentiable at \(a=(a_1,a_2)\) if there are numbers \(A,B\) with

\[\lim_{(h,k)\to(0,0)}\frac{f(a_1+h,a_2+k)-f(a_1,a_2)-Ah-Bk}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2}}=0.\]

Then necessarily \(A=f_x(a)\), \(B=f_y(a)\). In practice: compute \(f_x(0,0)\) and \(f_y(0,0)\) from the definition, then test whether \(\frac{f(h,k)-f(0,0)-f_xh-f_yk}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2}}\to0\). If it fails along any path, \(f\) is not differentiable.

Hierarchy (arrows are the only valid implications):

\[C^1\ \Rightarrow\ \text{differentiable}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{continuous, and all directional derivatives exist}.\]

Also: differentiable \(\Rightarrow\) \(f_x,f_y\) exist. Having \(f_x,f_y\) continuous in a neighbourhood of \(a\) (i.e. \(C^1\)) is sufficient for differentiability but not necessary (\(x^2\sin(1/x)\) in one variable). Existence of \(f_x,f_y\) alone gives neither continuity nor differentiability.

Standard checks at the origin (the 2025 PYQ is the first row).

\(f\) (\(f(0,0)=0\)) Continuous \(f_x,f_y\) at \(0\) Differentiable
\(\dfrac{xy}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\) Yes \(0,0\) No (\(\frac{f}{r}=\frac12\) along \(y=x\))
\(\dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}\) Yes \(0,0\) No (\(\frac fr=\frac1{2\sqrt2}\) along \(y=x\))
\(\dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\) No \(0,0\) No
\(\sqrt{\lvert xy\rvert}\) Yes \(0,0\) No
\(\dfrac{x^3y}{x^2+y^2}\) Yes \(0,0\) Yes (\(\lvert f\rvert\le r^2\))

Mixed partial derivatives

Schwarz-Clairaut theorem. If \(f_x,f_y,f_{xy}\) exist near \(a\) and \(f_{xy}\) is continuous at \(a\), then \(f_{yx}(a)\) exists and \(f_{yx}(a)=f_{xy}(a)\). In particular if \(f\in C^2\) then \(f_{xy}=f_{yx}\).

Counterexample: \(f(x,y)=\frac{xy(x^2-y^2)}{x^2+y^2}\), \(f(0,0)=0\). Here \(f_x(0,y)=-y\) and \(f_y(x,0)=x\), so \(\partial_y(f_x)(0,0)=-1\) but \(\partial_x(f_y)(0,0)=+1\). The mixed partials are not continuous at \(0\).

Chain rule, Jacobians, homogeneous functions

If \(z=f(u,v)\) with \(u=u(x,y)\), \(v=v(x,y)\) differentiable and \(f\) differentiable, then \(z_x=f_uu_x+f_vv_x\). In matrix form the derivative of a composite is the product of Jacobian matrices.

The Jacobian of \((u,v)\) with respect to \((x,y)\) is \(J=\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}=\det\begin{pmatrix}u_x&u_y\\v_x&v_y\end{pmatrix}\).

  • \(\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\cdot\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=1\) and \(\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(r,s)}=\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,s)}\).
  • If \(u,v\) are functionally dependent (\(u=\phi(v)\) or \(F(u,v)=0\)), then \(J=0\).
  • Example: \(u=x+y\), \(v=xy\) gives \(J=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\y&x\end{pmatrix}=x-y\). It vanishes only on \(y=x\), so \(u,v\) are not functionally dependent, but the map is not locally invertible at points of that line.
  • Polar: \(\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}=r\). Cylindrical: \(r\). Spherical \((\rho,\varphi,\theta)\): \(\rho^2\sin\varphi\). Change of variables in a double integral: \(dx\,dy=\lvert J\rvert\,du\,dv\).
  • Euler’s theorem. If \(f\) is homogeneous of degree \(n\) and differentiable, \(xf_x+yf_y=nf\). If twice differentiable: \(x^2f_{xx}+2xyf_{xy}+y^2f_{yy}=n(n-1)f\).

Inverse and implicit function theorems

Inverse function theorem. If \(F:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n\) is \(C^1\) and \(\det DF(a)\ne0\), then \(F\) is a \(C^1\) diffeomorphism of a neighbourhood of \(a\) onto a neighbourhood of \(F(a)\).

Implicit function theorem. Let \(F(x,y)\) be \(C^1\) near \((a,b)\) with \(F(a,b)=0\) and \(F_y(a,b)\ne0\). Then there is a unique \(C^1\) function \(y=g(x)\) near \(a\) with \(g(a)=b\) and \(F(x,g(x))=0\), and

\[g'(x)=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}.\]

General version: \(F:\mathbb{R}^{n+m}\to\mathbb{R}^m\) is \(C^1\), and the \(m\times m\) matrix \(\partial F/\partial y\) is invertible at \((a,b)\); then \(y=g(x)\) locally.

Taylor’s theorem and Hessian

If \(f\) is \(C^2\) near \(a\),

\[f(a+h)=f(a)+\nabla f(a)\cdot h+\tfrac12h^TH(a)h+o(\lVert h\rVert^2),\]

where \(H=\begin{pmatrix}f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\f_{xy}&f_{yy}\end{pmatrix}\). In two variables the second-order term is \(\frac12(f_{xx}h^2+2f_{xy}hk+f_{yy}k^2)\).

Maxima and minima (unconstrained)

A critical point has \(\nabla f=0\). At a local extremum of a differentiable function, \(\nabla f=0\); the converse is false.

Second derivative test (two variables). At a critical point with \(r=f_{xx}\), \(s=f_{xy}\), \(t=f_{yy}\) and \(D=rt-s^2\):

Condition Conclusion
\(D>0\), \(r>0\) Local minimum
\(D>0\), \(r<0\) Local maximum
\(D<0\) Saddle point
\(D=0\) Test fails: examine directly

In \(n\) variables: \(H\) positive definite gives a local minimum, negative definite a local maximum, indefinite a saddle; semidefinite is inconclusive. Examples with \(D=0\): \(x^4+y^4\) (minimum), \(-x^4-y^4\) (maximum), \(x^3+y^3\) (neither).

On a closed bounded region a continuous function attains its absolute maximum and minimum: compare the values at interior critical points with the extreme values on the boundary.

Global extrema on a disc. Find the extreme values of \(f=x^2+2y^2-x\) on \(x^2+y^2\le1\). Interior: \(f_x=2x-1=0\), \(f_y=4y=0\) give \((\frac12,0)\) with \(f=-\frac14\). Boundary: put \(y^2=1-x^2\), so \(f=2-x^2-x\) for \(x\in[-1,1]\); its critical point \(x=-\frac12\) gives \(\frac94\), and the ends give \(f(1)=0\), \(f(-1)=2\). Therefore the maximum is \(\frac94\) at \(\bigl(-\frac12,\pm\frac{\sqrt3}2\bigr)\) and the minimum is \(-\frac14\) at \((\frac12,0)\).

Minimum along every line is not a local minimum. \(f(x,y)=(y-x^2)(y-2x^2)=y^2-3x^2y+2x^4\) has a strict minimum at \(0\) along every line through the origin (\(f(t,mt)=m^2t^2+\dots>0\), and \(f(0,y)=y^2\)), yet \(f<0\) on the parabola \(y=\frac32x^2\) (value \(-\frac14x^4\)). So \(0\) is a saddle, and here \(D=0\) (\(f_{xx}=f_{xy}=0\), \(f_{yy}=2\) at the origin), so the Hessian test is silent.

Lagrange multipliers

To extremise \(f\) subject to \(g(x)=0\) (with \(\nabla g\ne0\)): solve \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) together with \(g=0\). For several constraints, \(\nabla f=\sum\lambda_i\nabla g_i\). The candidates are compared (the constraint set must be compact, or the nature of the point checked) to decide max or min. Equivalent: critical points of \(L=f-\lambda g\).

Two constraints. Minimise \(x^2+y^2+z^2\) subject to \(x+y+z=1\) and \(x+2y+3z=3\). Solve \(2x=\lambda+\mu\), \(2y=\lambda+2\mu\), \(2z=\lambda+3\mu\), so \(y=\frac{x+z}2\). With the constraints this gives \(y=\frac13\), \(x=-\frac16\), \(z=\frac56\), and the minimum value is \(\frac{1}{36}+\frac19+\frac{25}{36}=\frac56\). This is the squared distance from the origin to the line of intersection of the two planes.

Formulas and results

  • Path test: different values on two paths means no limit. Polar: \(\lvert f-L\rvert\le g(r)\to0\).
  • \(D_uf=\nabla f\cdot u\) if \(f\) is differentiable; max rate \(=\lVert\nabla f\rVert\).
  • \(C^1\Rightarrow\) differentiable \(\Rightarrow\) continuous and \(f_x,f_y\) exist. Reverse implications fail.
  • Differentiable at \(0\): \(\frac{f(h,k)-f_xh-f_yk}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2}}\to0\).
  • Schwarz: \(f_{xy}=f_{yx}\) if one mixed partial is continuous at the point (or \(f\in C^2\)).
  • \(\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}=1\); polar \(J=r\); spherical \(\rho^2\sin\varphi\).
  • Euler: \(xf_x+yf_y=nf\); \(x^2f_{xx}+2xyf_{xy}+y^2f_{yy}=n(n-1)f\).
  • Implicit: \(\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}\) if \(F_y\ne0\).
  • Hessian test: \(D=rt-s^2\); \(D>0\) with \(r>0\) is a minimum, \(r<0\) a maximum; \(D<0\) saddle.
  • Lagrange: \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\), \(g=0\).
  • \(\max\sin x\sin y\sin(x+y)=\frac{3\sqrt3}8\) at \(x=y=\frac\pi3\).

Worked examples

Example 1: continuity and differentiability at the origin

Let \(f(x,y)=\frac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}\), \(f(0,0)=0\).

  • Continuity: \(\lvert f\rvert=\frac{x^2}{x^2+y^2}\lvert y\rvert\le\lvert y\rvert\to0\). Continuous.
  • Partials: \(f(h,0)=0\), \(f(0,k)=0\), so \(f_x(0,0)=f_y(0,0)=0\).
  • Differentiable? Need \(\frac{f(h,k)}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2}}\to0\). Along \(k=h\): \(\frac{h^3/(2h^2)}{\sqrt2\lvert h\rvert}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt2}\ne0\). Not differentiable.

Example 2: path test

Show \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\frac{xy^2}{x^2+y^4}\) does not exist.

Along \(y=mx\): \(\frac{m^2x^3}{x^2+m^4x^4}=\frac{m^2x}{1+m^4x^2}\to0\). Along \(x=y^2\): \(\frac{y^4}{y^4+y^4}=\frac12\). Two different values, so no limit.

Example 3: classifying critical points

\(f(x,y)=x^3+y^3-3xy\). \(f_x=3x^2-3y=0\) and \(f_y=3y^2-3x=0\) give \(y=x^2\), \(x=y^2\), so \(x^4=x\): \(x=0\) or \(x=1\). Critical points: \((0,0)\) and \((1,1)\). \(r=6x\), \(s=-3\), \(t=6y\).

  • At \((0,0)\): \(D=0\cdot0-9=-9<0\), saddle point.
  • At \((1,1)\): \(D=36-9=27>0\), \(r=6>0\), local minimum with \(f(1,1)=-1\).

Example 4: the 2025 maximum

Maximise \(f=\sin x\sin y\sin(x+y)\) on \((0,\pi)^2\). \(f_x=\sin y\,\sin(2x+y)\) and \(f_y=\sin x\,\sin(x+2y)\). With \(\sin x,\sin y\ne0\) we need \(\sin(2x+y)=0\) and \(\sin(x+2y)=0\); inside the square the possibilities are \(2x+y=\pi=x+2y\), giving \(x=y=\frac\pi3\), and \(2x+y=2\pi=x+2y\), giving \(x=y=\frac{2\pi}3\) (the mixed cases lie on the boundary or outside). The second point has \(f=\frac34\sin\frac{4\pi}3=-\frac{3\sqrt3}8\), the minimum. At \(x=y=\frac\pi3\)

\[f=\left(\frac{\sqrt3}2\right)^3=\frac{3\sqrt3}8.\]

Hessian: \(r=t=-\sqrt3\), \(s=-\frac{\sqrt3}2\), \(D=3-\frac34>0\) and \(r<0\), so it is a local maximum. On the boundary \(f=0\), so it is the absolute maximum on the closed square.

Example 5: Lagrange multipliers

Maximise \(xyz\) subject to \(x+y+z=6\) with \(x,y,z>0\). \(\nabla f=(yz,xz,xy)=\lambda(1,1,1)\), so \(yz=xz=xy\), giving \(x=y=z=2\) and \(f=8\). This is the maximum since \(xyz\to0\) on the boundary of the simplex (or by AM-GM: \(xyz\le\left(\frac{x+y+z}3\right)^3=8\)).

Example 6: implicit differentiation and Jacobian

(a) For \(x^2+xy+y^2=3\) at \((1,1)\): \(F_x=2x+y=3\), \(F_y=x+2y=3\), so \(\frac{dy}{dx}=-\frac{3}{3}=-1\).

(b) For \(u=x+y\), \(v=xy\): \(\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\y&x\end{pmatrix}=x-y\). It vanishes on \(x=y\), where the map \((x,y)\mapsto(u,v)\) folds.

Example 7: a one-variable extremum (2025 style)

The distance between \(y=x\) and \(y=x^2\) at the same \(x\in[0,1]\) is \(d(x)=x-x^2\). \(d'(x)=1-2x=0\) at \(x=\frac12\), \(d(\frac12)=\frac14\), and \(d(0)=d(1)=0\). Maximum distance \(\frac14\).

Common traps

  • Lines are not enough. A limit that is the same along all lines \(y=mx\) can still fail (\(\frac{x^2y}{x^4+y^2}\) along \(y=x^2\)).
  • Partials exist is weak. Existence of \(f_x,f_y\) does not give continuity (\(\frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\)) or differentiability (\(\frac{xy}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\)).
  • Continuous plus partials still not enough. \(\frac{xy}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\) is continuous with partials \(0\) but not differentiable.
  • Directional derivatives for non-differentiable \(f\). The formula \(\nabla f\cdot u\) may fail; compute from the limit definition.
  • \(C^1\) is sufficient, not necessary.
  • Mixed partials. They can differ at a point where they are not continuous. Check which order is meant.
  • \(D=0\) means no conclusion, not saddle.
  • Boundary. On a closed region the absolute extremum may be on the boundary. In the Lagrange method also check that \(\nabla g\ne0\).
  • Jacobian order. \(\frac{\partial(u,v)}{\partial(x,y)}\) changes sign if \(u,v\) are swapped; \(\frac{\partial(y,x)}{\partial(\cdot)}\) is the negative.
  • Euler’s theorem factor. The right side is \(nf\), not \(f\) or \(n\).

Previous-year questions

2025, Q40

The function \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{xy}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\) for \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\), \(f(0,0)=0\), is: (A) not continuous at \((0,0)\); (B) differentiable at \((0,0)\); (C) such that \(f_x(0,0)\) and \(f_y(0,0)\) do not exist; (D) continuous but not differentiable at \((0,0)\). Answer: D (official key).

Solution: \(\lvert f\rvert\le\frac r2\to0\), so continuous. \(f(h,0)=f(0,k)=0\) gives \(f_x=f_y=0\). Along \(k=h\), \(\frac{f(h,h)}{\sqrt{2}\lvert h\rvert}=\frac12\ne0\), so the differentiability limit fails.

2025, Q43

The maximum value of \(\sin x\sin y\sin(x+y)\) is attained at (A) \((\frac\pi4,\frac\pi4)\); (B) \((\frac\pi3,\frac\pi3)\); (C) \((\frac{2\pi}3,\frac{2\pi}3)\); (D) \((\frac{3\pi}4,\frac{3\pi}4)\). Answer: B (official key).

Solution: critical point \(2x+y=\pi\), \(x+2y=\pi\) gives \(x=y=\frac\pi3\), maximum value \(\frac{3\sqrt3}8\) (Example 4). Check the others: at \((\frac\pi4,\frac\pi4)\) the value is \(\frac12<\frac{3\sqrt3}8\approx0.65\); at \((\frac{2\pi}3,\frac{2\pi}3)\) it is \(\frac34\sin\frac{4\pi}3<0\); at \((\frac{3\pi}4,\frac{3\pi}4)\) it is \(\frac12\sin\frac{3\pi}2=-\frac12\).

2025, Q79

The maximum distance between the curves \(y=x\) and \(y=x^2\) on \([0,1]\) is (A) \(1\); (B) \(\frac12\); (C) \(\frac14\); (D) \(\frac16\). Answer: C (official key).

Solution: the vertical gap is \(x-x^2\), maximised at \(x=\frac12\) with value \(\frac14\) (Example 7).

Quick revision

  • Limit: same value along all paths; one pair of different paths kills it; polar squeeze proves it.
  • \(\frac{x^2y}{x^4+y^2}\): \(0\) on lines, \(\frac12\) on \(y=x^2\), so no limit.
  • \(C^1\Rightarrow\) differentiable \(\Rightarrow\) continuous and directional derivatives exist; no reverse arrow.
  • \(\frac{xy}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\) at \(0\): continuous, partials \(0\), not differentiable.
  • \(D_uf=\nabla f\cdot u\) only when \(f\) is differentiable; max rate \(\lVert\nabla f\rVert\).
  • Schwarz: \(f_{xy}=f_{yx}\) if a mixed partial is continuous; counterexample \(\frac{xy(x^2-y^2)}{x^2+y^2}\) gives \(\mp1\).
  • Implicit: \(y'=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}\); polar Jacobian \(r\); Euler \(xf_x+yf_y=nf\).
  • Hessian: \(D=rt-s^2\); \(D>0,r>0\) min; \(D>0,r<0\) max; \(D<0\) saddle; \(D=0\) inconclusive.
  • Lagrange: \(\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g\) with \(g=0\).
  • \(\max\sin x\sin y\sin(x+y)=\frac{3\sqrt3}8\) at \((\frac\pi3,\frac\pi3)\).
