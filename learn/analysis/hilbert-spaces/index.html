Why it matters for the exam

Hilbert spaces were examined in both full papers: 2025 Q38 (self-adjoint iff \(\langle Tx,x\rangle\) is real) and Q39 (the space hierarchy), plus two 2019 questions on inner product spaces and \(\ell^p\). Typical one-liners: is this norm induced by an inner product, which of \(\ell^p\) is Hilbert, what does the projection or Riesz theorem need, which operator class is which.

Core ideas

Inner product spaces

An inner product on a vector space \(H\) over \(\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\) is \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:H\times H\to\mathbb{F}\) with

  • \(\langle x,x\rangle\ge0\) and \(=0\) iff \(x=0\);
  • \(\langle\alpha x+\beta y,z\rangle=\alpha\langle x,z\rangle+\beta\langle y,z\rangle\) (linear in the first slot);
  • \(\langle x,y\rangle=\overline{\langle y,x\rangle}\) (so conjugate-linear in the second slot; some books use the opposite convention).

The induced norm is \(\lVert x\rVert=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}\).

  • Cauchy-Schwarz: \(\lvert\langle x,y\rangle\rvert\le\lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert\), with equality iff \(x,y\) are linearly dependent.
  • The triangle inequality follows. The inner product is jointly continuous.
  • Pythagoras: \(x\perp y\Rightarrow\lVert x+y\rVert^2=\lVert x\rVert^2+\lVert y\rVert^2\).
  • Polarisation (real): \(\langle x,y\rangle=\frac14\big(\lVert x+y\rVert^2-\lVert x-y\rVert^2\big)\). Complex: \(\langle x,y\rangle=\frac14\sum_{k=0}^{3}i^k\lVert x+i^ky\rVert^2\).

Standard examples:

Space Inner product
\(\mathbb{R}^n\), \(\mathbb{C}^n\) \(\sum x_i\overline{y_i}\)
\(\ell^2\) \(\sum x_i\overline{y_i}\)
\(L^2[a,b]\) \(\int f\overline g\)
\(C[a,b]\) \(\int f\overline g\) (inner product space, not complete)
\(M_n(\mathbb{C})\) \(\operatorname{tr}(B^*A)\) (Frobenius)

The parallelogram law: which norms come from an inner product

In an inner product space, \(\lVert x+y\rVert^2+\lVert x-y\rVert^2=2\lVert x\rVert^2+2\lVert y\rVert^2.\)

Jordan-von Neumann theorem. A normed space has a norm coming from an inner product iff the parallelogram law holds for all \(x,y\); the inner product is then recovered by polarisation.

To show a norm is not induced, find \(x,y\) where the law fails:

Norm Test vectors Left side Right side Verdict
\(\lVert\cdot\rVert_p\) on \(\ell^p\) or \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (\(n\ge2\)), \(p<\infty\) \(e_1,e_2\) \(2\cdot2^{2/p}\) \(4\) holds only for \(p=2\)
\(\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty\) on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) or \(\ell^\infty\) \(e_1,e_2\) \(1+1=2\) \(4\) fails
sup norm on \(C[0,1]\) \(f=1\), \(g=t\) \(\lVert1+t\rVert^2+\lVert1-t\rVert^2=4+1=5\) \(2+2=4\) fails
\(L^p[0,1]\), \(p\ne2\) \(\chi_{[0,1/2]},\chi_{[1/2,1]}\) \(2\) \(4\cdot2^{-2/p}\) holds only for \(p=2\)

So \(\ell^p\) and \(L^p\) are Hilbert spaces only for \(p=2\); the sup norm on \(C[a,b]\) and \(\ell^\infty\) are never induced by an inner product. In \(\mathbb{R}^2\) the \(1\)-norm and \(\infty\)-norm are not Euclidean.

Hilbert spaces and the hierarchy

A Hilbert space is a complete inner product space (2019 asked this definition).

\(\text{Hilbert}\ \subsetneq\ \text{Banach}\ \subsetneq\ \text{normed}\ \subsetneq\ \text{metric}\ \subsetneq\ \text{topological space}.\)

Each inclusion means “is a special case of”, and each is strict:

Step Example showing it is strict
Hilbert \(\subsetneq\) Banach \(\ell^1\), \(\ell^p\) (\(p\ne2\)), \(C[0,1]_{\sup}\) (parallelogram law fails)
Banach \(\subsetneq\) normed \(c_{00}\), \(C[0,1]\) with \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_2\)
normed \(\subsetneq\) metric a set with the discrete metric (not a vector space norm); \((0,1)\)
metric \(\subsetneq\) topological an infinite set with the cofinite topology; two points with the indiscrete topology (not metrisable)

Also: inner product space (not complete) sits between normed and Hilbert. \(\ell^2\) is Hilbert, hence Banach, normed, metric and topological (2025 Q39). For \(p\ne2\), \(\ell^p\) is Banach but not Hilbert; \(\ell^2\) is not “not a topological space”.

Writing a sequence in the order “\(\ell^2\) as a topological space, normed space, Banach space, Hilbert space” is the order of increasing structure, which is what the official key chose in 2025 Q39.

Orthogonality and the orthogonal complement

\(x\perp y\) if \(\langle x,y\rangle=0\). For \(M\subseteq H\), \(M^\perp=\{x:\langle x,m\rangle=0\ \forall m\in M\}\).

  • \(M^\perp\) is always a closed subspace, even if \(M\) is not.
  • \(M\subseteq M^{\perp\perp}\); \(M^\perp=(\overline{\operatorname{span}M})^\perp\).
  • \(M\subseteq N\Rightarrow N^\perp\subseteq M^\perp\). \(\{0\}^\perp=H\), \(H^\perp=\{0\}\). \(M\cap M^\perp\subseteq\{0\}\).
  • In a Hilbert space: \(M^{\perp\perp}=\overline{\operatorname{span}M}\); so for a closed subspace \(M^{\perp\perp}=M\), and \(M\) is dense iff \(M^\perp=\{0\}\).
  • Finite dimension: \(\dim M+\dim M^\perp=\dim H\).

Projection theorem

Projection theorem. Let \(M\) be a closed subspace of a Hilbert space \(H\). Then every \(x\in H\) has a unique decomposition \(x=m+n\) with \(m\in M\), \(n\in M^\perp\), i.e. \(H=M\oplus M^\perp\). Moreover \(m=P_Mx\) is the unique closest point of \(M\) to \(x\): \(\lVert x-m\rVert=d(x,M)=\min_{y\in M}\lVert x-y\rVert\).

More generally, in a Hilbert space every nonempty closed convex set has a unique point of minimum norm (and a unique nearest point to any \(x\)).

Both hypotheses matter:

  • \(M\) not closed: \(M=c_{00}\subset\ell^2\) has \(M^\perp=\{0\}\), so \(M\oplus M^\perp=M\ne\ell^2\).
  • \(H\) not complete: the closed subspace \(M=\{f\in C[-1,1]:f=0\text{ on }[-1,0]\}\) of the inner product space \(C[-1,1]\) with the \(L^2\) inner product has \(M^\perp=\{f:f=0\text{ on }[0,1]\}\) and \(M\oplus M^\perp\ne C[-1,1]\).

The orthogonal projection \(P=P_M\) satisfies \(P^2=P=P^*\), \(R(P)=M\), \(N(P)=M^\perp\), \(\lVert P\rVert=1\) (if \(M\ne\{0\}\)), and \(P_{M^\perp}=I-P_M\). For \(M=\operatorname{span}\{n\}\): \(P_Mx=\frac{\langle x,n\rangle}{\langle n,n\rangle}n\).

Orthonormal sets, Gram-Schmidt, Bessel and Parseval

\(\{e_\alpha\}\) is orthonormal if \(\langle e_\alpha,e_\beta\rangle=\delta_{\alpha\beta}\). Orthonormal vectors are linearly independent.

Gram-Schmidt. From independent \(v_1,v_2,\dots\) define \(u_1=v_1,\qquad u_k=v_k-\sum_{j<k}\frac{\langle v_k,u_j\rangle}{\langle u_j,u_j\rangle}u_j,\qquad e_k=\frac{u_k}{\lVert u_k\rVert}.\) Then \(\{e_k\}\) is orthonormal and \(\operatorname{span}\{e_1,\dots,e_k\}=\operatorname{span}\{v_1,\dots,v_k\}\) for every \(k\). Applied to \(1,x,x^2,\dots\) on \(L^2[-1,1]\) it gives the Legendre polynomials \(1,\ x,\ x^2-\tfrac13,\dots\) (up to normalisation).

Bessel’s inequality. For any orthonormal sequence \(\{e_n\}\) and \(x\in H\), \(\sum_n\lvert\langle x,e_n\rangle\rvert^2\le\lVert x\rVert^2.\)

Parseval’s identity holds for a given \(x\) iff \(x=\sum\langle x,e_n\rangle e_n\). For all \(x\), the following are equivalent for an orthonormal set \(\{e_n\}\) in a Hilbert space:

  1. \(\{e_n\}\) is a complete orthonormal set (an orthonormal basis): maximal; equivalently \(\langle x,e_n\rangle=0\ \forall n\Rightarrow x=0\);
  2. \(\overline{\operatorname{span}}\{e_n\}=H\);
  3. \(x=\sum\langle x,e_n\rangle e_n\) for every \(x\) (convergence in norm);
  4. \(\lVert x\rVert^2=\sum\lvert\langle x,e_n\rangle\rvert^2\) for every \(x\) (Parseval); and then \(\langle x,y\rangle=\sum\langle x,e_n\rangle\overline{\langle y,e_n\rangle}\).

Riesz-Fischer. If \(\sum\lvert c_n\rvert^2<\infty\) then \(\sum c_ne_n\) converges in \(H\) and \(\lVert\sum c_ne_n\rVert^2=\sum\lvert c_n\rvert^2\).

Facts:

  • Every Hilbert space has an orthonormal basis (Zorn’s lemma). \(H\) is separable iff it has a countable orthonormal basis; every infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert space is isometrically isomorphic to \(\ell^2\).
  • An orthonormal basis of an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space is not a Hamel basis (a Hamel basis of a Banach space is uncountable if infinite).
  • Orthonormal bases: \(\{e_n\}\) in \(\ell^2\); \(\{e^{inx}/\sqrt{2\pi}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\) in \(L^2[0,2\pi]\); \(\{1/\sqrt{2\pi},\ \cos nx/\sqrt\pi,\ \sin nx/\sqrt\pi\}\); the normalised Legendre polynomials in \(L^2[-1,1]\).
  • Bessel can be strict: \(\{e_2,e_3,\dots\}\) in \(\ell^2\) is orthonormal but not complete, and \(x=e_1\) gives \(0<1\).
  • \(e_n\to0\) weakly but not in norm (\(\lVert e_n\rVert=1\)).

Riesz representation theorem

Theorem. Let \(H\) be a Hilbert space and \(f\in H^*\) (a bounded linear functional). Then there is a unique \(y\in H\) with \(f(x)=\langle x,y\rangle\) for all \(x\), and \(\lVert f\rVert=\lVert y\rVert\).

So \(H^*\cong H\) (conjugate-linear isometry in the complex case); Hilbert spaces are reflexive. Proof idea: if \(f\ne0\), \(N(f)\) is closed, so \(N(f)^\perp\) is one-dimensional, spanned by some \(z\); take \(y=\overline{f(z)}z/\lVert z\rVert^2\).

Completeness is needed: on the inner product space \(c_{00}\subset\ell^2\), \(f(x)=\sum x_n/n\) is bounded but no \(y\in c_{00}\) represents it (\(y_n=1/n\) is not finitely supported).

Adjoint operator

For \(T\in B(H)\) there is a unique \(T^*\in B(H)\) with \(\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,T^*y\rangle\) for all \(x,y\) (Riesz). Properties:

  • \((T^*)^*=T\), \((S+T)^*=S^*+T^*\), \((\alpha T)^*=\bar\alpha T^*\), \((ST)^*=T^*S^*\).
  • \(\lVert T^*\rVert=\lVert T\rVert\) and \(\lVert T^*T\rVert=\lVert T\rVert^2\).
  • \(N(T^*)=R(T)^\perp\), \(\overline{R(T)}=N(T^*)^\perp\); \(N(T)=R(T^*)^\perp\).
  • Matrices: \(T^*\) is the conjugate transpose. On \(\ell^2\): the adjoint of the right shift is the left shift. Multiplication by \(\phi\) on \(L^2\) has adjoint multiplication by \(\overline\phi\).
  • \(T\) invertible \(\Rightarrow\) \(T^*\) invertible with \((T^*)^{-1}=(T^{-1})^*\).

Self-adjoint, normal, unitary and other operators

Class Definition Eigenvalues and facts
Self-adjoint (Hermitian) \(T=T^*\) eigenvalues real; eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues orthogonal; \(\lVert T\rVert=\sup_{\lVert x\rVert=1}\lvert\langle Tx,x\rangle\rvert\)
Skew-adjoint \(T^*=-T\) eigenvalues purely imaginary
Unitary \(U^*U=UU^*=I\) \(\lvert\lambda\rvert=1\); surjective isometry; preserves inner products and maps orthonormal bases to orthonormal bases
Normal \(TT^*=T^*T\) \(\lVert Tx\rVert=\lVert T^*x\rVert\); \(N(T)=N(T^*)\); eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues orthogonal
Projection (orthogonal) \(P^2=P=P^*\) eigenvalues \(0,1\)
Positive \(\langle Tx,x\rangle\ge0\) self-adjoint (complex \(H\)), eigenvalues \(\ge0\)

Inclusions: self-adjoint, skew-adjoint and unitary operators are all normal; the converse fails. Normal operators in finite-dimensional complex spaces are exactly those diagonalisable by a unitary matrix. A compact self-adjoint operator has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors (spectral theorem).

Self-adjointness test (2025 Q38). On a complex Hilbert space, \(T=T^*\iff\langle Tx,x\rangle\in\mathbb{R}\text{ for all }x.\) Proof: if \(\langle Tx,x\rangle\) is real then \(\langle Tx,x\rangle=\overline{\langle Tx,x\rangle}=\langle x,Tx\rangle=\langle T^*x,x\rangle\), so \(\langle(T-T^*)x,x\rangle=0\) for all \(x\); in a complex space this forces \(T-T^*=0\) by polarisation. The test fails on real spaces: rotation by \(90^\circ\) on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) has \(\langle Tx,x\rangle=0\) for all \(x\) but \(T^*=-T\ne T\). Also \(\langle Tx,x\rangle=0\) for all \(x\) forces \(T=0\) on a complex space only.

Isometry versus unitary: an isometry has \(U^*U=I\). In finite dimensions every isometry is unitary. On \(\ell^2\) the right shift \(S\) is an isometry (\(S^*S=I\)) but not unitary: \(SS^*\ne I\) because \(S\) is not onto. \(S\) is not normal.

Non-normal examples: \(\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\) and the right shift. Normal but not self-adjoint: \(\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}\) (real orthogonal, skew) and \(\operatorname{diag}(i,1)\).

Formulas and results

  • \(\lvert\langle x,y\rangle\rvert\le\lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert\); Pythagoras for \(x\perp y\).
  • Parallelogram law \(\lVert x+y\rVert^2+\lVert x-y\rVert^2=2\lVert x\rVert^2+2\lVert y\rVert^2\) \(\iff\) norm from an inner product (Jordan-von Neumann).
  • Polarisation (real): \(\langle x,y\rangle=\frac14(\lVert x+y\rVert^2-\lVert x-y\rVert^2)\).
  • \(\ell^p\) is Hilbert only for \(p=2\); \(L^p\) likewise; sup norm never.
  • Hilbert \(\subset\) Banach \(\subset\) normed \(\subset\) metric \(\subset\) topological.
  • \(M^\perp\) closed; \(M^{\perp\perp}=\overline{\operatorname{span}}M\) in a Hilbert space.
  • Projection theorem: \(H=M\oplus M^\perp\) for closed \(M\) in a Hilbert \(H\); \(P_Mx=\sum\langle x,e_k\rangle e_k\) for an orthonormal basis \(\{e_k\}\) of \(M\).
  • Distance to a subspace: \(d(x,M)=\lVert x-P_Mx\rVert\), \(d(x,M)^2=\lVert x\rVert^2-\sum\lvert\langle x,e_k\rangle\rvert^2\).
  • Gram-Schmidt: \(u_k=v_k-\sum_{j<k}\frac{\langle v_k,u_j\rangle}{\langle u_j,u_j\rangle}u_j\).
  • Bessel: \(\sum\lvert\langle x,e_n\rangle\rvert^2\le\lVert x\rVert^2\); Parseval: equality for a complete orthonormal set.
  • Riesz: \(f(x)=\langle x,y\rangle\), \(\lVert f\rVert=\lVert y\rVert\) (Hilbert).
  • Adjoint: \(\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,T^*y\rangle\); \(\lVert T^*T\rVert=\lVert T\rVert^2\); \(N(T^*)=R(T)^\perp\).
  • Self-adjoint \(\iff\langle Tx,x\rangle\) real (complex \(H\)). Unitary \(\iff\) onto isometry. Normal \(\iff\lVert Tx\rVert=\lVert T^*x\rVert\).

Worked examples

Example 1: is the norm induced by an inner product?

Test \(\lVert(x,y)\rVert=\lvert x\rvert+\lvert y\rvert\) on \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Take \(u=(1,0)\), \(v=(0,1)\): \(\lVert u+v\rVert^2+\lVert u-v\rVert^2=4+4=8\), while \(2\lVert u\rVert^2+2\lVert v\rVert^2=4\). The parallelogram law fails, so no inner product induces this norm. Same test for \(\ell^p\) with \(e_1,e_2\): the left side is \(2\cdot2^{2/p}\), which equals \(4\) only for \(p=2\).

Example 2: Gram-Schmidt

Apply Gram-Schmidt to \(v_1=(1,1,0),\ v_2=(1,0,1),\ v_3=(0,1,1)\) in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

  • \(u_1=(1,1,0)\).
  • \(u_2=v_2-\frac{\langle v_2,u_1\rangle}{\langle u_1,u_1\rangle}u_1=(1,0,1)-\frac12(1,1,0)=(\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,1)\).
  • \(u_3=v_3-\frac{\langle v_3,u_1\rangle}{2}u_1-\frac{\langle v_3,u_2\rangle}{3/2}u_2=(0,1,1)-\frac12(1,1,0)-\frac{1/2}{3/2}(\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,1)=(-\tfrac23,\tfrac23,\tfrac23)\).

Normalising: \(e_1=\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)\), \(e_2=\frac1{\sqrt6}(1,-1,2)\), \(e_3=\frac1{\sqrt3}(-1,1,1)\).

Continued, in \(L^2[-1,1]\): from \(1,x,x^2\) the third vector is \(x^2-\frac13\) (since \(\langle x^2,1\rangle=\frac23\), \(\langle1,1\rangle=2\) and \(\langle x^2,x\rangle=0\)). The distance from \(x^2\) to \(\operatorname{span}\{1,x\}\) is \(\lVert x^2-\frac13\rVert=\sqrt{\int_{-1}^1(x^2-\frac13)^2dx}=\sqrt{8/45}\).

Example 3: orthogonal projection and distance

Let \(M=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^3:x_1+x_2+x_3=0\}\), \(n=(1,1,1)\) spans \(M^\perp\). For \(x=(1,2,3)\): \(P_{M^\perp}x=\frac{\langle x,n\rangle}{\langle n,n\rangle}n=\frac63(1,1,1)=(2,2,2)\), so

\(P_Mx=x-(2,2,2)=(-1,0,1),\qquad d(x,M)=\lVert(2,2,2)\rVert=2\sqrt3.\)

Check: \((-1,0,1)\in M\) and \(x-P_Mx=(2,2,2)\perp M\).

Example 4: Parseval gives \(\sum1/n^2\)

On \(L^2(-\pi,\pi)\) with the orthonormal basis \(\{1/\sqrt{2\pi},\cos nx/\sqrt\pi,\sin nx/\sqrt\pi\}\), take \(f(x)=x\). Odd \(f\) gives only sine coefficients \(b_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi x\sin nx\,dx=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}n\). Parseval: \(\int_{-\pi}^\pi x^2dx=\pi\sum b_n^2\), i.e. \(\frac{2\pi^3}{3}=\pi\sum\frac4{n^2}\). Hence

\(\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}.\)

Example 5: classify an operator

\(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\i&1\end{pmatrix}\) on \(\mathbb{C}^2\). \(A^*=\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\-i&1\end{pmatrix}\). Then \(AA^*=A^*A=2I\).

  • \(AA^*=A^*A\), so \(A\) is normal.
  • \(A^*\ne A\) (the entries \(i\) and \(-i\) differ), so not self-adjoint.
  • \(AA^*=2I\ne I\), so not unitary; but \(A/\sqrt2\) is unitary.

Example 6: Riesz representation on \(\ell^2\)

\(f(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}x_n/n\) is bounded on \(\ell^2\) by Cauchy-Schwarz. Its representer is \(y=(1/n)\in\ell^2\) (real case), and \(\lVert f\rVert=\lVert y\rVert=\big(\sum1/n^2\big)^{1/2}=\pi/\sqrt6\approx1.2825\).

Common traps

  • A norm induced by an inner product must satisfy the parallelogram law. \(\ell^p\), \(L^p\) (\(p\ne2\)), \(\ell^\infty\) and the sup norm on \(C[a,b]\) do not. Every \(\ell^p\) (\(1\le p\le\infty\)) is Banach; only \(\ell^2\) is Hilbert.
  • “Hilbert space” requires completeness. \(C[a,b]\) with \(\int f\bar g\) and \(c_{00}\subset\ell^2\) are inner product spaces that are not Hilbert.
  • Hierarchy direction: every Hilbert space is Banach, but not every Banach space is Hilbert; every normed space is metric but not conversely.
  • Projection theorem needs \(M\) closed and \(H\) complete. For non-closed \(M\), \(M^{\perp\perp}=\overline M\), not \(M\).
  • \(M^\perp\) is closed for every \(M\), but \(M^{\perp\perp}=M\) only for closed subspaces.
  • Bessel is an inequality for any orthonormal set; Parseval (equality for all \(x\)) needs a complete orthonormal set.
  • An orthonormal basis of an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space is not a Hamel basis.
  • Self-adjoint iff \(\langle Tx,x\rangle\) real holds only over \(\mathbb{C}\); real rotation by \(90^\circ\) is the counterexample.
  • Unitary \(=\) isometry and onto. The right shift on \(\ell^2\) is an isometry but not unitary and not normal.
  • Normal does not mean self-adjoint: unitary and skew-adjoint operators are normal.
  • \(\lVert T^*T\rVert=\lVert T\rVert^2\); \(\lVert T^*\rVert=\lVert T\rVert\) (not \(\lVert T\rVert^2\) for the adjoint alone).
  • Riesz representation needs completeness; \((\ell^p)^*=\ell^q\) is a different, Banach-space statement.
  • Unitary operators have \(\lvert\lambda\rvert=1\), not \(\lambda=\pm1\) (that is for self-adjoint unitary).

Previous-year questions

2025, Q38. An operator \(T\) on a Hilbert space is self-adjoint iff for all \(x\), \(\langle Tx,x\rangle\) is: (A) \(\lVert x\rVert^2\), (B) purely imaginary, (C) real, (D) zero only.

Answer: C (real). Over \(\mathbb{C}\), \(\langle Tx,x\rangle\in\mathbb{R}\) for all \(x\) gives \(\langle(T-T^*)x,x\rangle=0\) for all \(x\), hence \(T=T^*\). Conversely \(T=T^*\) gives \(\langle Tx,x\rangle=\langle x,Tx\rangle=\overline{\langle Tx,x\rangle}\), which is real. (A) would make \(T=I\)-like, (B) characterises skew-adjoint operators, (D) forces \(T=0\).

2025, Q39. Arrange in a sequence: I. \(\ell^2\) is a topological space; II. \(\ell^2\) is a Hilbert space; III. \(\ell^2\) is a Banach space; IV. \(\ell^2\) is a normed linear space. Options: (A) I, II, IV, III; (B) I, IV, III, II; (C) II, III, IV, I; (D) I, III, II, IV.

Answer: B (I, IV, III, II), the official key. Order of increasing structure: topological space, normed space, Banach space (complete normed), Hilbert space (norm from an inner product). The reverse chain II, III, IV, I (option C) is also a valid implication chain, but the key chose B. Learn the hierarchy in both directions.

2019, unnumbered 6 (reconstructed paper). Which statement is true: every inner product space is a Banach space; every inner product space is a Hilbert space; a Hilbert space is a complete inner product space; every Banach space is a Hilbert space?

Answer: a Hilbert space is a complete inner product space. The first two fail for \(C[a,b]\) with the \(L^2\) inner product (not complete); the last fails for \(\ell^1\) (parallelogram law).

2019, unnumbered 7 (reconstructed paper). Which statement is true: \(\ell^2\) is not a topological space; \(\ell^p\) (\(p\ne2\)) is a Hilbert space; \(\ell^p\) is not a Banach space; \(\ell^2\) is a Hilbert space?

Answer: \(\ell^2\) is a Hilbert space. \(\ell^2\) is complete with the inner product \(\sum x_i\bar y_i\). \(\ell^p\) is Banach for every \(p\ge1\), but Hilbert only for \(p=2\) (parallelogram law fails for \(e_1,e_2\) when \(p\ne2\)).

Quick revision

  • Inner product: positive definite, linear in the first slot, conjugate symmetric; Cauchy-Schwarz \(\lvert\langle x,y\rangle\rvert\le\lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert\).
  • Norm comes from an inner product iff the parallelogram law holds (Jordan-von Neumann).
  • \(\ell^p\), \(L^p\) are Hilbert only for \(p=2\); sup norms never.
  • Hilbert \(=\) complete inner product space; Hilbert \(\subset\) Banach \(\subset\) normed \(\subset\) metric \(\subset\) topological, all strict.
  • \(M^\perp\) is always closed; \(M^{\perp\perp}=\overline{\operatorname{span}}M\).
  • Projection theorem: closed subspace \(M\) of Hilbert \(H\) gives \(H=M\oplus M^\perp\) and a unique nearest point.
  • Gram-Schmidt subtracts projections onto earlier vectors.
  • Bessel: \(\sum\lvert\langle x,e_n\rangle\rvert^2\le\lVert x\rVert^2\); Parseval: equality iff the orthonormal set is complete.
  • Riesz: every bounded functional on a Hilbert space is \(\langle\cdot,y\rangle\), \(\lVert f\rVert=\lVert y\rVert\).
  • Adjoint: \(\langle Tx,y\rangle=\langle x,T^*y\rangle\); \(\lVert T^*T\rVert=\lVert T\rVert^2\); \(N(T^*)=R(T)^\perp\).
  • Self-adjoint iff \(\langle Tx,x\rangle\) real (complex space); unitary \(=\) onto isometry; normal \(\iff TT^*=T^*T\).
  • Self-adjoint, skew-adjoint, unitary are all normal; the right shift on \(\ell^2\) is an isometry, not unitary.
