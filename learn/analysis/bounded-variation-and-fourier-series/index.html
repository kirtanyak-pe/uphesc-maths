Why it matters for the exam

Bounded variation and Fourier series were both asked: a 2019 question on what a bounded function must satisfy, and the 2026 question on the decay of Fourier coefficients of a function of bounded variation that is also in \(L^2\). The usual exam moves are total variation of a simple function, the examples that separate BV from continuity and differentiability, the value of a Fourier series at a jump, a coefficient computed by integration, and the sums \(\sum\frac1{n^2}\), \(\sum\frac1{n^4}\) read off from Parseval.

Core ideas

Bounded variation

For \(f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) and a partition \(P=\{a=x_0<\dots<x_n=b\}\) let

\[V(f,P)=\sum_{i=1}^n\lvert f(x_i)-f(x_{i-1})\rvert,\qquad V_a^b(f)=\sup_PV(f,P).\]

\(f\) is of bounded variation (BV) on \([a,b]\) if \(V_a^b(f)<\infty\). The number \(V_a^b(f)\) is the total variation.

Basic facts:

  • BV implies bounded: \(\lvert f(x)\rvert\le\lvert f(a)\rvert+V_a^b(f)\). The converse is false: a bounded function need not be BV. The 2019 question asks exactly this: for a merely bounded function, the supremum of \(\lvert\alpha\rvert\) exists, but it need not be BV, continuous or constant.
  • Monotone implies BV, with \(V_a^b(f)=\lvert f(b)-f(a)\rvert\).
  • Lipschitz implies BV. If \(f\in C^1[a,b]\), then \(V_a^b(f)=\int_a^b\lvert f'(x)\rvert dx\). More generally, if \(\lvert f'\rvert\le K\) then \(V\le K(b-a)\).
  • Algebra. Sums, differences and products of BV functions are BV. A quotient \(f/g\) is BV if \(\lvert g\rvert\ge c>0\).
  • Additivity. \(V_a^c=V_a^b+V_b^c\) for \(a<b<c\).
  • Consequences. A BV function has one-sided limits at every point, has at most countably many discontinuities (all jumps), is differentiable almost everywhere, and is Riemann integrable.
  • \(\mathrm{BV}[a,b]\) is a Banach space under \(\lVert f\rVert=\lvert f(a)\rvert+V_a^b(f)\).

Jordan decomposition

Theorem. \(f\) is BV on \([a,b]\) if and only if \(f=g-h\) with \(g,h\) monotone increasing.

Construction: \(v(x)=V_a^x(f)\) is increasing and \(v-f\) is also increasing. So \(f=v-(v-f)\). The canonical form uses positive and negative variations \(P_a^x=\frac12\bigl(V_a^x+f(x)-f(a)\bigr)\), \(N_a^x=\frac12\bigl(V_a^x-f(x)+f(a)\bigr)\), so \(f(x)=f(a)+P_a^x-N_a^x\) and \(V_a^x=P_a^x+N_a^x\).

Because of this, any theorem about monotone functions (limits, countable discontinuities, a.e. differentiability) transfers to BV functions.

BV, continuity and differentiability: the examples

Function (value \(0\) at \(x=0\) where relevant) Continuous BV Differentiable
\(\operatorname{sgn}\)-type step, \(\lfloor x\rfloor\) on \([0,2.5]\) No Yes Not at jumps
\(\lvert x\rvert\) on \([-1,1]\) Yes Yes Not at \(0\)
\(x\sin(1/x)\) Yes No Not at \(0\)
\(\sin(1/x)\) No No Not at \(0\)
\(x^2\sin(1/x)\) Yes Yes Yes (derivative bounded, not continuous at \(0\))
\(x^2\sin(1/x^2)\) Yes No Yes (derivative unbounded)
Cantor function Yes Yes (monotone) \(f'=0\) a.e., not everywhere

So continuity and BV are independent; differentiability everywhere is not enough for BV (see \(x^2\sin(1/x^2)\)), but a bounded derivative is enough.

Why \(x\sin(1/x)\) is not BV: take the partition points \(x_k=\frac{2}{(2k+1)\pi}\), where \(f(x_k)=\pm x_k\) alternates in sign. Then \(V\ge\sum_k2x_k\sim\frac4\pi\sum\frac1{2k+1}\), which diverges like a harmonic series. The same computation for \(x^2\sin(1/x)\) gives \(\sum x_k^2<\infty\).

Riemann-Stieltjes integral

Let \(\alpha\) be increasing (or BV) on \([a,b]\). For a partition \(P\) and tags \(t_i\in[x_{i-1},x_i]\) form \(S=\sum f(t_i)\bigl(\alpha(x_i)-\alpha(x_{i-1})\bigr)\). If \(S\) tends to a limit as the partition is refined, that limit is \(\int_a^bf\,d\alpha\). For \(\alpha(x)=x\) this is the Riemann integral.

  • Existence. \(f\) continuous and \(\alpha\) BV \(\Rightarrow\) \(\int f\,d\alpha\) exists. If \(f\) and \(\alpha\) have a common point of discontinuity (on the same side) it fails.
  • Reduction. If \(\alpha\in C^1\) (or \(\alpha'\) Riemann integrable), \(\int f\,d\alpha=\int_a^bf(x)\alpha'(x)\,dx\). Example: \(\int_0^1x\,d(x^2)=\int_0^12x^2dx=\frac23\).
  • Step integrator. If \(\alpha\) is constant except for jumps \(c_k\) at points \(x_k\) and \(f\) is continuous there, \(\int f\,d\alpha=\sum c_kf(x_k)\). Example: \(\alpha(x)=\lfloor x\rfloor\) on \([0,3]\) has jumps \(1\) at \(1,2,3\), so \(\int_0^3x^2\,d\alpha=1+4+9=14\).
  • Properties. Linear in \(f\) and in \(\alpha\); \(\int_a^bd\alpha=\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)\); \(\bigl\lvert\int f\,d\alpha\bigr\rvert\le\sup\lvert f\rvert\,V_a^b(\alpha)\); integration by parts \(\int f\,d\alpha+\int\alpha\,df=f(b)\alpha(b)-f(a)\alpha(a)\) (if one side exists so does the other).
  • The total variation of \(\alpha\in C^1\) is \(\int\lvert\alpha'\rvert\); for \(\alpha=x^2\) on \([-1,1]\) it is \(\int_{-1}^1\lvert2x\rvert dx=2\), not \(\alpha(1)-\alpha(-1)=0\).

Fourier series

Let \(f\) be \(2\pi\)-periodic and integrable on \([-\pi,\pi]\). Its Fourier coefficients are

\[a_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\cos nx\,dx\ (n\ge0),\qquad b_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\sin nx\,dx\ (n\ge1),\]

and the Fourier series is \(\dfrac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n\ge1}(a_n\cos nx+b_n\sin nx)\). Note the \(\frac{a_0}2\) and the factor \(\frac1\pi\) for \(n=0\) too; the constant term of the series is the mean value of \(f\).

For period \(2L\), replace \(x\) by \(\frac{\pi x}{L}\): \(a_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^Lf(x)\cos\frac{n\pi x}L\,dx\), \(b_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^Lf(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}Ldx\).

Complex form: \(f\sim\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}c_ne^{inx}\) with \(c_n=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)e^{-inx}dx\), and \(c_n=\frac{a_n-ib_n}{2}\), \(c_{-n}=\overline{c_n}\) for real \(f\).

Symmetry. If \(f\) is even then \(b_n=0\) and \(a_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f\cos nx\,dx\) (cosine series). If \(f\) is odd then \(a_n=0\) (including \(a_0\)) and \(b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f\sin nx\,dx\) (sine series).

Half-range series. For \(f\) given on \((0,L)\): extend evenly to get the cosine series \(\frac{a_0}2+\sum a_n\cos\frac{n\pi x}L\) with \(a_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf\cos\frac{n\pi x}Ldx\), or extend oddly to get the sine series \(\sum b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}L\) with \(b_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf\sin\frac{n\pi x}Ldx\). The two series converge to different functions outside \((0,L)\) but agree with \(f\) on \((0,L)\) at continuity points.

Convergence

Dirichlet’s conditions. If \(f\) is \(2\pi\)-periodic, bounded, has finitely many discontinuities and finitely many maxima and minima in a period (piecewise monotone), then the Fourier series converges at every \(x\) to

\[\frac{f(x^+)+f(x^-)}2,\]

which equals \(f(x)\) at a point of continuity. At a jump it gives the mean of the two one-sided limits, not \(f(x)\).

Related and stronger results:

  • Jordan’s test. If \(f\) is BV near \(x\), the series converges at \(x\) to \(\frac{f(x^+)+f(x^-)}2\). (BV near \(x\) is all that is needed, which is why BV and Fourier series appear in one topic.)
  • Dini’s test. Convergence at \(x\) if \(\int_0^\delta\frac{\lvert f(x+t)+f(x-t)-2f(x)\rvert}{t}dt<\infty\).
  • Uniform convergence. If \(f\) is continuous, \(2\pi\)-periodic and piecewise \(C^1\), the series converges uniformly (and absolutely). If \(f\) has a jump the convergence is never uniform near it (Gibbs phenomenon).
  • Continuous and BV. If \(f\) is continuous, \(2\pi\)-periodic and of bounded variation, the Fourier series converges uniformly to \(f\) (Jordan’s test strengthened by continuity; Lipschitz or Holder continuity also suffices). Piecewise \(C^1\) is a special case. Without BV or similar smoothness, continuity alone gives only the next fact.
  • Continuity alone is not enough for pointwise convergence everywhere: there are continuous periodic functions whose Fourier series diverge at some point (du Bois-Reymond).
  • Fejer’s theorem. For continuous periodic \(f\) the Cesaro means \(\sigma_N f\) of the partial sums converge uniformly to \(f\).
  • Riemann-Lebesgue lemma. If \(f\in L^1\), then \(a_n,b_n\to0\).

Gibbs phenomenon. Near a jump, the partial sums overshoot by about \(8.95\%\) of the jump size (for the square wave \(\pm1\) the maxima tend to \(\frac2\pi\mathrm{Si}(\pi)\approx1.179\)), however many terms are used.

Decay of coefficients (the 2026 question).

If \(f\) is… then the coefficients satisfy
Integrable (\(L^1\)) \(a_n,b_n\to0\) (Riemann-Lebesgue)
BV on \([-\pi,\pi]\) \(a_n,b_n=O(1/n)\)
In \(L^2\) \(\sum(a_n^2+b_n^2)<\infty\) (Parseval)
Continuous, piecewise \(C^1\) \(\sum(\lvert a_n\rvert+\lvert b_n\rvert)<\infty\), so uniform convergence
\(C^k\) and periodic \(a_n,b_n=o(n^{-k})\)

For BV \(\cap\ L^2\) the strongest general conclusions are \(\sum a_n^2<\infty\) and \(a_n=O(1/n)\). One cannot upgrade to \(\sum(\lvert a_n\rvert+\lvert b_n\rvert)<\infty\), to \(O(1/n^2)\) or to uniform convergence, because the sawtooth \(f(x)=x\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\) is BV with \(b_n=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}n\).

Bessel’s inequality and Parseval’s identity

If \(f\in L^2[-\pi,\pi]\) (for example \(f\) Riemann integrable):

  • Bessel. \(\dfrac{a_0^2}2+\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}(a_n^2+b_n^2)\le\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2\).
  • Parseval (completeness of the trigonometric system). Equality holds: \(\dfrac{a_0^2}2+\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}(a_n^2+b_n^2)=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2\).
  • Complex form: \(\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}\lvert c_n\rvert^2=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi\lvert f\rvert^2\).

Bessel’s inequality holds for any orthonormal system, with equality exactly when the system is complete.

Term-by-term operations. A Fourier series may always be integrated term by term (the integrated series converges even if the original does not). It may be differentiated term by term if \(f\) is continuous and periodic with \(f'\) piecewise continuous.

Standard series (used to sum numerical series)

All on \((-\pi,\pi)\) extended with period \(2\pi\).

\(f(x)\) Series Values at special points
\(x\) \(2\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n\sin nx\) at \(\frac\pi2\): \(\frac\pi4=1-\frac13+\frac15-\dots\)
\(x^2\) \(\frac{\pi^2}3+4\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}\cos nx\) at \(0\): \(\sum\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{12}\); at \(\pi\): \(\sum\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}6\)
\(\lvert x\rvert\) \(\frac\pi2-\frac4\pi\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac{\cos nx}{n^2}\) at \(0\): \(\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}8\)
square wave: \(1\) on \((0,\pi)\), \(-1\) on \((-\pi,0)\) \(\frac4\pi\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac{\sin nx}n\) at \(\frac\pi2\): \(\frac\pi4\)
\(\frac{\pi-x}2\) on \((0,2\pi)\) \(\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\sin nx}n\) value \(0\) at the jump \(x=0\)
\(\lvert\sin x\rvert\) \(\frac2\pi-\frac4\pi\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\cos2nx}{4n^2-1}\) \(\sum\frac1{4n^2-1}=\frac12\)

Two more expansions. On \((-\pi,\pi)\) and for real \(a\ne0\),

\[e^{ax}=\frac{\sinh a\pi}{\pi}\left[\frac1a+2\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^n}{a^2+n^2}\bigl(a\cos nx-n\sin nx\bigr)\right],\]

and putting \(x=\pi\) gives \(\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n^2+a^2}=\frac{\pi}{2a}\coth a\pi-\frac1{2a^2}\) (letting \(a\to0\) recovers \(\frac{\pi^2}6\)). The sine series of \(x(\pi-x)\) on \((0,\pi)\) is \(\frac8\pi\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac{\sin nx}{n^3}\), and \(x=\frac\pi2\) gives \(\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)^3}=\frac{\pi^3}{32}\).

Parseval gives: from \(f=x\): \(\sum\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}6\); from \(f=x^2\): \(\sum\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{90}\); hence \(\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{96}\).

Formulas and results

  • \(V_a^b(f)=\sup_P\sum\lvert f(x_i)-f(x_{i-1})\rvert\); monotone: \(\lvert f(b)-f(a)\rvert\); \(C^1\): \(\int_a^b\lvert f'\rvert\).
  • BV \(\Rightarrow\) bounded, countably many discontinuities, a.e. differentiable, Riemann integrable. \(f\) BV iff \(f=\) increasing \(-\) increasing.
  • \(a_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f\cos nx\), \(b_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f\sin nx\); series starts with \(\frac{a_0}2\).
  • Even \(\Rightarrow b_n=0\); odd \(\Rightarrow a_n=0\). Half-range: \(\frac2L\int_0^L\).
  • Dirichlet: converges to \(\frac{f(x^+)+f(x^-)}{2}\).
  • Bessel: \(\frac{a_0^2}2+\sum(a_n^2+b_n^2)\le\frac1\pi\int f^2\); Parseval: equality.
  • \(\sum\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}6\), \(\sum\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{12}\), \(\sum_{\text{odd}}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}8\), \(\sum\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{90}\), \(\sum_{\text{odd}}\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{96}\).
  • Gibbs overshoot \(\approx8.95\%\) of the jump. BV gives \(a_n=O(1/n)\); \(L^2\) gives \(\sum a_n^2<\infty\).

Worked examples

Example 1: total variation

Find the total variation of \(f(x)=x^3-3x\) on \([-2,2]\).

\(f'(x)=3x^2-3=0\) at \(x=\pm1\). Values: \(f(-2)=-2\), \(f(-1)=2\), \(f(1)=-2\), \(f(2)=2\). \(f\) is monotone on \([-2,-1]\), \([-1,1]\), \([1,2]\), so

\[V=\lvert2-(-2)\rvert+\lvert-2-2\rvert+\lvert2-(-2)\rvert=4+4+4=12.\]

(Check: \(\int_{-2}^2\lvert3x^2-3\rvert dx=12\).)

Example 2: variation of a step-like function

Total variation of \(f(x)=\lfloor x\rfloor\) on \([0,2.5]\): it jumps by \(1\) at \(x=1\) and at \(x=2\) and is constant elsewhere, so \(V=2\).

Example 3: Fourier series of \(x^2\) and two sums

On \((-\pi,\pi)\), \(f(x)=x^2\) is even, so \(b_n=0\). \(a_0=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi x^2dx=\frac{2\pi^2}3\) and, integrating by parts twice, \(a_n=\frac{4(-1)^n}{n^2}\). Hence

\[x^2=\frac{\pi^2}3+4\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}\cos nx.\]

At \(x=0\) (continuity point): \(0=\frac{\pi^2}3+4\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}\), so \(\sum\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{12}\). At \(x=\pi\): \(\pi^2=\frac{\pi^2}3+4\sum\frac1{n^2}\), so \(\sum\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}6\).

Parseval: \(\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi x^4dx=\frac{2\pi^4}5=\frac{(2\pi^2/3)^2}{2}+16\sum\frac1{n^4}\), so \(16\sum\frac1{n^4}=\frac{2\pi^4}5-\frac{2\pi^4}9=\frac{8\pi^4}{45}\), giving \(\sum\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{90}\).

Example 4: value at a jump

Let \(f(x)=0\) for \(-\pi<x<0\) and \(f(x)=x\) for \(0<x<\pi\). Then \(a_0=\frac\pi2\), \(a_n=\frac{(-1)^n-1}{\pi n^2}\), \(b_n=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n\).

By Dirichlet’s theorem the series converges to: at \(x=0\), \(\frac{0+0}2=0\); at \(x=\pi\), \(\frac{f(\pi^-)+f(-\pi^+)}2=\frac{\pi+0}2=\frac\pi2\).

From \(x=0\): \(0=\frac\pi4+\sum a_n=\frac\pi4-\frac2\pi\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac1{n^2}\), so \(\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}8\).

Example 5: half-range sine series

Expand \(f(x)=1\) on \((0,\pi)\) in a sine series. \(b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin nx\,dx=\frac{2(1-(-1)^n)}{n\pi}\), which is \(\frac4{n\pi}\) for odd \(n\) and \(0\) for even \(n\). So \(1=\frac4\pi\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac{\sin nx}n\) on \((0,\pi)\). At \(x=\frac\pi2\): \(1=\frac4\pi\left(1-\frac13+\frac15-\dots\right)\), i.e. \(\frac\pi4=1-\frac13+\frac15-\dots\). At \(x=0\) the series sums to \(0\), the mean of the odd extension’s one-sided limits \(\pm1\).

Example 6: \(x\sin(1/x)\) is not BV

Take \(x_k=\frac2{(2k+1)\pi}\), \(k=1,\dots,K\). Then \(f(x_k)=x_k\sin\frac{(2k+1)\pi}2=(-1)^kx_k\). The variation along these points is at least \(\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}(x_k+x_{k+1})\approx\frac4\pi\sum\frac1{2k+1}\), which tends to \(\infty\) with \(K\). So \(V_0^1=\infty\) even though \(f\) is continuous.

Common traps

  • Series value at a jump. It is the mean \(\frac{f(x^+)+f(x^-)}2\), not \(f(x)\), and at the end point \(\pm\pi\) it is \(\frac{f(\pi^-)+f(-\pi^+)}2\).
  • The \(\frac{a_0}2\) convention. Some books write \(a_0\) without the half. Check: the constant term of the series must equal the average of \(f\).
  • Odd functions have \(a_0=0\); even functions have \(b_n=0\).
  • Half-range extension. Cosine series uses the even extension, sine series the odd one. On \((0,\pi)\) both equal \(f\) at continuity points.
  • Continuous does not mean BV. \(x\sin(1/x)\). Also, BV does not mean continuous.
  • Differentiable everywhere does not mean BV. \(x^2\sin(1/x^2)\). A bounded derivative does.
  • Bounded does not mean BV. The Dirichlet function and \(\sin(1/x)\) are bounded and not BV.
  • BV \(\cap\ L^2\): \(O(1/n)\) and \(\sum a_n^2<\infty\) only. Absolute summability and uniform convergence need continuity and more smoothness.
  • Parseval normalisation. With the \(\frac{a_0}2\) convention the left side is \(\frac{a_0^2}2+\sum(a_n^2+b_n^2)\) and the right side is \(\frac1\pi\int f^2\), not \(\frac1{2\pi}\).
  • Odd versus all: \(\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{96}\) and \(\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{90}\).

If \(\alpha\) is a bounded function on \([a,b]\), which of the following is true? (a) \(\alpha\) is of bounded variation; (b) \(\alpha\) is continuous; (c) \(\alpha\) is constant; (d) \(\sup\lvert\alpha\rvert\) exists. Answer: (d). (From a re-typed copy in a solution video, not the official booklet; the presenter ticked (d).)

Solution: a bounded function has a finite supremum of its absolute value by definition. A bounded function need not be BV (\(\sin(1/x)\)), continuous (Dirichlet function), or constant.

2026, Q60

Let \(f\) be of bounded variation on \((-\pi,\pi)\) with Fourier coefficients \(a_n\), and suppose in addition \(f\in L^2\). Which is the strongest valid conclusion? (A) \(\sum\lvert a_n\rvert<\infty\); (B) \(\sum\lvert a_n\rvert^2<\infty\) and \(a_n=O(1/n)\); (C) \(a_n=O(1/n^2)\); (D) uniform convergence of the Fourier series. Answer: B (worked key, unofficial).

Solution: BV gives \(a_n=O(1/n)\); \(L^2\) gives Parseval, \(\sum\lvert a_n\rvert^2<\infty\). A, C and D fail for the Fourier coefficients \((a_n,b_n)\) of a suitable BV function. For \(f(x)=x\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\) (all \(a_n=0\), \(b_n=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}n\), a jump at \(\pm\pi\)) the sine coefficients are not absolutely summable, not \(O(1/n^2)\), and the series is not uniformly convergent. A cosine-side example is \(f=\operatorname{sgn}(\cos x)\), where \(a_n=\frac4{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi}2\) is of exact order \(\frac1n\) for odd \(n\). (Reading \(a_n\) alone, \(f=x\) would not refute A or C, since all its \(a_n\) vanish.)

Quick revision

  • BV: \(\sup_P\sum\lvert\Delta f\rvert<\infty\); monotone gives \(\lvert f(b)-f(a)\rvert\); \(C^1\) gives \(\int\lvert f'\rvert\).
  • BV \(\Rightarrow\) bounded; bounded \(\not\Rightarrow\) BV. Jordan: BV \(=\) increasing \(-\) increasing.
  • \(x\sin(1/x)\): continuous, not BV. \(x^2\sin(1/x)\): BV, differentiable. \(x^2\sin(1/x^2)\): differentiable, not BV.
  • BV functions: countably many discontinuities, differentiable a.e., Riemann integrable.
  • \(a_n,b_n\) use \(\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi\); series starts at \(\frac{a_0}2\); even gives cosines, odd gives sines.
  • Dirichlet: converges to \(\frac{f(x^+)+f(x^-)}2\); Jordan: BV near \(x\) suffices.
  • Continuous and piecewise smooth gives uniform convergence; continuity alone is not enough pointwise.
  • Bessel \(\le\), Parseval \(=\): \(\frac{a_0^2}2+\sum(a_n^2+b_n^2)=\frac1\pi\int f^2\).
  • \(\sum\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}6\), \(\sum_{\text{odd}}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}8\), \(\sum\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{12}\), \(\sum\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{90}\).
  • Gibbs: about \(9\%\) overshoot at a jump. BV gives \(O(1/n)\); \(L^2\) gives \(\ell^2\) coefficients.
