Why it matters for the exam

Functional analysis is the most reliably examined part of this unit: the recovered 2019 paper had questions on equivalent norms, the norm of the identity operator and the null space of a bounded operator, and the 2025 paper’s hierarchy question (next topic) sits on the same ground. Expect statement-type questions where the options differ by one hypothesis: which space must be complete in the open mapping theorem, what the dual of \(\ell^p\) is, which spaces are Banach.

Core ideas

Norms

A norm on a vector space \(X\) (over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\)) is \(\lVert\cdot\rVert:X\to[0,\infty)\) with

  • \(\lVert x\rVert=0\iff x=0\);
  • \(\lVert\alpha x\rVert=\lvert\alpha\rvert\lVert x\rVert\);
  • \(\lVert x+y\rVert\le\lVert x\rVert+\lVert y\rVert\).

It gives the metric \(d(x,y)=\lVert x-y\rVert\), which is translation invariant and homogeneous. The norm is continuous: \(\lvert\lVert x\rVert-\lVert y\rVert\rvert\le\lVert x-y\rVert\). Addition and scalar multiplication are continuous. If the first axiom is weakened to \(\lVert x\rVert\ge0\) it is a seminorm.

Space Norm
\(\mathbb{R}^n,\mathbb{C}^n\) \(\lVert x\rVert_p=(\sum\lvert x_i\rvert^p)^{1/p}\) (\(1\le p<\infty\)), \(\lVert x\rVert_\infty=\max\lvert x_i\rvert\)
\(\ell^p\) (\(1\le p<\infty\)) \((\sum_{i\ge1}\lvert x_i\rvert^p)^{1/p}\)
\(\ell^\infty\) \(\sup_i\lvert x_i\rvert\) (bounded sequences)
\(c_0\), \(c\), \(c_{00}\) sup norm; null sequences, convergent sequences, finitely supported sequences
\(C[a,b]\) \(\lVert f\rVert_\infty=\max\lvert f\rvert\), or \(\lVert f\rVert_1=\int\lvert f\rvert\), or \(\lVert f\rVert_2=(\int\lvert f\rvert^2)^{1/2}\)
\(C^1[a,b]\) \(\lVert f\rVert_\infty+\lVert f'\rVert_\infty\)
\(L^p[a,b]\) \((\int\lvert f\rvert^p)^{1/p}\) (classes of a.e. equal functions)

For \(0<p<1\), \((\sum\lvert x_i\rvert^p)^{1/p}\) is not a norm (the triangle inequality fails; the unit ball is not convex).

Hölder: \(\sum\lvert x_iy_i\rvert\le\lVert x\rVert_p\lVert y\rVert_q\) with \(\frac1p+\frac1q=1\). Minkowski: the triangle inequality for \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_p\).

Comparison of \(p\)-norms:

  • In \(\mathbb{R}^n\): \(\lVert x\rVert_\infty\le\lVert x\rVert_2\le\lVert x\rVert_1\le\sqrt n\lVert x\rVert_2\le n\lVert x\rVert_\infty\).
  • Sequences: \(\ell^1\subsetneq\ell^p\subsetneq\ell^q\subsetneq\ell^\infty\) for \(1<p<q<\infty\), and \(\lVert x\rVert_q\le\lVert x\rVert_p\) for \(p\le q\). Example: \(x_n=n^{-0.4}\) lies in \(\ell^3\) but not in \(\ell^2\).
  • \(L^p\) on a finite measure space: \(L^q\subseteq L^p\) for \(p<q\) (reverse of sequences). On \(\mathbb{R}\) there is no inclusion.

Equivalent norms

Norms \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_a\) and \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_b\) on \(X\) are equivalent if there are constants \(\alpha,\beta>0\) with \(\alpha\lVert x\rVert_a\le\lVert x\rVert_b\le\beta\lVert x\rVert_a\quad\text{for all }x.\) Both inequalities are needed (2019 asked exactly this).

Equivalent norms give the same open sets, the same convergent and Cauchy sequences and the same bounded sets; one is complete iff the other is. Equivalence is an equivalence relation.

  • Finite dimension: all norms on a finite-dimensional space are equivalent. Proof idea: compare with \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_1\); the unit sphere is compact and a norm is continuous, so it has a positive minimum and a finite maximum on it.
  • Infinite dimension: not so. On \(C[0,1]\), \(\lVert f\rVert_1\le\lVert f\rVert_\infty\) but \(f_n=t^n\) has \(\lVert f_n\rVert_\infty=1\), \(\lVert f_n\rVert_1=\frac1{n+1}\), so no \(\beta\) works the other way: the two norms are not equivalent.
  • If \(X\) is complete under two norms and \(\lVert x\rVert_a\le C\lVert x\rVert_b\) for all \(x\), then the norms are equivalent (a consequence of the open mapping theorem).

Banach spaces

A Banach space is a complete normed space.

Banach:

  • every finite-dimensional normed space (\(\mathbb{R}^n\) with any norm);
  • \(\ell^p\) for \(1\le p\le\infty\); \(c_0\) and \(c\) (closed subspaces of \(\ell^\infty\));
  • \(C[a,b]\) and \(B(S)\) with the sup norm; \(C^1[a,b]\) with \(\lVert f\rVert_\infty+\lVert f'\rVert_\infty\);
  • \(L^p\) for \(1\le p\le\infty\) (Riesz-Fischer);
  • \(X^*\) for every normed \(X\); \(B(X,Y)\) whenever \(Y\) is Banach;
  • a closed subspace of a Banach space; \(X/M\) with \(M\) closed.

Not Banach (incomplete):

Space Reason
\(c_{00}\) with any \(\ell^p\) norm \((1,\tfrac12,\dots,\tfrac1n,0,\dots)\) is Cauchy in \(\ell^2\) with limit outside \(c_{00}\)
polynomials on \([0,1]\) with sup norm Taylor polynomials of \(e^x\) converge to a non-polynomial
\(C[0,1]\) with \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_1\) or \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_2\) continuous functions converge to a step function
\(C^1[0,1]\) with sup norm \(\sqrt{(t-\frac12)^2+\frac1{n}}\) converges uniformly to \(\lvert t-\frac12\rvert\), which is not \(C^1\)
\(\ell^p\), \(0<p<1\) not a normed space at all

A normed space is Banach iff every absolutely convergent series converges (\(\sum\lVert x_n\rVert<\infty\Rightarrow\sum x_n\) converges). Every normed space has a completion (a Banach space containing it densely).

Bounded linear operators and the operator norm

\(T:X\to Y\) linear is bounded if there is \(M\) with \(\lVert Tx\rVert\le M\lVert x\rVert\) for all \(x\). For a linear map the following are equivalent: bounded; continuous; continuous at \(0\); uniformly continuous; Lipschitz; maps bounded sets to bounded sets.

\(\lVert T\rVert=\sup_{\lVert x\rVert\le1}\lVert Tx\rVert=\sup_{\lVert x\rVert=1}\lVert Tx\rVert=\sup_{x\ne0}\frac{\lVert Tx\rVert}{\lVert x\rVert}=\inf\{M:\lVert Tx\rVert\le M\lVert x\rVert\ \forall x\}.\)

Then \(\lVert Tx\rVert\le\lVert T\rVert\lVert x\rVert\), \(\lVert ST\rVert\le\lVert S\rVert\lVert T\rVert\) and \(\lVert T^n\rVert\le\lVert T\rVert^n\).

  • Identity on \(X\ne\{0\}\): \(\lVert I\rVert=1\) (2019). The zero operator has norm \(0\).
  • Finite-dimensional domain: every linear map from a finite-dimensional normed space is bounded.
  • Infinite dimension: unbounded linear maps exist. Differentiation \(D:(\text{polynomials},\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty)\to(\text{polynomials},\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty)\) on \([0,1]\) has \(\lVert t^n\rVert=1\), \(\lVert Dt^n\rVert=n\), so \(D\) is unbounded.
  • \(B(X,Y)\) with the operator norm is a normed space, and is Banach if \(Y\) is.

Standard operator norms:

Operator Norm
diagonal \(\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_n)\) on \(\ell^p\) \(\sup\lvert\lambda_n\rvert\)
right or left shift on \(\ell^p\) \(1\)
multiplication by \(t\) on \(C[0,1]\) (sup) or \(L^2[0,1]\) \(1\)
integration \((Vf)(x)=\int_0^xf\) on \(C[0,1]\) with sup norm \(1\)
integration \(V\) on \(L^2[0,1]\) \(2/\pi\)
matrix \(A\) on \((\mathbb{R}^n,\lVert\cdot\rVert_1)\) maximum absolute column sum
matrix \(A\) on \((\mathbb{R}^n,\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty)\) maximum absolute row sum
matrix \(A\) on \((\mathbb{R}^n,\lVert\cdot\rVert_2)\) \(\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(A^{\mathsf T}A)}\) (largest singular value)

For the functional \(\varphi_a(x)=\sum a_ix_i\): on \(\ell^p\) its norm is \(\lVert a\rVert_q\) with \(\frac1p+\frac1q=1\) (so on \(\ell^1\) it is \(\sup\lvert a_i\rvert\), on \(\ell^\infty\) it is \(\sum\lvert a_i\rvert\)). The evaluation \(f\mapsto f(t_0)\) on \(C[a,b]\) has norm \(1\); \(f\mapsto\int_0^1f\) on \(C[0,1]\) with sup norm has norm \(1\).

Null space and range

For a bounded \(T:X\to Y\), the null space \(N(T)=T^{-1}(\{0\})\) is a closed subspace of \(X\) (preimage of a closed set under a continuous map; 2019 asked this). It is not necessarily \(\{0\}\), open or all of \(X\).

  • The range \(R(T)\) need not be closed: \(T=\operatorname{diag}(1,\frac12,\frac13,\dots)\) on \(\ell^2\) has \(R(T)\) dense but \(\ne\ell^2\).
  • A linear functional is bounded iff its kernel is closed. An unbounded linear functional has a dense kernel.
  • The null space of an unbounded operator need not be closed.

Finite-dimensional facts

  • All norms on a finite-dimensional space are equivalent; the space is complete; every linear operator out of it is bounded.
  • Every finite-dimensional subspace of a normed space is closed.
  • Riesz’s lemma: if \(M\) is a proper closed subspace of a normed space \(X\) and \(0<\theta<1\), there is a unit vector \(x\) with \(d(x,M)\ge\theta\).
  • Consequence: the closed unit ball of a normed space is compact iff \(X\) is finite-dimensional. So Heine-Borel (closed and bounded implies compact) characterises finite dimension.
  • \(\dim X^*=\dim X\); \(X\) is reflexive; finite-rank operators are compact.
  • An infinite-dimensional Banach space has no countable Hamel basis (Baire).

Hahn-Banach theorem

Analytic form. Let \(X\) be a real vector space, \(p:X\to\mathbb{R}\) sublinear (\(p(x+y)\le p(x)+p(y)\) and \(p(\lambda x)=\lambda p(x)\) for \(\lambda\ge0\)), \(M\) a subspace and \(f:M\to\mathbb{R}\) linear with \(f\le p\) on \(M\). Then there is a linear \(F:X\to\mathbb{R}\) with \(F\vert_M=f\) and \(F\le p\) on \(X\). (Complex version: \(p\) a seminorm and \(\lvert f\rvert\le p\).)

Normed form. Let \(X\) be a normed space over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\), \(M\) a subspace and \(f\in M^*\). Then there is \(F\in X^*\) with \(F\vert_M=f\) and \(\lVert F\rVert=\lVert f\rVert\).

No completeness is required, and the extension need not be unique.

Corollaries:

  • For every \(x_0\ne0\) there is \(f\in X^*\) with \(\lVert f\rVert=1\) and \(f(x_0)=\lVert x_0\rVert\). Hence \(X^*\) separates points of \(X\), and \(\lVert x\rVert=\max_{\lVert f\rVert\le1}\lvert f(x)\rvert\).
  • A subspace \(M\) is dense iff the only \(f\in X^*\) vanishing on \(M\) is \(0\). \(x_0\in\overline M\) iff every \(f\in X^*\) vanishing on \(M\) vanishes at \(x_0\).
  • \(d(x_0,M)=\max\{\lvert f(x_0)\rvert: f\in X^*,\ \lVert f\rVert\le1,\ f\vert_M=0\}\) for a closed subspace \(M\).
  • The canonical map \(J:X\to X^{**}\) is an isometric embedding. \(X^*\ne\{0\}\) whenever \(X\ne\{0\}\).

The three big theorems on Banach spaces

All three use the Baire category theorem.

Uniform boundedness principle (Banach-Steinhaus). Let \(X\) be a Banach space, \(Y\) a normed space and \(\{T_\alpha\}\subseteq B(X,Y)\). If \(\sup_\alpha\lVert T_\alpha x\rVert<\infty\) for every \(x\in X\) (pointwise bounded), then \(\sup_\alpha\lVert T_\alpha\rVert<\infty\). Consequences: if \(T_nx\to Tx\) for every \(x\) (\(X\) Banach) then \(T\) is bounded and \(\sup\lVert T_n\rVert<\infty\); a weakly convergent sequence is bounded; a pointwise convergent sequence of continuous linear functionals on a Banach space has a bounded limit functional. Hypothesis check: \(X\) must be complete (take \(c_{00}\) with sup norm and \(T_nx=nx_n\): pointwise bounded, not uniformly).

Open mapping theorem. If \(X,Y\) are Banach and \(T\in B(X,Y)\) is surjective then \(T\) is an open map (it sends open sets to open sets). Corollary (bounded inverse theorem): a bijective bounded operator between Banach spaces has a bounded inverse. Corollary: two complete norms with \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_a\le C\lVert\cdot\rVert_b\) are equivalent.

Closed graph theorem. If \(X,Y\) are Banach and \(T:X\to Y\) is linear and defined on all of \(X\) with a closed graph \(\{(x,Tx)\}\subseteq X\times Y\), then \(T\) is bounded. Closed graph means: \(x_n\to x\) and \(Tx_n\to y\) imply \(y=Tx\). Conversely, a bounded operator always has a closed graph.

Theorem Needs complete Needs
Hahn-Banach nothing \(M\) any subspace, \(f\) bounded on \(M\)
Uniform boundedness domain \(X\) family pointwise bounded; \(Y\) any normed space
Open mapping \(X\) and \(Y\) \(T\) bounded and onto
Closed graph \(X\) and \(Y\) \(T\) linear on all of \(X\), closed graph

Standard counterexamples when a hypothesis is dropped:

  • Identity \((C[0,1],\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty)\to(C[0,1],\lVert\cdot\rVert_1)\): bounded, bijective, but the inverse is unbounded. No contradiction: the target is not complete.
  • Differentiation \(D:C^1[0,1]\to C[0,1]\) with sup norm on both: closed graph, linear, but unbounded. No contradiction: the domain \((C^1,\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty)\) is not complete. Taking \(\lVert f\rVert_\infty+\lVert f'\rVert_\infty\) on \(C^1\) makes \(D\) bounded.
  • An unbounded linear functional on a Banach space (it exists via a Hamel basis, using the axiom of choice) has a dense kernel and a graph that is not closed. This is consistent with the closed graph theorem, which would otherwise force it to be bounded.

Dual spaces

\(X^*=B(X,\mathbb{F})\) is the dual space. It is always a Banach space, even if \(X\) is not.

\(X\) \(X^*\) Pairing
\(\mathbb{R}^n\) with \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_p\) \(\mathbb{R}^n\) with \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_q\), \(\frac1p+\frac1q=1\) \(\sum a_ix_i\)
\(\ell^p\), \(1<p<\infty\) \(\ell^q\), \(\frac1p+\frac1q=1\) \(\sum a_ix_i\)
\(\ell^1\) \(\ell^\infty\) \(\sum a_ix_i\)
\(c_0\) \(\ell^1\) \(\sum a_ix_i\)
\(c\) \(\ell^1\) \(a_0\lim x+\sum a_ix_i\)
\(\ell^\infty\) strictly larger than \(\ell^1\) (contains functionals vanishing on \(c_0\))
\(L^p\), \(1\le p<\infty\) \(L^q\) (\(\sigma\)-finite for \(p=1\)) \(\int fg\)
\(C[a,b]\) normalised functions of bounded variation, i.e. signed regular Borel measures (Riesz representation) \(\int f\,dg\)
Hilbert space \(H\) \(H\) (conjugate-linearly) \(\langle x,y\rangle\) (Riesz)

Proof for \((\ell^p)^*=\ell^q\) (\(1\le p<\infty\)). For \(a\in\ell^q\), \(\varphi_a(x)=\sum a_ix_i\) is bounded with \(\lVert\varphi_a\rVert\le\lVert a\rVert_q\) by Hölder; equality follows by testing \(x_i=\lvert a_i\rvert^{q-1}\operatorname{sgn}a_i\). Conversely, given \(\varphi\in(\ell^p)^*\) set \(a_i=\varphi(e_i)\); testing on truncated vectors shows \(a\in\ell^q\) with \(\lVert a\rVert_q\le\lVert\varphi\rVert\), and \(\varphi=\varphi_a\) on the dense set \(c_{00}\) (here \(p<\infty\) is needed). The map \(a\mapsto\varphi_a\) is an isometric isomorphism.

Reflexive means \(J:X\to X^{**}\) is onto. Reflexive: finite-dimensional spaces, \(\ell^p\) and \(L^p\) for \(1<p<\infty\), every Hilbert space. Not reflexive: \(\ell^1\), \(\ell^\infty\), \(c_0\), \(C[a,b]\), \(L^1\). A reflexive space is Banach; a closed subspace of a reflexive space is reflexive; \(X\) is reflexive iff \(X^*\) is.

Formulas and results

  • Norm axioms; \(d(x,y)=\lVert x-y\rVert\); the norm is continuous.
  • Equivalent: \(\alpha\lVert x\rVert_a\le\lVert x\rVert_b\le\beta\lVert x\rVert_a\). Finite dimension: all norms equivalent.
  • Banach \(=\) complete normed space; \(\ell^p,L^p,C[a,b]_{\sup},c_0,X^*\) are Banach; \(c_{00}\), polynomials, \(C[a,b]_{L^1/L^2}\) are not.
  • Operator norm \(\lVert T\rVert=\sup_{\lVert x\rVert=1}\lVert Tx\rVert\); \(\lVert I\rVert=1\); \(\lVert ST\rVert\le\lVert S\rVert\lVert T\rVert\).
  • Bounded \(\iff\) continuous \(\iff\) Lipschitz (for linear maps).
  • \(N(T)\) closed for bounded \(T\); range need not be closed.
  • Matrix norms: column sum (\(\ell^1\)), row sum (\(\ell^\infty\)), largest singular value (\(\ell^2\)).
  • \(\ell^1\subset\ell^p\subset\ell^q\subset\ell^\infty\) (\(p<q\)); on a finite measure space the inclusions reverse.
  • Hahn-Banach: extension with equal norm, no completeness needed.
  • Uniform boundedness: domain Banach; open mapping: both Banach, onto; closed graph: both Banach, linear, defined everywhere.
  • \((\ell^p)^*=\ell^q\) for \(1\le p<\infty\), \(\frac1p+\frac1q=1\); \((\ell^1)^*=\ell^\infty\); \(c_0^*=\ell^1\); \((\ell^\infty)^*\supsetneq\ell^1\).
  • Closed unit ball compact iff finite dimension.

Worked examples

Example 1: norm of a functional on different \(\ell^p\)

Let \(\varphi(x)=\sum_{i\ge1}x_i/2^i\), so \(a_i=2^{-i}\). Then:

  • on \(\ell^1\): \(\lVert\varphi\rVert=\sup\lvert a_i\rvert=\tfrac12\);
  • on \(\ell^2\) (\(q=2\)): \(\lVert\varphi\rVert=\big(\sum4^{-i}\big)^{1/2}=\sqrt{1/3}\approx0.577\);
  • on \(\ell^\infty\) (\(q=1\)): \(\lVert\varphi\rVert=\sum2^{-i}=1\).

Example 2: operator norm of a matrix in three norms

\(A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}\).

  • \(\lVert A\rVert_1\) is the maximum column sum: \(\max(1+3,\,2+4)=6\).
  • \(\lVert A\rVert_\infty\) is the maximum row sum: \(\max(1+2,\,3+4)=7\).
  • \(A^{\mathsf T}A=\begin{pmatrix}10&14\\14&20\end{pmatrix}\) has eigenvalues \(15\pm\sqrt{221}\), so \(\lVert A\rVert_2=\sqrt{15+\sqrt{221}}\approx5.465\).

Order check: \(\lVert A\rVert_2\le\sqrt{\lVert A\rVert_1\lVert A\rVert_\infty}=\sqrt{42}\approx6.48\).

Example 3: \(C[0,1]\) with \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_1\) is not complete

Let \(f_n(t)=0\) for \(t\le\frac12\), \(f_n(t)=n(t-\frac12)\) for \(\frac12\le t\le\frac12+\frac1n\), and \(f_n(t)=1\) afterwards. For \(m>n\), \(\int\lvert f_n-f_m\rvert\le\frac1n\), so \((f_n)\) is Cauchy in \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_1\). It converges in \(L^1\) to \(\chi_{(1/2,1]}\), which has no continuous representative; a continuous \(g\) with \(\lVert f_n-g\rVert_1\to0\) would equal \(0\) on \([0,\frac12)\) and \(1\) on \((\frac12,1]\). So the space is incomplete, while \((C[0,1],\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty)\) is complete.

Example 4: no inequality \(\lVert f\rVert_\infty\le\beta\lVert f\rVert_1\) on \(C[0,1]\)

With \(f_n=t^n\): \(\lVert f_n\rVert_\infty=1\) but \(\lVert f_n\rVert_1=\frac1{n+1}\to0\). Hence no constant \(\beta\) with \(\lVert f\rVert_\infty\le\beta\lVert f\rVert_1\) exists, the norms are not equivalent, and this does not contradict the open mapping theorem because \((C[0,1],\lVert\cdot\rVert_1)\) is not complete.

Example 5: why the closed graph theorem needs completeness of the domain

Let \(D:(C^1[0,1],\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty)\to(C[0,1],\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty)\), \(Df=f'\). If \(f_n\to f\) uniformly and \(f_n'\to g\) uniformly then \(f\) is \(C^1\) with \(f'=g\), so the graph is closed. But \(\lVert Dt^n\rVert=n\) while \(\lVert t^n\rVert=1\), so \(D\) is unbounded. The closed graph theorem is not violated since the domain is not complete.

Common traps

  • Equivalence of norms needs both inequalities with positive constants. One inequality is not enough.
  • Finite dimension: all norms equivalent. Infinite dimension: no (\(\lVert\cdot\rVert_\infty\) and \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_1\) on \(C[0,1]\)).
  • \(\ell^p\) is Banach for all \(1\le p\le\infty\), but \(\ell^p\) is a Hilbert space only for \(p=2\).
  • \(c_{00}\) is a normed space that is not Banach. \(C[0,1]\) is Banach in sup norm, not in \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_1\) or \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_2\).
  • Operator norm: \(\lVert I\rVert=1\), not \(0\) or \(\dim X\). Matrix \(\ell^1\)-norm is the column sum, the \(\ell^\infty\)-norm is the row sum.
  • \(N(T)\) is closed for bounded \(T\). It is not “open”, and not necessarily \(\{0\}\) or \(X\).
  • Open mapping needs \(T\) onto and both spaces Banach. A bounded one-to-one map need not have a bounded inverse.
  • Closed graph needs \(T\) defined on all of \(X\) and both spaces Banach. Bounded implies closed graph, not the other way without completeness.
  • Uniform boundedness: only the domain must be Banach; \(Y\) can be any normed space. Without completeness the principle fails.
  • Hahn-Banach needs no completeness, but the extension is not unique in general.
  • \((\ell^1)^*=\ell^\infty\) but \((\ell^\infty)^*\ne\ell^1\). \((\ell^p)^*=\ell^q\) requires \(p<\infty\).
  • Closed and bounded (Heine-Borel) implies compact only in finite dimensions; the unit ball of an infinite-dimensional space is never compact.
  • \(L^p\) on a finite measure space: \(L^q\subseteq L^p\) for \(p<q\), the opposite of \(\ell^p\).

Previous-year questions

These 2019 items come from a partial reconstruction of the 2019 paper (re-typed in a solution video); answers are the options ticked there and agree with the mathematics.

2019, unnumbered 5. Two norms \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_1,\lVert\cdot\rVert_2\) on a vector space are equivalent iff: (a) there exist \(\alpha,\beta>0\) with \(\alpha\lVert x\rVert_1\le\lVert x\rVert_2\le\beta\lVert x\rVert_1\) for all \(x\), and three other statements.

Answer: (a). This is the definition. A one-sided inequality or a single constant is not enough.

2019, unnumbered 8. The norm of the identity operator on a normed space \(X\ne\{0\}\) is: \(1\), \(0\), \(10\), \(2\).

Answer: \(1\). \(\lVert I\rVert=\sup_{x\ne0}\lVert x\rVert/\lVert x\rVert=1\).

2019, unnumbered 9. For a bounded linear operator \(T\) between normed spaces, its null space \(N(T)\) is: \(\{0\}\), open, all of \(X\), or closed?

Answer: closed. \(N(T)=T^{-1}(\{0\})\) is the preimage of the closed set \(\{0\}\) under the continuous map \(T\).

Quick revision

  • Norm: definiteness, homogeneity, triangle inequality; \(d=\lVert x-y\rVert\).
  • Equivalent norms: \(\alpha\lVert x\rVert_a\le\lVert x\rVert_b\le\beta\lVert x\rVert_a\); automatic in finite dimension.
  • Banach \(=\) complete normed: \(\ell^p\), \(L^p\), \(C[a,b]_{\sup}\), \(c_0\), every \(X^*\); not: \(c_{00}\), polynomials, \(C[a,b]_{L^1}\).
  • \(\lVert T\rVert=\sup_{\lVert x\rVert=1}\lVert Tx\rVert\); \(\lVert I\rVert=1\); bounded \(\iff\) continuous.
  • \(N(T)\) of a bounded operator is a closed subspace; the range need not be.
  • Finite dimension: all norms equivalent, complete, subspaces closed, linear maps bounded, unit ball compact (and conversely).
  • Hahn-Banach: norm-preserving extension of functionals; no completeness needed.
  • Uniform boundedness: \(X\) Banach and pointwise bounded family implies norm bounded.
  • Open mapping: \(X,Y\) Banach, \(T\) bounded onto implies \(T\) open; inverse theorem.
  • Closed graph: \(X,Y\) Banach, \(T\) linear everywhere defined, closed graph implies bounded.
  • \((\ell^p)^*=\ell^q\) (\(1\le p<\infty\)), \((\ell^1)^*=\ell^\infty\), \(c_0^*=\ell^1\), \((\ell^\infty)^*\supsetneq\ell^1\).
  • Reflexive: \(\ell^p,L^p\) (\(1<p<\infty\)), Hilbert, finite-dimensional; not \(\ell^1\), \(\ell^\infty\), \(c_0\), \(C[a,b]\).
