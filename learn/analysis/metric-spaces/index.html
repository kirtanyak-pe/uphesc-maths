Why it matters for the exam

Metric-space facts appear every year, usually as one-line theory questions: 2025 Q96 (totally bounded implies separable), 2026 Q54 (every sequence has a Cauchy subsequence), 2026 Q33 (uniform limit of uniformly continuous functions). The same vocabulary (complete, compact, Cauchy) is the base for the normed-space and Hilbert-space questions, and the Baire and contraction theorems are standard “which hypothesis is needed” items.

Core ideas

Metric, open and closed sets

A metric on \(X\) is \(d:X\times X\to[0,\infty)\) with \(d(x,y)=0\iff x=y\), \(d(x,y)=d(y,x)\) and \(d(x,z)\le d(x,y)+d(y,z)\).

Metric space \(d\)
\(\mathbb{R}^n\) \(d_p(x,y)=\big(\sum\lvert x_i-y_i\rvert^p\big)^{1/p}\) (\(p\ge1\)), \(d_\infty=\max\lvert x_i-y_i\rvert\)
any set discrete metric: \(d(x,y)=1\) for \(x\ne y\)
\(C[a,b]\) \(d_\infty(f,g)=\sup\lvert f-g\rvert\) (sup metric)
\(C[a,b]\) \(d_1(f,g)=\int_a^b\lvert f-g\rvert\)
\(\ell^p\) \(\big(\sum\lvert x_i-y_i\rvert^p\big)^{1/p}\)
\(\mathbb{R}\) \(d(x,y)=\lvert\arctan x-\arctan y\rvert\) (same topology as usual, but not complete)

If \(d\) is a metric then so is \(\min(d,1)\) and \(d/(1+d)\): they give the same open sets and the same Cauchy sequences, and they make every space bounded.

Open ball \(B(x,r)=\{y:d(x,y)<r\}\). A set \(U\) is open if every point has a ball inside \(U\). A set is closed if its complement is open.

  • Arbitrary unions and finite intersections of open sets are open. Dually for closed sets: arbitrary intersections and finite unions.
  • \(\varnothing\) and \(X\) are both open and closed. In a discrete metric space every subset is open and closed.
  • \(\{x\}\) is closed in every metric space (every metric space is \(T_1\), in fact \(T_4\)).
  • Subspace \(Y\subseteq X\): \(V\subseteq Y\) is open in \(Y\) iff \(V=U\cap Y\) with \(U\) open in \(X\). A subset can be closed and open in \(Y\) without being so in \(X\) (for example \(\{q\in\mathbb Q:q^2<2\}\) is open and closed in \(\mathbb Q\), because it equals both \((-\sqrt2,\sqrt2)\cap\mathbb Q\) and \([-\sqrt2,\sqrt2]\cap\mathbb Q\), but it is neither open nor closed in \(\mathbb R\)).

Closure, interior, boundary, dense sets

For \(A\subseteq X\):

  • Limit point \(x\) of \(A\): every ball around \(x\) contains a point of \(A\) other than \(x\).
  • Closure \(\overline{A}=A\cup A'\) (\(A'\) is the set of limit points) \(=\) smallest closed set containing \(A\) \(=\) set of limits of sequences in \(A\) \(=\{x:B(x,r)\cap A\ne\varnothing\ \forall r\}\).
  • Interior \(A^\circ=\) largest open subset of \(A\) \(=\{x:B(x,r)\subseteq A\text{ for some }r\}\).
  • Boundary \(\partial A=\overline{A}\setminus A^\circ\).
  • \(\overline{A^c}=(A^\circ)^c\) and \((\overline{A})^c=(A^c)^\circ\).
  • \(A\) is dense if \(\overline{A}=X\). \(A\) is nowhere dense if \((\overline{A})^\circ=\varnothing\). \(X\) is separable if it has a countable dense set.

Standard facts in \(\mathbb{R}\):

\(A\) \(\overline{A}\) \(A^\circ\) \(\partial A\)
\(\mathbb{Q}\) \(\mathbb{R}\) \(\varnothing\) \(\mathbb{R}\)
\((a,b)\) \([a,b]\) \((a,b)\) \(\{a,b\}\)
\(\{1/n:n\ge1\}\) \(\{0\}\cup\{1/n\}\) \(\varnothing\) \(\{0\}\cup\{1/n\}\)
Cantor set itself \(\varnothing\) itself

Traps: in a general metric space \(\overline{B(x,r)}\) can be smaller than the closed ball \(\{y:d(x,y)\le r\}\) (discrete metric: \(\overline{B(x,1)}=\{x\}\) but the closed ball of radius \(1\) is all of \(X\)). \(\overline{A\cup B}=\overline{A}\cup\overline{B}\) but \(\overline{A\cap B}\subseteq\overline{A}\cap\overline{B}\) may be strict (\(A=(0,1)\), \(B=(1,2)\)). Separable spaces: \(\mathbb{R}^n\), \(C[a,b]\) (polynomials with rational coefficients), \(\ell^p\) for \(p<\infty\). Not separable: \(\ell^\infty\), and an uncountable discrete space.

Convergence and Cauchy sequences

\(x_n\to x\) iff \(d(x_n,x)\to0\). Limits are unique. A set \(F\) is closed iff it contains the limits of all convergent sequences in \(F\).

A sequence is Cauchy if \(d(x_n,x_m)\to0\) as \(n,m\to\infty\).

  • Convergent \(\Rightarrow\) Cauchy. The converse is false in general.
  • A Cauchy sequence is bounded.
  • A Cauchy sequence with a convergent subsequence converges.
  • Equivalent metrics (same topology) need not have the same Cauchy sequences.

Completeness

\(X\) is complete if every Cauchy sequence in \(X\) converges in \(X\).

Complete: \(\mathbb{R}\), \(\mathbb{R}^n\), \(\mathbb{C}\); discrete spaces; \(C[a,b]\) with the sup metric (uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous); \(\ell^p\) (\(1\le p\le\infty\)); \(c_0\), \(c\); the space \(B(S)\) of bounded functions with the sup metric; \(L^p\).

Incomplete:

Space Cauchy sequence with no limit in the space
\(\mathbb{Q}\) \(x_n=\sum_{k\le n}1/k!\to e\notin\mathbb{Q}\) (or \(1.4,1.41,1.414,\dots\to\sqrt2\))
\((0,1)\) with usual metric \(x_n=1/n\)
\(\mathbb{R}\) with \(\lvert\arctan x-\arctan y\rvert\) \(x_n=n\)
\(C[0,1]\) with \(d_1(f,g)=\int\lvert f-g\rvert\) continuous \(f_n\) rising from \(0\) to \(1\) on \([\tfrac12,\tfrac12+\tfrac1n]\) converge to a step function
polynomials with the sup metric Taylor polynomials of \(e^x\)
\(c_{00}\) (finitely supported sequences) in \(\ell^p\) \((1,\tfrac12,\dots,\tfrac1n,0,\dots)\) in \(\ell^2\)

Theorems:

  • A subspace of a complete space is complete iff it is closed.
  • Completeness is not a topological property: \((0,1)\) and \(\mathbb{R}\) are homeomorphic but only \(\mathbb{R}\) is complete. It is preserved by uniformly equivalent metrics.
  • Cantor intersection theorem. \(X\) is complete iff every decreasing sequence of nonempty closed sets with \(\operatorname{diam}\to0\) has a one-point intersection. (Without \(\operatorname{diam}\to0\), or for incomplete \(X\), it fails: \([n,\infty)\).)
  • Every metric space has a completion: a complete space containing it as a dense subspace, unique up to isometry (\(\mathbb{R}\) for \(\mathbb{Q}\)).

Continuity and uniform continuity

\(f:X\to Y\) is continuous at \(a\) if \(\forall\varepsilon\ \exists\delta:\ d(x,a)<\delta\Rightarrow d(f(x),f(a))<\varepsilon\). Equivalent on all of \(X\): preimages of open sets are open; preimages of closed sets are closed; \(x_n\to x\Rightarrow f(x_n)\to f(x)\). Compositions of continuous maps are continuous.

\(f\) is uniformly continuous if \(\delta\) can be chosen independent of \(a\): \(\forall\varepsilon\ \exists\delta\ \ \forall x,y:\ d(x,y)<\delta\Rightarrow d(f(x),f(y))<\varepsilon.\)

Hierarchy: Lipschitz (\(d(f(x),f(y))\le Kd(x,y)\)) \(\Rightarrow\) uniformly continuous \(\Rightarrow\) continuous. None of the arrows reverses: \(\sqrt{x}\) on \([0,1]\) is uniformly continuous but not Lipschitz; \(x^2\) on \(\mathbb{R}\) is continuous but not uniformly continuous.

Function Domain Uniformly continuous?
\(x^2\) \(\mathbb{R}\) No (\(x_n=n+\frac1n\), \(y_n=n\): \(\lvert x_n-y_n\rvert\to0\), \(\lvert x_n^2-y_n^2\rvert\to2\))
\(x^2\) \([0,M]\) Yes (compact)
\(1/x\) \((0,1)\) No (\(x_n=\frac1n\), \(y_n=\frac1{n+1}\))
\(\sin(1/x)\) \((0,1)\) No
\(\sqrt{x}\) \([0,\infty)\) Yes, since \(\lvert\sqrt x-\sqrt y\rvert\le\sqrt{\lvert x-y\rvert}\)
\(\sin x\), \(\cos x\) \(\mathbb{R}\) Yes (Lipschitz with \(K=1\))
\(x\sin x\) \(\mathbb{R}\) No
\(\arctan x\) \(\mathbb{R}\) Yes (Lipschitz, \(\lvert f'\rvert\le1\))

Key theorems:

  • Compact domain: continuous \(\Rightarrow\) uniformly continuous (Heine-Cantor).
  • A uniformly continuous map sends Cauchy sequences to Cauchy sequences; a continuous map need not (\(1/x\) on \((0,1)\) sends \(1/n\) to \(n\)). Hence a uniformly continuous map on a dense subset of \(X\) into a complete \(Y\) extends uniquely to a uniformly continuous map on \(X\).
  • A differentiable \(f\) on an interval with \(f'\) bounded is Lipschitz (mean value theorem), hence uniformly continuous. \(f(x)=\sqrt x\) on \([0,1]\) has unbounded \(f'\) but is uniformly continuous anyway: bounded derivative is sufficient, not necessary.
  • Uniform limits (2026 Q33): if \(f_n\to f\) uniformly and each \(f_n\) is continuous then \(f\) is continuous; if each \(f_n\) is uniformly continuous then \(f\) is uniformly continuous. Proof (\(3\varepsilon\)): choose \(n\) with \(\sup\lvert f_n-f\rvert<\varepsilon/3\) and \(\delta\) for \(f_n\); then \(d(f(x),f(y))\le\frac\varepsilon3+\varepsilon/3+\frac\varepsilon3\). This holds on any metric space \(X\). The limit need not be Lipschitz.
  • Pointwise limits of continuous functions need not be continuous (\(x^n\) on \([0,1]\)).

A homeomorphism is a continuous bijection with continuous inverse; an isometry preserves \(d\). Homeomorphisms preserve open sets, compactness, connectedness, but not completeness, boundedness or Cauchy sequences.

Compactness

\(K\) is compact if every open cover has a finite subcover.

Heine-Borel. In \(\mathbb{R}^n\) (with the Euclidean or any equivalent norm), \(K\) is compact iff \(K\) is closed and bounded. This is special to finite dimensions. It fails in general:

  • the closed unit ball of \(\ell^2\) (or of \(C[0,1]\)): closed and bounded, not compact (\(\lVert e_n-e_m\rVert=\sqrt2\) for \(\ell^2\), no convergent subsequence);
  • an infinite discrete space with \(d\le1\);
  • \([0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}\) is closed and bounded in \(\mathbb{Q}\) but not compact.

Theorem (metric spaces). For a metric space \(X\) the following are equivalent:

  1. \(X\) is compact;
  2. \(X\) is sequentially compact (every sequence has a convergent subsequence);
  3. every infinite subset has a limit point;
  4. \(X\) is complete and totally bounded.

\(X\) is totally bounded if for every \(\varepsilon>0\) it is covered by finitely many balls of radius \(\varepsilon\).

Total boundedness:

  • Totally bounded \(\Rightarrow\) bounded. The converse fails: infinite discrete spaces, the unit ball of \(\ell^2\), \(\{e_n\}\) in \(\ell^\infty\) (mutual distance \(1\)).
  • Totally bounded \(\Rightarrow\) separable (2025 Q96; the centres of the \(1/k\)-nets form a countable dense set).
  • A subset of a totally bounded space is totally bounded; so is its closure.
  • \(X\) is totally bounded iff every sequence in \(X\) has a Cauchy subsequence (2026 Q54). It does not force completeness or compactness: \((0,1)\) is totally bounded, and the sequence \(1/n\) has a Cauchy subsequence that does not converge in \((0,1)\).
  • Complete + totally bounded = compact. Neither alone is enough: \((0,1)\) is totally bounded but not complete; \(\mathbb{R}\) is complete but not totally bounded.
  • A metric space is totally bounded iff its completion is compact.

Properties of compact sets in a metric space:

  • Compact \(\Rightarrow\) closed, bounded, complete, totally bounded, separable.
  • Closed subset of a compact set is compact.
  • Continuous image of a compact set is compact. A continuous real function on a compact space is bounded and attains its maximum and minimum.
  • Continuous bijection from a compact space to a metric (Hausdorff) space is a homeomorphism.
  • Lebesgue number lemma: every open cover of a compact metric space has a \(\lambda>0\) such that every set of diameter \(<\lambda\) lies in one member.
  • Disjoint compact \(K\) and closed \(F\) have \(d(K,F)>0\). For two disjoint closed sets this can fail: \(\{n\}\) and \(\{n+\frac1n\}\) in \(\mathbb{R}\) (\(n\ge2\)).
  • Finite products of compact spaces are compact (Tychonoff for arbitrary products).
  • Arzelà-Ascoli. For compact \(X\), a subset of \(C(X)\) (sup metric) is relatively compact iff it is bounded and equicontinuous.
  • A discrete metric space is compact iff it is finite.

Connectedness

\(X\) is connected if it cannot be written as a union of two disjoint nonempty open sets.

  • A subset of \(\mathbb{R}\) is connected iff it is an interval (this includes points and rays).
  • The continuous image of a connected set is connected. (Intermediate value theorem.)
  • The closure of a connected set is connected. A union of connected sets with a common point (or a chain of overlapping sets) is connected. A product of connected spaces is connected.
  • Path connected \(\Rightarrow\) connected. The converse is false: the topologist’s sine curve \(\{(x,\sin\frac1x):x>0\}\cup(\{0\}\times[-1,1])\) is connected but not path connected. For open subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) the two notions agree.
  • Convex subsets of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) are path connected.
  • Connected components partition \(X\) into closed sets. In a discrete space the components are the singletons. \(\mathbb{Q}\) and the Cantor set are totally disconnected (components are points), yet neither is discrete.
  • \(\mathbb{R}^n\setminus\{pt\}\) is connected for \(n\ge2\) but \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\) is not. \(\mathbb{R}\) and \(\mathbb{R}^2\) are not homeomorphic.
  • \(GL_n(\mathbb{R})\) has two components (\(\det>0\) and \(\det<0\)). \(O(n)\) has two components. \(SO(n)\) and \(GL_n(\mathbb{C})\) are connected.

Baire category theorem

Theorem. If \(X\) is a complete metric space (or a locally compact Hausdorff space) and \(X=\bigcup_{n}F_n\) is a countable union of closed sets, then some \(F_n\) has nonempty interior. Equivalent forms:

  • a nonempty complete metric space is not a countable union of nowhere dense sets (it is of the second category in itself);
  • a countable intersection of dense open sets in a complete metric space is dense.

Applications:

  • \(\mathbb{R}\) is uncountable: it is a countable union of singletons if countable, and singletons are nowhere dense.
  • \(\mathbb{Q}\) is not a \(G_\delta\) in \(\mathbb{R}\); no function on \(\mathbb{R}\) is continuous exactly at the rationals.
  • An infinite-dimensional Banach space has no countable Hamel basis.
  • Completeness is needed: \(\mathbb{Q}=\bigcup\{q\}\) is a countable union of nowhere dense sets.
  • Baire theorem underlies the uniform boundedness, open mapping and closed graph theorems (next topic).

Contraction mapping theorem (Banach fixed point theorem)

Let \((X,d)\) be a nonempty complete metric space and \(T:X\to X\) a contraction: there is a fixed \(\alpha\in[0,1)\) with \(d(Tx,Ty)\le\alpha\,d(x,y)\) for all \(x,y\). Then

  1. \(T\) has exactly one fixed point \(x^*\);
  2. for any \(x_0\), the iterates \(x_{n+1}=Tx_n\) converge to \(x^*\);
  3. \(d(x_n,x^*)\le\dfrac{\alpha^n}{1-\alpha}d(x_1,x_0)\) (a priori) and \(d(x_n,x^*)\le\dfrac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}d(x_n,x_{n-1})\) (a posteriori).

Variants and warnings:

  • If some power \(T^k\) is a contraction, \(T\) still has a unique fixed point.
  • The constant must be uniform. \(d(Tx,Ty)<d(x,y)\) for \(x\ne y\) is not enough on a non-compact space: \(T(x)=x+1/x\) on \([1,\infty)\) satisfies \(\lvert Tx-Ty\rvert=\lvert x-y\rvert\,\lvert1-\frac1{xy}\rvert<\lvert x-y\rvert\) but has no fixed point. (On a compact metric space the strict inequality does suffice.)
  • Completeness is needed: \(T(x)=x/2\) on \((0,1]\) is a contraction with \(\alpha=\tfrac12\) but its only candidate fixed point \(0\) is outside the space.
  • \(T\) must map \(X\) into \(X\).
  • On an interval, if \(T\) is differentiable and \(\lvert T'\rvert\le\alpha<1\) then \(T\) is a contraction (mean value theorem).
  • Applications: Picard’s existence and uniqueness theorem for \(y'=f(x,y)\) with \(f\) Lipschitz in \(y\); the implicit function theorem; solving \(x=\cos x\); Newton and fixed-point iterations in numerical analysis.

Formulas and results

  • Closure: \(\overline{A}=A\cup A'\). Interior: largest open subset. \(\partial A=\overline{A}\setminus A^\circ\).
  • Closed iff contains all limit points iff complement open iff sequentially closed.
  • Convergent \(\Rightarrow\) Cauchy \(\Rightarrow\) bounded.
  • Subspace of complete space is complete iff closed.
  • Lipschitz \(\Rightarrow\) uniformly continuous \(\Rightarrow\) continuous; compact domain: continuous \(\Rightarrow\) uniformly continuous.
  • Uniform limit of continuous (resp. uniformly continuous) functions is continuous (resp. uniformly continuous).
  • Compact \(\iff\) sequentially compact \(\iff\) complete and totally bounded (metric spaces).
  • Heine-Borel: compact \(\iff\) closed and bounded, in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) only.
  • Totally bounded \(\iff\) every sequence has a Cauchy subsequence; totally bounded \(\Rightarrow\) bounded and separable.
  • Continuous image of compact is compact; of connected is connected.
  • Baire: complete metric space \(\ne\) countable union of nowhere dense sets.
  • Banach: \(d(Tx,Ty)\le\alpha d(x,y)\), \(\alpha<1\), \(X\) complete \(\Rightarrow\) unique fixed point, \(d(x_n,x^*)\le\frac{\alpha^n}{1-\alpha}d(x_1,x_0)\).

Worked examples

Example 1: closure, interior, boundary

Let \(A=\{1/n:n\ge1\}\cup(2,3)\cup(\mathbb{Q}\cap[4,5])\subseteq\mathbb{R}\).

  • Closure: points \(1/n\) plus their limit \(0\), then \([2,3]\), then the closure of \(\mathbb{Q}\cap[4,5]\), which is \([4,5]\). So \(\overline{A}=\{0\}\cup\{1/n\}\cup[2,3]\cup[4,5]\).
  • Interior: only \((2,3)\) is open (isolated points \(1/n\) and the rationals have no ball inside \(A\)). \(A^\circ=(2,3)\).
  • Boundary: \(\overline{A}\setminus A^\circ=\{0\}\cup\{1/n\}\cup\{2,3\}\cup[4,5]\).
  • \(A\) is not closed (misses \(0\)) and not open.

Example 2: a Cauchy sequence that does not converge

In \(X=(0,1)\) with \(d(x,y)=\lvert x-y\rvert\) the sequence \(x_n=1/n\) is Cauchy because \(\lvert\frac1n-\frac1m\rvert\le\max(\frac1n,\frac1m)\to0\). Its limit in \(\mathbb{R}\) is \(0\notin X\), so \(X\) is not complete. Yet \(X\) is totally bounded (cover by intervals of length \(\varepsilon\)) and every sequence has a Cauchy subsequence (Bolzano-Weierstrass in \([0,1]\)).

Example 3: uniform continuity of \(\sqrt x\) on \([0,\infty)\)

For \(x\ge y\ge0\), \((\sqrt x-\sqrt y)^2\le(\sqrt x-\sqrt y)(\sqrt x+\sqrt y)=x-y\), so \(\lvert\sqrt x-\sqrt y\rvert\le\sqrt{\lvert x-y\rvert}\). Given \(\varepsilon\) take \(\delta=\varepsilon^2\). Therefore \(\sqrt x\) is uniformly continuous on all of \([0,\infty)\). It is not Lipschitz near \(0\) since \(f'(x)=\frac1{2\sqrt x}\to\infty\).

Example 4: how many iterations of \(x\mapsto\cos x\)?

\(T(x)=\cos x\) maps \([0,1]\) into \([\cos1,1]\subseteq[0,1]\), and \(\lvert T'(x)\rvert=\sin x\le\sin1\approx0.8415=\alpha<1\) on \([0,1]\). So \(T\) is a contraction of the complete space \([0,1]\) and has a unique fixed point \(x^*\approx0.739085\). Start at \(x_0=0.5\): \(x_1=\cos0.5\approx0.87758\), so \(d(x_1,x_0)\approx0.37758\). The a priori bound gives

\(d(x_n,x^*)\le\frac{0.8415^n}{1-0.8415}\cdot0.37758<10^{-6}\iff n\ge86.\)

This is a guaranteed bound; in practice the iteration converges much faster (the true error at \(n=86\) is about \(10^{-16}\)).

Example 5: closed and bounded but not compact

Let \(B=\{x\in\ell^2:\lVert x\rVert\le1\}\). It is closed and bounded. The sequence \(e_n=(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots)\) lies in \(B\) and \(\lVert e_n-e_m\rVert=\sqrt2\) for \(n\ne m\), so no subsequence is Cauchy, hence none converges. \(B\) is not sequentially compact, so not compact (and not totally bounded: no finite \(\frac12\)-net, since the \(e_n\) are \(\sqrt2\) apart).

Example 6: counting connected components

Determine the number of components of \(\{A\in M_2(\mathbb{R}):\det A\ne0\}\). The determinant is continuous, so the image of a connected piece lies in \((0,\infty)\) or \((-\infty,0)\), giving at least two components. Each of \(\{\det>0\}\) and \(\{\det<0\}\) is connected (generated by elementary matrices, or path connected via row operations). So there are exactly \(2\) components.

Common traps

  • Heine-Borel outside \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Closed and bounded does not mean compact in \(\ell^2\), \(C[0,1]\) or discrete spaces. Compact needs complete and totally bounded.
  • Totally bounded does not imply complete or compact. \((0,1)\) is the standard example (2026 Q54).
  • “Every sequence has a Cauchy subsequence” is exactly total boundedness, not completeness and not compactness.
  • Bounded \(\ne\) totally bounded: the discrete metric \(d\le1\) on an infinite set is bounded but not totally bounded.
  • Completeness is not preserved by homeomorphism: \((0,1)\cong\mathbb{R}\). It is preserved by isometry.
  • Continuous images: compact and connected survive; completeness, boundedness, openness, closedness do not (\(\tan:(-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2)\to\mathbb{R}\) is an homeomorphism taking a bounded set to an unbounded one).
  • Continuous does not imply uniformly continuous (needs compact domain). A bounded function is not automatically uniformly continuous.
  • Closure of an open ball need not be the closed ball.
  • Uniform limit of uniformly continuous functions is uniformly continuous (true), while a pointwise limit of continuous functions need not be continuous.
  • Contraction: the constant must be \(<1\) and uniform; \(d(Tx,Ty)<d(x,y)\) is not enough; \(X\) must be complete; \(T\) must map \(X\) into itself.
  • Baire needs completeness: \(\mathbb{Q}\) is a countable union of singletons.
  • Path connected implies connected, not conversely.
  • Every metric space is \(T_1,T_2,T_3,T_4\) (2025 Q94 asked for the “incorrect” separation statement; all four are true, so the question is defective and the official key’s D is a guess).

Previous-year questions

2025, Q96. Every totally bounded metric space is: (A) separable, (B) reducible, (C) compact, (D) locally compact.

Answer: A (separable). For each \(k\) choose a finite \(\frac1k\)-net; the union of these nets is countable and dense. Compact would need completeness too (take \((0,1)\)); “reducible” has no meaning here.

2025, Q93. Match List I with List II. List I: (a) \((a,b)\), (b) \((a,b]\), (c) \([a,b)\), (d) \([a,b]\). List II: (I) \(\{x:a\le x<b\}\), (II) \(\{x:a\le x\le b\}\), (III) \(\{x:a<x<b\}\), (IV) \(\{x:a<x\le b\}\). Options: (A) a-I, b-II, c-III, d-IV; (B) (garbled in the scanned paper); (C) a-I, b-III, c-II, d-IV; (D) a-III, b-IV, c-I, d-II.

Answer: D. A round bracket excludes the endpoint and a square bracket includes it. So \((a,b)=\{a<x<b\}\) is III, \((a,b]=\{a<x\le b\}\) is IV, \([a,b)=\{a\le x<b\}\) is I and \([a,b]=\{a\le x\le b\}\) is II. This agrees with the official key (D); A and C both match \((a,b)\) with I, which wrongly includes \(a\). In the metric-space language of this note, \((a,b)\) is open, \([a,b]\) is closed and the two half-open intervals are neither.

2026, Q54. Every sequence in a metric space \(X\) has a Cauchy subsequence. Which of “compact”, “totally bounded”, “complete” must hold for \(X\)? (Official worked key.)

Answer: totally bounded only. A metric space is totally bounded iff every sequence has a Cauchy subsequence. Completeness (and hence compactness) need not hold: \((0,1)\) has the property but is incomplete.

2026, Q33. Let \(f_n\to f\) uniformly on a metric space \(X\), each \(f_n\) uniformly continuous. What follows for \(f\)? (Worked key; the key is unofficial.)

Answer: \(f\) is uniformly continuous. Given \(\varepsilon\) pick \(n\) with \(\sup\lvert f_n-f\rvert<\varepsilon/3\) and \(\delta\) with \(d(x,y)<\delta\Rightarrow\lvert f_n(x)-f_n(y)\rvert<\varepsilon/3\). Then \(\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert<\varepsilon\) by the triangle inequality (\(3\varepsilon\) argument).

Quick revision

  • Closure \(=\) set plus limit points; interior \(=\) points with a ball inside; dense \(=\) closure is \(X\).
  • Cauchy does not imply convergent; complete means every Cauchy sequence converges.
  • \(\mathbb{Q}\), \((0,1)\), \(C[0,1]\) with \(L^1\) metric, polynomials with sup metric are incomplete; \(\mathbb{R}^n\), \(\ell^p\), \(C[a,b]\) with sup metric are complete.
  • Subspace of a complete space is complete iff closed.
  • Lipschitz \(\Rightarrow\) uniformly continuous \(\Rightarrow\) continuous; compact domain closes the gap.
  • Metric space: compact \(\iff\) sequentially compact \(\iff\) complete and totally bounded.
  • Heine-Borel (closed and bounded) holds only in \(\mathbb{R}^n\); the unit ball of \(\ell^2\) is a counterexample.
  • Totally bounded \(\iff\) every sequence has a Cauchy subsequence; it implies bounded and separable.
  • Continuous image of compact is compact; of connected is connected.
  • Connected subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\) are intervals; path connected \(\Rightarrow\) connected, not conversely.
  • Baire: a complete metric space is not a countable union of nowhere dense sets.
  • Contraction on a complete space: unique fixed point, \(d(x_n,x^*)\le\frac{\alpha^n}{1-\alpha}d(x_1,x_0)\).
