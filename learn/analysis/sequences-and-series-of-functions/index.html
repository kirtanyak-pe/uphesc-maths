Why it matters for the exam

This is the most reliably asked part of Unit 3. In both the 2025 and 2026 papers at least two questions came from uniform convergence: choose the interval on which a series or sequence converges uniformly, or decide whether a limit can be passed through an integral. The tools needed are small: the sup-norm test, the Weierstrass M-test, and the three “interchange” theorems. Learn the standard examples in the table below, because the exam reuses them with small changes.

Core ideas

Pointwise convergence

Let \(f_n, f : E \to \mathbb{R}\). We say \(f_n \to f\) pointwise on \(E\) if for each fixed \(x \in E\) and each \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(N = N(\varepsilon, x)\) with \(\lvert f_n(x) - f(x)\rvert < \varepsilon\) for all \(n \ge N\).

Pointwise convergence preserves almost nothing. A pointwise limit of continuous functions can be discontinuous, and a pointwise limit of integrable functions can fail to be integrable or can have the wrong integral.

Uniform convergence

\(f_n \to f\) uniformly on \(E\) if for each \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(N = N(\varepsilon)\), independent of \(x\), such that \(\lvert f_n(x) - f(x)\rvert < \varepsilon\) for all \(n \ge N\) and all \(x \in E\).

Equivalent forms you must be able to use:

  • Sup-norm test. Put \(M_n = \sup_{x\in E}\lvert f_n(x) - f(x)\rvert\). Then \(f_n \to f\) uniformly on \(E\) if and only if \(M_n \to 0\). This is the main computational tool: find the pointwise limit \(f\) first, then maximise \(\lvert f_n - f\rvert\) by calculus.
  • Negation (to disprove). \(f_n \not\to f\) uniformly iff there exist \(\varepsilon_0 > 0\) and points \(x_n \in E\) with \(\lvert f_n(x_n) - f(x_n)\rvert \ge \varepsilon_0\) for infinitely many \(n\). One bad moving point is enough.
  • Cauchy criterion. \((f_n)\) converges uniformly on \(E\) iff for each \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(N\) with \(\sup_E \lvert f_n - f_m\rvert < \varepsilon\) for all \(m, n \ge N\). This lets you prove uniform convergence without knowing the limit.

Uniform convergence on \(E\) implies uniform convergence on every subset of \(E\). Failure on a set that has \(0\) (or \(\infty\)) as a limit point is the typical situation: the sequence fails near the bad point but is fine on intervals that stay away from it.

Series of functions

\(\sum f_n\) converges (pointwise or uniformly) when the partial sums \(s_N = \sum_{n\le N} f_n\) do. A necessary condition for uniform convergence of \(\sum f_n\) is \(f_n \to 0\) uniformly. The Cauchy criterion becomes: \(\sup_E \lvert f_{m+1} + \dots + f_n\rvert < \varepsilon\) for \(n > m \ge N\).

Weierstrass M-test. If \(\lvert f_n(x)\rvert \le M_n\) for all \(x \in E\) and \(\sum M_n < \infty\), then \(\sum f_n\) converges uniformly and absolutely on \(E\). The converse is false: uniform convergence does not need \(\sum \sup\lvert f_n\rvert < \infty\). Example: \(\sum (-1)^n/(n + x^2)\) converges uniformly on \(\mathbb{R}\), yet \(\sup_x \frac{1}{n+x^2} = \frac1n\) and \(\sum \frac1n\) diverges, so the M-test cannot be used.

Dirichlet test (uniform version). Suppose the partial sums of \(\sum f_n\) are uniformly bounded on \(E\), i.e. \(\bigl\lvert \sum_{k\le N} f_k(x)\bigr\rvert \le K\) for all \(x, N\); and for each \(x\) the sequence \(g_n(x)\) is monotone in \(n\) with \(g_n \to 0\) uniformly on \(E\). Then \(\sum f_n g_n\) converges uniformly on \(E\).

Abel test (uniform version). Suppose \(\sum f_n\) converges uniformly on \(E\), and for each \(x\) the sequence \(g_n(x)\) is monotone in \(n\) and uniformly bounded. Then \(\sum f_n g_n\) converges uniformly on \(E\).

Standard uses: \(\sum \frac{\sin nx}{n}\) converges uniformly on \([\delta, 2\pi - \delta]\) for every \(\delta > 0\) (Dirichlet: \(\lvert\sum_{k\le N}\sin kx\rvert \le 1/\sin(\delta/2)\)) but not on \([0, 2\pi]\). Also \(\sum (-1)^n g_n(x)\) with \(g_n \downarrow 0\) uniformly converges uniformly, because the remainder is at most \(g_{N+1}(x)\).

What uniform convergence preserves

Property Theorem (all on \([a,b]\) or a metric space as stated) Converse or weaker hypothesis
Continuity If \(f_n\) are continuous and \(f_n \to f\) uniformly, then \(f\) is continuous A discontinuous limit proves the convergence is not uniform. Converse is false: pointwise limit can be continuous without uniform convergence
Uniform continuity Uniform limit of uniformly continuous functions is uniformly continuous, on any metric space No compactness needed
Boundedness Uniform limit of bounded functions is bounded Pointwise limit need not be bounded
Integration If \(f_n\) are Riemann integrable on \([a,b]\) and \(f_n \to f\) uniformly, then \(f\) is integrable and \(\int f_n \to \int f\) Only a sufficient condition
Differentiation If \(f_n\) are differentiable, \(f_n(x_0)\) converges for some \(x_0\), and \(f_n'\) converges uniformly on \([a,b]\), then \(f_n \to f\) uniformly, \(f\) is differentiable and \(f' = \lim f_n'\) Uniform convergence of \(f_n\) alone is not enough

Note the difference in the differentiation theorem: the hypothesis is on the derivatives. Uniform convergence of \(f_n\) alone does not make the limit differentiable, and even when the limit is differentiable, \(f_n'\) need not converge to \(f'\).

Dini’s theorem. If \(K\) is compact, \(f_n : K \to \mathbb{R}\) are continuous, \(f_n \to f\) pointwise with \(f\) continuous, and \(f_n(x)\) is monotone in \(n\) for every \(x\), then the convergence is uniform. Each hypothesis is needed. For \(x^n\) on \([0,1]\) the limit is discontinuous. For \(x^n\) on \([0,1)\) the set is not compact (the limit \(0\) is continuous and the sequence is monotone, yet \(\sup = 1\)). For \(nxe^{-nx^2}\) on \([0,1]\) the limit \(0\) is continuous and the set is compact, but the sequence is not monotone in \(n\), and the convergence is not uniform.

Weierstrass approximation theorem. Every continuous function on \([a,b]\) is a uniform limit of polynomials. Therefore polynomials are dense in \(C[a,b]\) under the sup norm.

Arzela-Ascoli (statement only). A sequence in \(C[a,b]\) that is uniformly bounded and equicontinuous has a uniformly convergent subsequence.

Power series

For \(\sum a_n (x-c)^n\) the radius of convergence is \(R = \frac{1}{\limsup \lvert a_n\rvert^{1/n}} = \lim \left\lvert \frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}\right\rvert \quad\text{(second form only when the limit exists).}\)

  • It converges absolutely for \(\lvert x - c\rvert < R\) and diverges for \(\lvert x - c\rvert > R\). Nothing general holds at the end points; test them separately.
  • It converges uniformly on every compact subset \([c - r, c + r]\), \(0 < r < R\), but not necessarily on the whole open interval \((c-R, c+R)\) (e.g. \(\sum x^n\) on \((-1,1)\)).
  • The sum is continuous and differentiable inside, and can be differentiated or integrated term by term inside the interval. The differentiated and integrated series have the same radius \(R\).
  • Abel’s theorem. If \(\sum a_n R^n\) converges, then the series converges uniformly on \([0, R]\) (taking \(c = 0\)) and the sum is continuous on \([0,R]\). In particular \(\ln 2 = 1 - \frac12 + \frac13 - \dots\) and \(\frac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \dots\).
  • If the series has gaps (such as \(\sum x^{n^2}\), with \(a_k = 0\) unless \(k\) is a perfect square), use the root test through the limsup form.

Standard examples

Learn this table. “Sup” is \(\sup_E \lvert f_n - f\rvert\).

\(f_n(x)\) Set \(E\) Pointwise limit Sup Uniform?
\(x^n\) \([0,1]\) \(0\) for \(x<1\), \(1\) at \(x=1\) \(1\) No (limit discontinuous)
\(x^n\) \([0,a]\), \(a<1\) \(0\) \(a^n \to 0\) Yes
\(x^n(1-x)\) \([0,1]\) \(0\) \(\frac{1}{n+1}\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n \to 0\) Yes
\(x^n - x^{2n}\) \([0,1]\) \(0\) \(\frac14\) No
\(\dfrac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}\) \([0,\infty)\) \(0\) \(\frac12\) at \(x = 1/n\) No on any \([0,b]\); yes on \([a,\infty)\), \(a>0\)
\(\dfrac{x}{1+nx^2}\) \(\mathbb{R}\) \(0\) \(\frac{1}{2\sqrt n}\) Yes on \(\mathbb{R}\)
\(nx\,e^{-nx^2}\) \([0,a]\) \(0\) \(\sqrt{n/2}\,e^{-1/2} \to \infty\) No; yes on \([1,2]\)
\(\dfrac{\sin nx}{\sqrt n}\) \(\mathbb{R}\) \(0\) \(\frac1{\sqrt n}\) Yes, but \(f_n'\) does not converge
\(\dfrac{x}{n}\) \(\mathbb{R}\) \(0\) unbounded No on \(\mathbb{R}\); yes on bounded sets

Two instructive facts about these examples.

  1. \(f_n(x) = \frac{x}{1+nx^2}\) converges uniformly to \(0\), yet \(f_n'(0) = 1\) for all \(n\) while \(f'(0) = 0\). So \(\lim f_n' \ne (\lim f_n)'\) at \(0\).
  2. \(f_n(x) = \frac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}\) is not uniformly convergent on \([0,1]\), yet \(\int_0^1 f_n = \frac{\ln(1+n^2)}{2n} \to 0 = \int_0^1 f\). So the integral theorem is a sufficient condition only. By contrast \(nxe^{-nx^2}\) gives \(\int_0^1 f_n = \frac{1 - e^{-n}}{2} \to \frac12 \ne 0\): a genuine failure.

Series of the PYQ type

For \(\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{1}{1+n^2x}\): on \([1,\infty)\) we have \(0 < \frac{1}{1+n^2x} \le \frac{1}{n^2}\), so the M-test gives uniform convergence. On any interval \((0,c]\) it fails: take \(x_N = 1/N^2\), then \(\sum_{n=N}^{2N}\frac{1}{1+n^2/N^2} \ge N\cdot\frac15\), which is unbounded in \(N\). The rule is that the troublesome point is \(x = 0\), where the series diverges.

Formulas and results

  • \(f_n \to f\) uniformly on \(E\) \(\iff\) \(M_n = \sup_E\lvert f_n - f\rvert \to 0\).
  • Uniform Cauchy: \(\sup_E \lvert f_n - f_m\rvert \to 0\) as \(m,n\to\infty\).
  • M-test: \(\lvert f_n\rvert \le M_n\) on \(E\), \(\sum M_n<\infty\) \(\Rightarrow\) uniform and absolute convergence.
  • Uniform limit of continuous (uniformly continuous, bounded) functions is continuous (uniformly continuous, bounded).
  • \(f_n \to f\) uniformly on \([a,b]\) and integrable \(\Rightarrow \int_a^b f_n \to \int_a^b f\).
  • Derivative theorem: \(f_n(x_0)\) converges and \(f_n' \to g\) uniformly \(\Rightarrow\) \(f_n \to f\) uniformly, \(f' = g\).
  • Dini: compact set, continuous \(f_n\), monotone in \(n\), continuous limit \(\Rightarrow\) uniform.
  • Power series: \(R = 1/\limsup\lvert a_n\rvert^{1/n}\); uniform on \([c-r,c+r]\), \(r<R\); same \(R\) after differentiation and integration; Abel’s theorem at the end point.
  • Maxima for the sup test: \(\frac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}\) has maximum \(\frac12\) at \(x = \frac1n\); \(nxe^{-nx^2}\) has maximum \(\sqrt{n/2}\,e^{-1/2}\) at \(x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\); \(\frac{x}{1+nx^2}\) has maximum \(\frac{1}{2\sqrt n}\) at \(x = \frac{1}{\sqrt n}\).
  • Geometric: \(\sum_{n\ge0} x^n = \frac{1}{1-x}\), \(\sum_{n\ge1} n x^n = \frac{x}{(1-x)^2}\), \(\sum_{n \ge 1}\frac{x^n}{n} = -\ln(1-x)\) for \(\lvert x\rvert<1\).

Worked examples

Example 1: sup-norm test with a non-uniform limit

Test \(f_n(x) = \dfrac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}\) on \([0,1]\) for uniform convergence, and find \(\lim \int_0^1 f_n\).

  • Pointwise: for \(x=0\), \(f_n=0\); for \(x>0\), \(f_n(x) \sim \frac{1}{nx}\to 0\). So \(f = 0\).
  • \(f_n(1/n) = \frac{1}{1+1} = \frac12\), and calculus shows this is the maximum. So \(M_n = \frac12 \not\to 0\): not uniform.
  • Even so, \(\int_0^1 f_n\,dx = \frac{\ln(1+n^2)}{2n} \to 0 = \int_0^1 f\). The limit can still be interchanged here.

Example 2: uniform convergence of \(x^n(1-x)\)

On \([0,1]\) the pointwise limit is \(0\). Let \(h(x)=x^n(1-x)\). Then \(h'(x) = x^{n-1}(n - (n+1)x) = 0\) at \(x = \frac{n}{n+1}\), so \(M_n = \left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n \frac{1}{n+1} \le \frac{1}{n+1} \to 0.\) Hence uniform convergence. (Compare \(x^n\), where the factor \((1-x)\) is absent and convergence fails.)

Example 3: M-test on a series

Show that \(\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{x}{n(1+nx^2)}\) converges uniformly on \(\mathbb{R}\).

Since \(\sup_x \frac{x}{1+nx^2} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt n}\) (at \(x=\frac1{\sqrt n}\)), we get \(\left\lvert\frac{x}{n(1+nx^2)}\right\rvert \le \frac{1}{2n^{3/2}}\). The series \(\sum n^{-3/2}\) converges, so the M-test applies.

Example 4: radius of convergence

(a) \(\sum \frac{(n!)^2}{(2n)!}x^n\): \(\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}} = \frac{(2n+2)(2n+1)}{(n+1)^2} \to 4\), so \(R = 4\).

(b) \(\sum \left(1+\frac1n\right)^{n^2}x^n\): \(\lvert a_n\rvert^{1/n} = \left(1+\frac1n\right)^n \to e\), so \(R = \frac1e\).

Example 5: sums by term-by-term operations

Start from \(\sum_{n\ge0} x^n = \frac{1}{1-x}\) on \((-1,1)\). Differentiating term by term (allowed inside the radius): \(\sum_{n\ge1} n x^{n-1} = \frac{1}{(1-x)^2}\). Multiply by \(x\): \(\sum n x^n = \frac{x}{(1-x)^2}\). At \(x = \frac12\): \(\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{n}{2^n} = \frac{1/2}{1/4} = 2.\)

Example 6: failure of the interchange

\(f_n(x) = nxe^{-nx^2}\) on \([0,1]\). Pointwise limit is \(0\). \(\int_0^1 f_n\,dx = \frac{1-e^{-n}}{2}\to\frac12\), but \(\int_0^1 0 = 0\). The maximum \(\sqrt{n/2}\,e^{-1/2}\to\infty\) confirms the failure of uniform convergence on \([0,a]\) for every \(a > 0\). On \([1,2]\), \(0\le f_n \le 2ne^{-n}\to 0\), so convergence is uniform there.

Common traps

  • Limit function first. Always find the pointwise limit before testing uniformity. Computing \(\sup\lvert f_n\rvert\) is wrong if the limit is not \(0\) (as with \(x^n\) at \(x=1\)).
  • Sup over the right set. The same \(f_n\) may be uniform on \([a,\infty)\) and not on \((0,\infty)\). Compare options by whether \(0\) (or \(\infty\)) is a limit point of the interval.
  • Open versus closed. A power series is uniform on closed subintervals of the open interval of convergence, not necessarily on the whole open interval.
  • Converse of continuity. A continuous limit does not prove uniform convergence (\(\frac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}\), \(nxe^{-nx^2}\)). Only a discontinuous limit disproves it.
  • Interchange is sufficient, not necessary. Failure of uniform convergence does not by itself mean the integral limit differs. Always compute \(\int f_n\) directly if asked.
  • Differentiation needs the derivatives. \(\frac{\sin nx}{\sqrt n}\to 0\) uniformly, but \(f_n'(0)=\sqrt n\to\infty\).
  • M-test is one-way. If you cannot bound by a convergent \(\sum M_n\), the series may still converge uniformly (Dirichlet/Abel, alternating series).
  • Term check. If \(\sup\lvert f_n\rvert \not\to 0\) then \(\sum f_n\) cannot converge uniformly (the series may not even converge pointwise).
  • Dini needs everything. Compactness, continuous limit, and monotonicity in \(n\).

Previous-year questions

2025, Q41

The series \(\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{1}{1+n^2x}\) is uniformly convergent on which interval? The options are three intervals of the type \((0,c]\) or \((0,1)\) and \([1,\infty)\). (The exact fractions printed in options A and B are hard to read in the available copy.) Answer: D, \([1,\infty)\) (official key).

Solution: on \([1,\infty)\), \(\frac{1}{1+n^2x}\le\frac1{n^2}\) and \(\sum n^{-2}<\infty\), so the M-test applies. On any interval containing points arbitrarily close to \(0\) the series is not uniformly convergent (tail estimate above).

2025, Q45

The sequence \(f_n(x) = nx\,e^{-nx^2}\) converges uniformly on which of (A) \([0,1]\), (B) \([0,2]\), (C) \([0,3]\), (D) \([1,2]\)? Answer: D (official key).

Solution: \(f_n\to0\) pointwise. The maximum on \([0,a]\) is at \(x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\) and equals \(\sqrt{n/2}\,e^{-1/2}\to\infty\), so \([0,a]\) fails for every \(a>0\). On \([1,2]\), \(f_n \le 2ne^{-n}\to0\) (the function is decreasing for \(x\ge\frac1{\sqrt{2n}}\)). The same answer comes out if the exponent is read as \(-nx\).

2026, Q33

If \(f_n \to f\) uniformly and each \(f_n\) is uniformly continuous on a metric space \(X\), then (A) \(f\) is uniformly continuous, (B) \(f\) is continuous only, (C) \(f\) is not continuous, (D) it depends on compactness. Answer: A (worked key, unofficial).

Solution: given \(\varepsilon\), choose \(n\) with \(\sup\lvert f_n-f\rvert<\varepsilon/3\), then \(\delta\) for \(f_n\) with \(\varepsilon/3\). Then \(\lvert f(x)-f(y)\rvert\le\lvert f(x)-f_n(x)\rvert+\lvert f_n(x)-f_n(y)\rvert+\lvert f_n(y)-f(y)\rvert<\varepsilon\) whenever \(d(x,y)<\delta\). No compactness is used.

2026, Q44

Let \(f_n(x)=\dfrac{nx}{1+n^3x^2}\) on \([0,\infty)\) (the printed exponent is hard to read; the answer is the same for \(n^2x^2\)) and \(F=\sum f_n\). Which holds about uniform convergence on \([0,\infty)\) and interchange of the limit with \(\int_0^\infty\)? (A) uniformly convergent and interchange valid, (B) not uniformly convergent but interchange valid, (C) uniformly convergent but interchange invalid, (D) not uniformly convergent and interchange invalid. Answer: D (worked key, unofficial; the overrides note that B is arguable only if “valid” is read as \(\infty=\infty\)).

Solution: for fixed \(x>0\) the terms behave like \(\frac{1}{n^2x}\) (or \(\frac1{nx}\) for the \(n^2\) reading). With \(x_N=N^{-3/2}\) (use \(x_N=1/N\) for the \(n^2\) reading) the block \(\sum_{n=N}^{2N}f_n(x_N)\) grows like \(\sqrt N\) (for the \(n^2\) reading with \(x_N=1/N\) each term is about \(0.4\) to \(0.5\), so the block sum grows like \(N\), not \(\sqrt N\)), so the Cauchy criterion fails either way and the series is not uniform. Each \(\int_0^\infty f_n\,dx\) diverges (logarithmically at infinity), so integrating term by term is invalid.

Quick revision

  • Uniform convergence on \(E\) iff \(\sup_E\lvert f_n-f\rvert\to0\); find \(f\) first.
  • A discontinuous limit of continuous functions means not uniform. A continuous limit proves nothing.
  • M-test: \(\lvert f_n\rvert\le M_n\), \(\sum M_n<\infty\) gives uniform and absolute convergence on \(E\).
  • Uniform limit of uniformly continuous functions is uniformly continuous, on any metric space.
  • Integral interchange holds under uniform convergence on \([a,b]\); derivative interchange needs uniform convergence of \(f_n'\).
  • \(\frac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}\): sup \(\frac12\), not uniform near \(0\); uniform on \([a,\infty)\).
  • \(nxe^{-nx^2}\): sup \(\to\infty\), not uniform on \([0,a]\); \(\int_0^1\to\frac12\); uniform on \([1,2]\).
  • \(\frac{x}{1+nx^2}\): sup \(\frac1{2\sqrt n}\), uniform on \(\mathbb{R}\); \(f_n'(0)=1\not\to f'(0)=0\).
  • Power series: \(R=1/\limsup\lvert a_n\rvert^{1/n}\), uniform on \([c-r,c+r]\) for \(r<R\), Abel at the end point.
  • Dini: compact, continuous, monotone, continuous limit. Fails for \(x^n\) on \([0,1]\).
