Why it matters for the exam

This is the most examined topic of the mechanics half: five of the ten mechanics questions in 2025 and 2026 come from it (Poisson-bracket identities, a Hamiltonian from a Lagrangian, what a canonical transformation preserves, what \(\{A,H\}=0\) means, action-angle variables). Questions are short and direct. You need the exact definitions, the fundamental brackets, the four generating functions, and the conditions for a transformation to be canonical.

Core ideas

Legendre transform and Hamilton’s equations

Given \(L(q,\dot q,t)\), define the generalized momenta \(p_j=\partial L/\partial\dot q_j\). If the Hessian \(\det\left(\partial^2L/\partial\dot q_i\partial\dot q_j\right)\ne0\), this can be inverted to give \(\dot q_j=\dot q_j(q,p,t)\). The Hamiltonian is the Legendre transform

\(H(q,p,t)=\sum_jp_j\dot q_j-L(q,\dot q,t),\)

with every \(\dot q\) replaced by its expression in \((q,p,t)\). Taking the differential and using the definition of \(p_j\) gives Hamilton’s canonical equations:

\(\boxed{\dot q_j=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_j},\qquad \dot p_j=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_j},\qquad \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}.}\)

For \(n\) degrees of freedom these are \(2n\) first-order equations in phase space \((q_1..q_n,p_1..p_n)\). Along a motion,

\(\frac{dH}{dt}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}.\)

So \(H\) is constant when it has no explicit time. A coordinate \(q_k\) missing from \(H\) is cyclic: \(\dot p_k=0\).

When is \(H=T+V\)?

\(H=T+V\) holds if both

  1. the constraints are scleronomic (time independent), so \(T\) is a homogeneous quadratic form in \(\dot q\), and
  2. the potential \(V\) depends on coordinates only, not on velocities.

Reason: \(T=\tfrac12\sum m_{jk}\dot q_j\dot q_k\) gives \(\sum p_j\dot q_j=2T\), so \(H=2T-(T-V)=T+V\).

Two separate questions are asked about \(H\):

\(\partial H/\partial t=0\) \(\partial H/\partial t\ne0\)
Scleronomic, \(V=V(q)\) \(H=T+V=E\), conserved \(H=T+V\) but not conserved (time-dependent \(V\))
Rheonomic constraints \(H\) conserved but \(H\ne T+V\) (rotating wire) neither

Examples:

  • Free particle: \(H=p^2/2m\). Harmonic oscillator: \(H=\dfrac{p^2}{2m}+\tfrac12m\omega^2q^2\).
  • Central force in plane polar coordinates: \(H=\dfrac{p_r^2}{2m}+\dfrac{p_\theta^2}{2mr^2}+V(r)\).
  • Charged particle in a field: \(H=\dfrac{(\mathbf p-q\mathbf A)^2}{2m}+q\phi\). This is \(T+q\phi\) but \(\mathbf p\ne m\mathbf v\).
  • Bead on a horizontal wire rotating at \(\omega\): \(L=\tfrac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\omega^2)\), \(p=m\dot r\), \(H=\dfrac{p^2}{2m}-\tfrac12m\omega^2r^2\). It is conserved but is not \(T+V\).

Procedure to find \(H\): (1) write \(L\), (2) compute \(p_j=\partial L/\partial\dot q_j\), (3) solve for \(\dot q_j\), (4) form \(\sum p_j\dot q_j-L\), (5) express everything in \((q,p)\). For a quadratic \(T=\tfrac12\dot q^TM\dot q\) the result is \(H=\tfrac12p^TM^{-1}p+V\).

Hamilton’s variational principle

The motion of a holonomic system with forces derivable from a (generalized) potential makes the action

\(S=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}L\,dt\)

stationary for variations \(\delta q_j(t)\) that vanish at the end points, \(\delta q_j(t_1)=\delta q_j(t_2)=0\):

\(\delta\int_{t_1}^{t_2}L(q,\dot q,t)\,dt=0.\)

Applying the calculus of variations (Euler-Lagrange equation for the integrand \(L\)) gives Lagrange’s equations. The path need not minimise \(S\); it makes it stationary.

Modified Hamilton’s principle (phase space). Vary \(q\) and \(p\) independently, with \(\delta q=0\) at the ends:

\(\delta\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\Big(\sum_jp_j\dot q_j-H\Big)dt=0,\)

which gives the canonical equations.

Principle of least action (Maupertuis): for scleronomic systems with conserved energy, and fixed energy \(E\), \(\delta\int\sum p_j\,dq_j=0\) (varied paths with the same \(E\) and fixed end points). Do not confuse it with Hamilton’s principle: Hamilton fixes the time interval and varies energy, Maupertuis fixes the energy and varies the time.

Poisson brackets

For two functions \(u(q,p,t)\), \(v(q,p,t)\) the Poisson bracket is

\([u,v]=\{u,v\}=\sum_j\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial q_j}\frac{\partial v}{\partial p_j}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial p_j}\frac{\partial v}{\partial q_j}\right).\)

(This is the Goldstein/Landau sign. It matches the PYQ notation \([q_r,p_s]\).)

Properties.

  1. Antisymmetry: \([u,v]=-[v,u]\), so \([u,u]=0\).
  2. Linearity: \([au+bv,w]=a[u,w]+b[v,w]\).
  3. \([u,c]=0\) for a constant \(c\).
  4. Product rule (Leibniz): \([uv,w]=u[v,w]+[u,w]v\).
  5. Jacobi identity: \([u,[v,w]]+[v,[w,u]]+[w,[u,v]]=0\).
  6. Partial-derivative rules: \([q_j,F]=\partial F/\partial p_j\), \([p_j,F]=-\partial F/\partial q_j\).
  7. Time derivative: \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}[u,v]=[\partial_tu,v]+[u,\partial_tv]\).

Fundamental brackets.

\([q_i,q_j]=0,\qquad [p_i,p_j]=0,\qquad [q_i,p_j]=\delta_{ij}.\)

Note \([q_r,p_s]=\delta_{rs}\), which is \(0\) only for \(r\ne s\). The identity “\([q_r,p_s]=0\) always” is false (the option that fails in 2025-Q69).

Equation of motion in bracket form. For any \(u(q,p,t)\),

\(\frac{du}{dt}=[u,H]+\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}.\)

With \(u=q_j\) and \(u=p_j\) this gives the canonical equations: \(\dot q_j=[q_j,H]=\partial H/\partial p_j\), \(\dot p_j=[p_j,H]=-\partial H/\partial q_j\).

Constants of motion.

  • If \(u\) has no explicit \(t\) and \([u,H]=0\), then \(u\) is conserved (2026-Q50). If \(u\) depends on \(t\), the condition is \([u,H]+\partial_tu=0\).
  • Poisson’s theorem: if \(u\) and \(v\) are constants of motion, so is \([u,v]\) (proof from the Jacobi identity). It may be trivial (a constant) or may produce a new integral.
  • Example: if \(L_x\) and \(L_y\) are conserved, then \([L_x,L_y]=L_z\) is conserved.

Angular-momentum brackets (\(\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p\)):

\([L_x,L_y]=L_z,\quad[L_y,L_z]=L_x,\quad[L_z,L_x]=L_y,\qquad [L_i,L_j]=\epsilon_{ijk}L_k,\)

\([L^2,L_i]=0.\)

For a vector \(\mathbf A\) built from \(\mathbf r\) or \(\mathbf p\): \([L_i,A_j]=\epsilon_{ijk}A_k\), for example \([L_z,x]=y\), \([L_z,y]=-x\), \([L_z,p_x]=p_y\), \([L_z,p_y]=-p_x\), \([L_z,z]=[L_z,p_z]=0\). For any scalar \(f(r^2,p^2,\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf p)\): \([L_i,f]=0\). Hence \(L_z\) commutes with every function of \(r\), \(p^2\) and \(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf p\), so angular momentum is conserved in any central field, \(H=p^2/2m+V(r)\).

Canonical transformations

A transformation \((q,p)\to(Q,P)\), \(Q_j=Q_j(q,p,t)\), \(P_j=P_j(q,p,t)\), is canonical if there is a new Hamiltonian \(K(Q,P,t)\) such that

\(\dot Q_j=\frac{\partial K}{\partial P_j},\qquad\dot P_j=-\frac{\partial K}{\partial Q_j}.\)

It is enough that the two phase-space action integrals differ by a total derivative:

\(\sum p_j\dot q_j-H=\sum P_j\dot Q_j-K+\frac{dF}{dt}.\)

\(F\) is the generating function.

Conditions for a transformation to be canonical (any one is equivalent to the others):

  1. The Poisson-bracket test:

\([Q_i,Q_j]_{q,p}=0,\quad[P_i,P_j]_{q,p}=0,\quad[Q_i,P_j]_{q,p}=\delta_{ij}.\)

  1. Symplectic condition. Let \(\eta=(q,p)\), \(\zeta=(Q,P)\), \(M=\partial\zeta/\partial\eta\) the Jacobian matrix, \(J=\begin{pmatrix}0&I\\-I&0\end{pmatrix}\). Then \(MJM^T=J\) (equivalently \(M^TJM=J\)).
  2. The differential form \(\sum(p_j\,dq_j-P_j\,dQ_j)\) is exact (at fixed \(t\)).
  3. A generating function exists.

For one degree of freedom the symplectic condition reduces to

\(\frac{\partial(Q,P)}{\partial(q,p)}=\frac{\partial Q}{\partial q}\frac{\partial P}{\partial p}-\frac{\partial Q}{\partial p}\frac{\partial P}{\partial q}=1,\)

i.e. unit Jacobian: the transformation preserves phase-space area. This is the answer to 2026-Q40. Only for one degree of freedom is “Jacobian \(=1\)” equivalent to canonical. In general the invariants are the Poisson brackets, the symplectic 2-form, phase-space volume (Liouville’s theorem) and the Poincare integral invariants. Energy, position and momentum alone are not preserved by a general canonical transformation.

Consequences: Poisson brackets are invariant, \([u,v]_{q,p}=[u,v]_{Q,P}\), and the form of the equation of motion \(\dot u=[u,H]+\partial_tu\) is the same in every canonical coordinate system. The motion itself \((q_0,p_0)\to(q(t),p(t))\) is a canonical transformation, with \(H\) as the generator.

A scale change \(Q=\lambda q\), \(P=\lambda p\) is not canonical for \(\lambda^2\ne1\) (the bracket is \(\lambda^2\)), but \(Q=\lambda q\), \(P=p/\lambda\) is. The transformation is “canonical up to scale” if \([Q,P]=c\) constant.

Four types of generating function

Type Function Relations New Hamiltonian
1 \(F_1(q,Q,t)\) \(p_j=\dfrac{\partial F_1}{\partial q_j},\ P_j=-\dfrac{\partial F_1}{\partial Q_j}\) \(K=H+\dfrac{\partial F_1}{\partial t}\)
2 \(F_2(q,P,t)\) \(p_j=\dfrac{\partial F_2}{\partial q_j},\ Q_j=\dfrac{\partial F_2}{\partial P_j}\) \(K=H+\dfrac{\partial F_2}{\partial t}\)
3 \(F_3(p,Q,t)\) \(q_j=-\dfrac{\partial F_3}{\partial p_j},\ P_j=-\dfrac{\partial F_3}{\partial Q_j}\) \(K=H+\dfrac{\partial F_3}{\partial t}\)
4 \(F_4(p,P,t)\) \(q_j=-\dfrac{\partial F_4}{\partial p_j},\ Q_j=\dfrac{\partial F_4}{\partial P_j}\) \(K=H+\dfrac{\partial F_4}{\partial t}\)

The types are related by Legendre transforms:

\(F_2=F_1+\sum Q_jP_j,\qquad F_3=F_1-\sum q_jp_j,\qquad F_4=F_1-\sum q_jp_j+\sum Q_jP_j.\)

Standard ones:

  • \(F_2=\sum q_jP_j\) generates the identity: \(Q_j=q_j\), \(P_j=p_j\).
  • \(F_1=\sum q_jQ_j\) exchanges coordinates and momenta: \(Q_j=p_j\), \(P_j=-q_j\).
  • \(F_2=\sum f_j(q,t)P_j\) generates a point transformation \(Q_j=f_j(q,t)\).
  • Harmonic oscillator: \(F_1=\tfrac12m\omega q^2\cot Q\) gives \(q=\sqrt{2P/m\omega}\,\sin Q\), \(p=\sqrt{2m\omega P}\,\cos Q\), and \(K=\omega P\). \(Q\) is cyclic, so \(P=E/\omega\) is constant.

If a time-independent canonical transformation is used, \(K(Q,P)=H(q(Q,P),p(Q,P))\), but the generating-function term \(\partial F/\partial t\) matters when \(F\) depends on \(t\).

Infinitesimal canonical transformations

\(Q_j=q_j+\epsilon\,\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial p_j}\), \(P_j=p_j-\epsilon\,\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial q_j}\) with generator \(G\); for any \(u\), \(\delta u=\epsilon[u,G]\). Choosing \(G=H\) and \(\epsilon=dt\) gives the actual motion. \(G=p_x\) generates translations; \(G=L_z\) generates rotations. If \([G,H]=0\) then \(G\) is conserved and the transformation is a symmetry of \(H\).

Action-angle variables (bounded motion)

For a one-dimensional system \(H=p^2/2m+V(q)\) with bounded periodic motion (libration between turning points, or rotation) define the action

\(J=\frac1{2\pi}\oint p\,dq.\)

There is a canonical transformation to \((\theta,J)\) in which \(H=H(J)\) only, \(\dot J=0\), \(\dot\theta=\partial H/\partial J=\omega(J)\) and \(\theta\) grows by \(2\pi\) over one period. The harmonic oscillator has \(J=E/\omega\) and \(H=\omega J\) (with the \(\tfrac1{2\pi}\) convention). This is the answer to 2026-Q55: action-angle variables require periodic, bounded motion, not non-periodic, chaotic or unbounded motion.

Hamilton-Jacobi in one line: seek a canonical transformation with \(K=0\); then \(F_2=S(q,P,t)\) satisfies \(H(q,\partial S/\partial q,t)+\partial S/\partial t=0\).

Worked examples

Example 1: Hamiltonian from a Lagrangian (2025 style)

\(L=\dot x^2+2\dot y^2-xy\) (with \(a=1\), \(b=2\), \(k=1\)). Then \(p_x=2\dot x\), \(p_y=4\dot y\), so \(\dot x=p_x/2\), \(\dot y=p_y/4\).

\(H=p_x\dot x+p_y\dot y-L=\frac{p_x^2}{2}+\frac{p_y^2}{4}-\left(\frac{p_x^2}{4}+\frac{2p_y^2}{16}-xy\right)=\frac{p_x^2}{4}+\frac{p_y^2}{8}+xy.\)

General rule: \(L=a\dot x^2+b\dot y^2-U\) gives \(H=\dfrac{p_x^2}{4a}+\dfrac{p_y^2}{4b}+U\). The kinetic term is \(p^2/2m_{\rm eff}\) with \(m_{\rm eff}=2a\), and the potential keeps its sign: \(+kxy\) when \(L\) has \(-kxy\).

Example 2: Poisson brackets of \(L_z\)

\(L_z=xp_y-yp_x\). Then \([L_z,x]=-\dfrac{\partial L_z}{\partial p_x}\cdot1=y\) and \([L_z,p_x]=\dfrac{\partial L_z}{\partial x}\cdot1=p_y\). Also \([L_z,x^2+y^2]=0\) since \(L_z\) generates rotations about \(z\) and \(x^2+y^2\) is invariant.

Example 3: is \(Q=q^2\), \(P=p/(2q)\) canonical?

\([Q,P]=\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial q}\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial p}-\dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial p}\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial q}=2q\cdot\dfrac1{2q}-0=1.\) Yes. By contrast \(Q=q+p\), \(P=q-p\) gives \([Q,P]=1\cdot(-1)-1\cdot1=-2\): not canonical. And \(Q=(q+p)/\sqrt2\), \(P=(p-q)/\sqrt2\) gives \(+1\): canonical (rotation by \(45^\circ\)).

Example 4: generating function \(F_2\)

Find the transformation with \(F_2(q,P)=qP+\tfrac12\lambda q^2\). Then \(p=\partial_qF_2=P+\lambda q\) and \(Q=\partial_PF_2=q\). So \(Q=q\), \(P=p-\lambda q\). Check: \([Q,P]=1\cdot1-0=1\). This is a “shear” in phase space, area preserving.

Example 5: \(H=T+V\) or not?

\(L=\tfrac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)-V(r)\): scleronomic, \(V\) velocity independent, so \(H=T+V\), conserved. For a bead on a wire rotating at \(\omega\): \(H=p^2/2m-\tfrac12m\omega^2r^2\ne T+V\) but \(\partial H/\partial t=0\), so \(H\) is conserved. For a pendulum of length \(l(t)\) the constraint is rheonomic and \(\partial H/\partial t\ne0\): \(H\) is neither conserved nor \(T+V\).

Example 6: one degree of freedom, area preserved

Under \(Q=\ln(1+\sqrt q\cos p)\), \(P=2(1+\sqrt q\cos p)\sqrt q\sin p\) the bracket \([Q,P]=1\), so the transformation is canonical (a classical test-case from Goldstein). Under \(Q=2q\), \(P=2p\) the Jacobian is \(4\): not canonical.

Common traps

  • \([q_r,p_s]=\delta_{rs}\), not \(0\). Only \([q_r,q_s]=0\) and \([p_r,p_s]=0\) vanish. The Jacobi identity and antisymmetry always hold.
  • Sign convention of the bracket: with \([u,v]=\sum(\partial_qu\,\partial_pv-\partial_pu\,\partial_qv)\) one gets \([q,p]=+1\) and \(\dot u=[u,H]\). The opposite convention gives \([q,p]=-1\).
  • \(\{A,H\}=0\) means \(A\) is conserved (if \(A\) has no explicit time). It does not mean \(A\) is zero, linear in time or oscillating.
  • Conserved \(H\) is not always energy and \(H\) is not always \(T+V\).
  • Canonical transformations do not preserve energy, position or momentum: they preserve the symplectic structure, hence phase-space area (1 dof) or volume, and all Poisson brackets. A scale change \(Q=2q\), \(P=2p\) is not canonical.
  • Jacobian \(=1\) is necessary for all \(n\) but sufficient only for \(n=1\).
  • Generating functions: \(F_1(q,Q)\) and \(F_2(q,P)\) both give \(p=+\partial_qF\). Every derivative with respect to \(p\) carries a minus sign (\(q=-\partial_pF\) for \(F_3,F_4\)), and \(P=-\partial_QF\) for \(F_1,F_3\). Mixing up \(F_2\) (\(Q=+\partial_PF_2\)) and \(F_3\) is the standard slip.
  • Poisson’s theorem produces a conserved quantity, but it may be trivial or a function of the earlier ones.
  • Action-angle variables need bounded periodic motion, not any motion. Unbounded motion in a repulsive potential has no action variable of this type.
  • Hamilton’s principle varies the path at fixed times with fixed end points; the endpoints in \(q\) are fixed, not the momenta.
  • \(H\) is a function of \((q,p,t)\). Leaving \(\dot q\) inside \(H\) (not eliminated) is an error.

2025-Q69: which Poisson-bracket relation does not always hold

Question. For any functions \(f,g,h\) of \((q,p)\), with \([f,g]\) the Poisson bracket, which relation does not always hold? (A) \([f,g]=-[g,f]\) (B) \([[f,g],h]+[[g,h],f]+[[h,f],g]=0\) (C) \([q_r,q_s]=0\) (D) \([q_r,p_s]=0\).

Answer: D. Antisymmetry (A), the Jacobi identity (B) and \([q_r,q_s]=0\) (C) always hold. The fundamental bracket is \([q_r,p_s]=\delta_{rs}\), which is \(1\) when \(r=s\). (Official key.)

2025-Q73: Hamiltonian for a given Lagrangian

Question. The Hamiltonian corresponding to \(L=a\dot x^2+b\dot y^2-kxy\) is: (A) \(\frac{p_x^2}{2a}+\frac{p_y^2}{2b}+kxy\) (B) \(\frac{p_x^2}{4a}+\frac{p_y^2}{4b}-kxy\) (C) \(\frac{p_x^2}{4a}+\frac{p_y^2}{4b}+kxy\) (D) \(\frac{p_x^2}{4a}-\frac{p_y^2}{4b}+kxy\).

Answer: C. \(p_x=2a\dot x\), \(p_y=2b\dot y\). \(H=p_x\dot x+p_y\dot y-L=a\dot x^2+b\dot y^2+kxy=\dfrac{p_x^2}{4a}+\dfrac{p_y^2}{4b}+kxy\). Option A uses \(m=a\) instead of \(2a\). (Official key.)

2026-Q40: canonical transformation and invariants

Question. A canonical transformation preserves the symplectic structure. Which quantity is invariant: (A) energy only (B) position only (C) momentum only (D) phase-space area.

Answer: D. The Jacobian matrix satisfies \(MJM^T=J\), so \(\det M=1\) and phase-space area (for one degree of freedom) or volume is preserved. Energy \(H\) becomes \(K\ne H\) in general; \(q\) and \(p\) are mixed. (Worked key, unofficial.)

2026-Q50: Poisson bracket \(\{A,H\}=0\)

Question. In Poisson-bracket algebra, if \(\{A,H\}=0\), the implication is: (A) \(A\) increases linearly (B) \(A\) is conserved (C) \(A\) oscillates (D) undefined.

Answer: B. \(\dot A=\{A,H\}+\partial_tA=0\) for a time-independent \(A\). (Worked key, unofficial.)

2026-Q55: action-angle variables

Question. For \(H=p^2/2m+V(q)\), if the transformation leads to action-angle variables, identify the condition on the motion: (A) non-periodic (B) chaotic (C) periodic and bounded (D) unstable.

Answer: C. Action-angle variables exist for bounded periodic (libration or rotation) motion, with \(J=\frac1{2\pi}\oint p\,dq\). (Worked key, unofficial. The Hindi option reads “periodic, bounded”.)

Quick revision

  • \(H=\sum p\dot q-L\), then eliminate \(\dot q\) using \(p=\partial L/\partial\dot q\).
  • \(\dot q=\partial H/\partial p\), \(\dot p=-\partial H/\partial q\); \(dH/dt=\partial H/\partial t\).
  • \(H=T+V\) iff scleronomic constraints and velocity-independent \(V\).
  • \(L=a\dot x^2+\dots\) gives \(p^2/4a\), not \(p^2/2a\).
  • Hamilton’s principle: \(\delta\int L\,dt=0\) with fixed end points; gives Lagrange’s equations.
  • \([q_i,p_j]=\delta_{ij}\); \([q,q]=[p,p]=0\); antisymmetric; Jacobi holds always.
  • \(\dot u=[u,H]+\partial_tu\); \([u,H]=0\) means \(u\) conserved; \([L_x,L_y]=L_z\); \([L^2,L_i]=0\).
  • Canonical iff \([Q,P]=1\) (one dof: \(\partial(Q,P)/\partial(q,p)=1\)); phase-space area/volume preserved.
  • \(F_1\): \(p=\partial_qF_1\), \(P=-\partial_QF_1\). \(F_2\): \(p=\partial_qF_2\), \(Q=\partial_PF_2\). \(K=H+\partial_tF\).
  • \(F_2=\sum q_jP_j\) is the identity; \(F_1=\sum q_jQ_j\) swaps \(q\to p\).
  • Action variable \(J=\frac1{2\pi}\oint p\,dq\) needs bounded periodic motion; \(\omega=\partial H/\partial J\).
