Why it matters for the exam

The syllabus names the motion of a sphere in a perfect fluid, the motion of liquid past a sphere, and the Navier-Stokes equations for viscous flows. The 2026 paper asked a conceptual question on what viscosity does to the zero drag of a sphere in inviscid flow (Q61). Likely forms: the Stokes stream function and velocity potential of a doublet or of flow past a sphere, the maximum surface speed, the kinetic energy of the liquid (added mass), the Navier-Stokes equation, the Reynolds number, and the three standard viscous flows.

Core ideas

Axisymmetric flow and the Stokes stream function

Take spherical coordinates \((r,\theta,\phi)\) with the axis along \(\theta=0\). In axisymmetric flow nothing depends on \(\phi\) and \(q_\phi=0\). Continuity for an incompressible fluid becomes

\[\frac1{r^2}\frac{\partial(r^2q_r)}{\partial r}+\frac1{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial(q_\theta\sin\theta)}{\partial\theta}=0 .\]

This is satisfied identically by the Stokes stream function \(\psi(r,\theta)\):

\[q_r=-\frac1{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\theta},\qquad q_\theta=\frac1{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial r}.\]

(In cylindrical coordinates \((\varpi,\phi,z)\) with \(\varpi=r\sin\theta\): \(q_\varpi=\dfrac1\varpi\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial z}\), \(q_z=-\dfrac1\varpi\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial\varpi}\). Some texts use the opposite sign.)

  • Surfaces \(\psi=\text{const}\) are stream surfaces (surfaces of revolution). A fixed rigid surface of revolution has \(\psi\) constant on it.
  • The volume flux between two stream surfaces \(\psi_1,\psi_2\) is \(2\pi\lvert\psi_1-\psi_2\rvert\) (the factor \(2\pi\) distinguishes it from the 2-D case).
  • For irrotational flow the potential satisfies \(\nabla^2\phi=0\) (axisymmetric Laplace equation) and \(\psi\) satisfies \(E^2\psi=0\), with
\[E^2=\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r^2}+\frac{\sin\theta}{r^2}\frac\partial{\partial\theta}\left(\frac1{\sin\theta}\frac\partial{\partial\theta}\right).\]

Unlike 2-D, \(\psi\) is not harmonic in the usual sense. The two functions are linked by \(\psi_\theta=r^2\sin\theta\,\phi_r\) and \(\psi_r=-\sin\theta\,\phi_\theta\) (for \(\mathbf q=-\nabla\phi\)).

Basic axisymmetric flows (\(\mathbf q=-\nabla\phi\), axis along \(\theta=0\))

Flow \(\phi\) \(\psi\) Velocity
Uniform stream \(U\) along the axis \(-Ur\cos\theta\) \(-\tfrac12Ur^2\sin^2\theta\) \(q_r=U\cos\theta\), \(q_\theta=-U\sin\theta\)
Point source of strength \(m\) (flux \(4\pi m\)) \(m/r\) \(m\cos\theta\) \(q_r=m/r^2\)
Point sink \(-m/r\) \(-m\cos\theta\)
Doublet of strength \(\mu\) \(\mu\cos\theta/r^2\) \(-\mu\sin^2\theta/r\) \(q_r=2\mu\cos\theta/r^3\), \(q_\theta=\mu\sin\theta/r^3\)

The doublet is the limit of a source \(m\) and a sink \(m\) at distance \(2\varepsilon\) with \(\mu=2m\varepsilon\) fixed, axis from sink to source. Its speed is \(\dfrac{\mu}{r^3}\sqrt{1+3\cos^2\theta}\). In 3-D source and doublet fall off as \(1/r^2\) and \(1/r^3\), faster than in 2-D.

Sphere moving in a perfect fluid

A sphere of radius \(a\) moves with velocity \(U\) along the axis through liquid at rest at infinity. Look for a doublet potential \(\phi=\mu\cos\theta/r^2\), which decays at infinity. The boundary condition is that the normal fluid velocity equals the normal velocity of the sphere’s surface: \(q_r=U\cos\theta\) at \(r=a\). Since \(q_r=2\mu\cos\theta/r^3\), \(\mu=\tfrac12Ua^3\):

\[\phi=\frac{Ua^3\cos\theta}{2r^2},\qquad\psi=-\frac{Ua^3\sin^2\theta}{2r},\qquad q_r=\frac{Ua^3\cos\theta}{r^3},\quad q_\theta=\frac{Ua^3\sin\theta}{2r^3}.\]

On the surface \(q_\theta=\tfrac12U\sin\theta\).

Kinetic energy. \(q^2=\dfrac{\mu^2}{r^6}(1+3\cos^2\theta)\), so

\[T=\tfrac12\rho\int q^2dV=\tfrac12\rho\,\mu^2\,2\pi\int_a^\infty\frac{dr}{r^4}\int_0^\pi(1+3\cos^2\theta)\sin\theta\,d\theta=\tfrac12\rho\mu^2\,2\pi\cdot\frac1{3a^3}\cdot4=\frac{4\pi\rho\mu^2}{3a^3}.\]

With \(\mu=\tfrac12Ua^3\): \(T=\dfrac\pi3\rho a^3U^2\). (Equivalently \(T=-\tfrac12\rho\oint\phi\,\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial n}\,dS\) over the sphere, with \(n\) the normal drawn out of the fluid, that is into the sphere.)

Added (virtual) mass. Write \(T=\tfrac12M'U^2\). Then

\[M'=\frac{2\pi}3\rho a^3=\tfrac12\Big(\tfrac43\pi a^3\rho\Big),\]

half the mass of the displaced fluid. If the sphere has mass \(M\) the total kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12(M+M')U^2\).

Force. From the energy equation, the force the fluid exerts on the sphere is \(-M'\,dU/dt\). Check with pressure: at the surface the unsteady term contributes \(\tfrac12\rho a\dot U\cos\theta\) to the pressure and \(\oint p\cos\theta\,dS=\tfrac{2\pi}3\rho a^3\dot U\). At constant \(U\) the force is zero (d’Alembert’s paradox: a sphere moving uniformly in an infinite perfect fluid feels no drag). The equation of motion under an external force \(F\) is \((M+M')\dot U=F\).

For a sphere of density \(\sigma\) in liquid of density \(\rho\), released from rest in a perfect liquid: \((\sigma+\tfrac12\rho)\dot U=(\sigma-\rho)g\), taking buoyancy into account. A very light bubble (\(\sigma\to0\)) has initial upward acceleration \(2g\).

Cylinder for comparison: added mass per unit length is \(\pi\rho a^2\), equal to the displaced mass.

Liquid streaming past a fixed sphere

Uniform stream \(U\) along the axis at infinity past a fixed sphere \(r=a\). Superpose a uniform stream and a doublet and choose the doublet so that \(q_r=0\) at \(r=a\):

\[\phi=-U\cos\theta\left(r+\frac{a^3}{2r^2}\right),\qquad\psi=-\tfrac12U\sin^2\theta\left(r^2-\frac{a^3}r\right).\]
\[q_r=U\cos\theta\left(1-\frac{a^3}{r^3}\right),\qquad q_\theta=-U\sin\theta\left(1+\frac{a^3}{2r^3}\right).\]
  • \(\psi=0\) on \(r=a\) and on the axis: the sphere is part of the dividing stream surface.
  • On the surface \(q_r=0\) and \(q_\theta=-\tfrac32U\sin\theta\). Stagnation points at \(\theta=0,\pi\); maximum surface speed \(\tfrac32U\) at the equator \(\theta=\pi/2\).
  • Pressure from Bernoulli, \(p=p_\infty+\tfrac12\rho(U^2-q^2)\): on the sphere \(p=p_\infty+\tfrac12\rho U^2\left(1-\tfrac94\sin^2\theta\right)\), \(C_p=1-\tfrac94\sin^2\theta\). At the stagnation points \(p_\infty+\tfrac12\rho U^2\); at the equator \(p_\infty-\tfrac58\rho U^2\). The pressure is symmetric fore and aft, so the drag is zero: \(\int_0^\pi\left(1-\tfrac94\sin^2\theta\right)\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta=0\).
  • Comparison with the cylinder: surface speed \(2U\lvert\sin\theta\rvert\) and \(C_p=1-4\sin^2\theta\); the sphere has the milder values \(\tfrac32U\) and \(-\tfrac54\) at the equator, because it can be bypassed in three dimensions.

Viscous fluid: stress and rate of strain

In a viscous fluid the stress across a surface has a tangential part. A Newtonian fluid has stress linear in the rate of strain \(e_{ij}=\tfrac12\left(\partial_iq_j+\partial_jq_i\right)\) and isotropic:

\[\sigma_{ij}=-p\,\delta_{ij}+2\mu\,e_{ij}-\tfrac23\mu\,(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q)\,\delta_{ij}.\]

For an incompressible fluid \(\sigma_{ij}=-p\,\delta_{ij}+2\mu e_{ij}\), i.e.

\[\sigma_{xx}=-p+2\mu\frac{\partial u}{\partial x},\qquad\sigma_{xy}=\mu\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\right),\]

and for a simple shear \(u=u(y)\) the shear stress is \(\tau=\mu\,du/dy\). \(\mu\) is the dynamic viscosity (Pa s), \(\nu=\mu/\rho\) the kinematic viscosity (m\(^2\)/s). The coefficient \(-\tfrac23\mu\) is Stokes’ hypothesis (zero bulk viscosity). In a perfect fluid \(\mu=0\).

Navier-Stokes equations

Newton’s second law with \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\) in place of \(-\nabla p\) gives, for constant \(\mu\),

\[\rho\frac{D\mathbf q}{Dt}=\rho\mathbf F-\nabla p+\mu\nabla^2\mathbf q+\tfrac13\mu\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q).\]

For an incompressible fluid (\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0\)) of constant \(\mu\):

\[\boxed{\ \frac{\partial\mathbf q}{\partial t}+(\mathbf q\cdot\nabla)\mathbf q=\mathbf F-\frac1\rho\nabla p+\nu\nabla^2\mathbf q\ },\qquad\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0.\]

In Cartesian components the \(x\)-equation is \(\dfrac{Du}{Dt}=F_x-\dfrac1\rho\dfrac{\partial p}{\partial x}+\nu\left(\dfrac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}+\dfrac{\partial^2u}{\partial y^2}+\dfrac{\partial^2u}{\partial z^2}\right)\). For axial flow \(w(R)\) in a pipe the \(z\)-equation viscous term is \(\nu\left(w''+w'/R\right)\).

Remarks:

  • The only new term compared with Euler’s equation is \(\nu\nabla^2\mathbf q\) (the diffusion of momentum).
  • The identity \(\nabla^2\mathbf q=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q)-\nabla\times\boldsymbol\omega\) shows that for incompressible flow the viscous force is \(-\mu\nabla\times\boldsymbol\omega\). Hence an incompressible irrotational flow satisfies the Navier-Stokes equations with zero viscous force, but it cannot satisfy the no-slip condition at a wall.
  • Boundary condition. At a solid wall the fluid velocity equals the wall velocity (no slip): both tangential and normal components. A perfect fluid only needs the normal component.
  • The curl of the equation gives the vorticity equation \(\dfrac{D\boldsymbol\omega}{Dt}=(\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\nabla)\mathbf q+\nu\nabla^2\boldsymbol\omega\) (for conservative \(\mathbf F\) and constant \(\rho\)). Viscosity diffuses vorticity.

Reynolds number and its consequences

\[\mathrm{Re}=\frac{\rho UL}{\mu}=\frac{UL}{\nu}=\frac{\text{inertial force}}{\text{viscous force}}.\]

In non-dimensional variables the equation is \(D\mathbf q^*/Dt^*=-\nabla p^*+\mathrm{Re}^{-1}\nabla^2\mathbf q^*\). Two flows of geometrically similar bodies with the same \(\mathrm{Re}\) are dynamically similar.

  • Small \(\mathrm{Re}\) (creeping or Stokes flow, \(\mathrm{Re}\lesssim1\)): inertia negligible. Drag on a sphere of radius \(a\): \(D=6\pi\mu aU\) (Stokes). A sphere of density \(\sigma\) falling in fluid of density \(\rho\) reaches terminal speed \(U=\dfrac{2a^2(\sigma-\rho)g}{9\mu}\), from \(6\pi\mu aU=\tfrac43\pi a^3(\sigma-\rho)g\).
  • Large \(\mathrm{Re}\): viscous effects confined to a thin boundary layer near the body and a wake; outside it the flow is nearly the perfect-fluid solution. Pipe flow becomes turbulent for \(\mathrm{Re}\) (based on diameter) above roughly \(2000\) to \(2300\).
  • d’Alembert’s paradox and viscosity. The perfect-fluid solution gives zero drag on a sphere (or cylinder) moving steadily. Any real fluid, however small \(\mu\), gives finite drag: skin friction plus pressure drag from the separated wake. At low Reynolds number it is Stokes’ \(6\pi\mu aU\). Drag does not vanish as \(\mu\to0\).

Stokes flow past a sphere (creeping flow)

For \(\mathrm{Re}\ll1\) the inertia terms are dropped: \(\mu\nabla^2\mathbf q=\nabla p\), \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0\). In terms of the Stokes stream function this is \(E^4\psi=0\). For a uniform stream \(U\) along the axis past a fixed sphere of radius \(a\) (no slip: \(\mathbf q=0\) at \(r=a\)) the solution, in the sign convention of this note, is

\[\psi=-\tfrac12U\sin^2\theta\left(r^2-\frac{3ar}2+\frac{a^3}{2r}\right).\]

The velocity components are \(q_r=U\cos\theta\left(1-\dfrac{3a}{2r}+\dfrac{a^3}{2r^3}\right)\) and \(q_\theta=-U\sin\theta\left(1-\dfrac{3a}{4r}-\dfrac{a^3}{4r^3}\right)\); both vanish at \(r=a\), and both tend to the uniform stream far away. The pressure is \(p=p_\infty-\dfrac{3\mu aU}{2}\dfrac{\cos\theta}{r^2}\), higher at the front stagnation point (\(\theta=\pi\)). On the surface the pressure force along the stream is \(2\pi\mu aU\) and the viscous shear force is \(4\pi\mu aU\), which add to the drag

\[D=6\pi\mu aU.\]

The perfect-fluid solution (zero drag, no-slip violated) and this solution are two different limits. Trap: the Stokes solution is not uniformly valid far from the sphere (the neglected inertia terms matter at distances of order \(a/\mathrm{Re}\); Oseen’s correction repairs this), and it cannot be used for \(\mathrm{Re}\gtrsim1\).

Exact solutions: parallel flows

For steady unidirectional flow \(\mathbf q=(u(y),0,0)\) continuity holds automatically, the convective term \(u\,\partial u/\partial x\) vanishes, the \(y\)-equation gives \(\partial p/\partial y=0\) (no body force) so \(p=p(x)\), and the \(x\)-equation reduces to the linear equation

\[\mu\frac{d^2u}{dy^2}=\frac{dp}{dx}=-G,\qquad G=-\frac{dp}{dx}=\text{const}.\]

Plane Couette flow. Fixed plate \(y=0\), plate \(y=h\) moving with speed \(U\), \(dp/dx=0\):

\[u=\frac{Uy}h,\qquad\tau=\mu\frac Uh\ (\text{uniform}).\]

With a pressure gradient (generalised Couette flow): \(u=\dfrac{Uy}h+\dfrac G{2\mu}y(h-y)\). Back-flow near the lower plate occurs if \(G<-2\mu U/h^2\).

Plane Poiseuille flow. Both plates fixed. Plates at \(y=\pm h\):

\[u=\frac G{2\mu}(h^2-y^2),\quad u_{\max}=\frac{Gh^2}{2\mu},\quad\bar u=\frac23u_{\max}=\frac{Gh^2}{3\mu},\quad Q=2h\bar u=\frac{2Gh^3}{3\mu},\quad\tau_w=Gh.\]

For plates at \(y=0\) and \(y=d\) (width \(d=2h\)): \(u=\dfrac G{2\mu}y(d-y)\), \(u_{\max}=\dfrac{Gd^2}{8\mu}\), \(\bar u=\dfrac{Gd^2}{12\mu}\), \(Q=\dfrac{Gd^3}{12\mu}\) per unit width. The profile is a parabola.

Hagen-Poiseuille flow in a circular pipe of radius \(a\). Axial flow \(w=w(R)\) only; the radial and azimuthal Navier-Stokes components give \(p=p(z)\), and the axial one gives

\[\frac\mu R\frac d{dR}\left(R\frac{dw}{dR}\right)=\frac{dp}{dz}=-G .\]

Integrate and require \(w\) finite at \(R=0\) and \(w(a)=0\):

\[w=\frac G{4\mu}(a^2-R^2).\]
  • Maximum speed (axis): \(w_{\max}=\dfrac{Ga^2}{4\mu}\); mean speed \(\bar w=\tfrac12w_{\max}=\dfrac{Ga^2}{8\mu}\).
  • Volume flux (Poiseuille’s law): \(Q=\displaystyle\int_0^aw\,2\pi R\,dR=\dfrac{\pi Ga^4}{8\mu}=\dfrac{\pi a^4(p_1-p_2)}{8\mu L}\). Flux is proportional to \(a^4\).
  • Wall shear stress \(\tau_w=\mu\lvert w'(a)\rvert=\dfrac{Ga}2\); drag on a length \(L\) of wall: \(\tau_w\cdot2\pi aL=\pi a^2LG=\pi a^2\Delta p\), balancing the pressure force.
  • Pressure drop for a given flux: \(\Delta p=\dfrac{8\mu LQ}{\pi a^4}\).

Pipe versus channel means: pipe \(\bar w=\tfrac12w_{\max}\); plane channel \(\bar u=\tfrac23u_{\max}\).

Film flowing down an inclined plane. A liquid film of thickness \(h\) runs down a plane inclined at \(\alpha\) to the horizontal. The effective driving force per unit volume is \(\rho g\sin\alpha\), the plane is no-slip and the free surface is stress free: \(\mu u''=-\rho g\sin\alpha\), \(u(0)=0\), \(u'(h)=0\). Hence

\[u=\frac{\rho g\sin\alpha}{2\mu}\,y(2h-y),\qquad u_{\max}=\frac{\rho gh^2\sin\alpha}{2\mu},\qquad\bar u=\frac23u_{\max},\qquad Q=\frac{\rho gh^3\sin\alpha}{3\mu}.\]

It is half of a plane Poiseuille profile (the free surface is the symmetry plane).

Flow between rotating coaxial cylinders. Inner radius \(a\) with angular speed \(\Omega_1\), outer radius \(b\) with \(\Omega_2\). Steady circular flow \(q_\theta=v(r)\) has \(\dfrac{d}{dr}\left[\dfrac1r\dfrac{d(rv)}{dr}\right]=0\), so \(v=Ar+B/r\) with

\[A=\frac{\Omega_2b^2-\Omega_1a^2}{b^2-a^2},\qquad B=\frac{(\Omega_1-\Omega_2)a^2b^2}{b^2-a^2}.\]

The shear stress is \(\tau_{r\theta}=\mu r\,\dfrac{d}{dr}(v/r)=-2\mu B/r^2\), so the torque per unit length on the inner cylinder has magnitude \(4\pi\mu\lvert B\rvert\). Rigid rotation (\(\Omega_1=\Omega_2\)) has \(B=0\) and no stress. If the inner cylinder is fixed and the outer rotates at \(\Omega_2\), the torque is \(4\pi\mu\Omega_2a^2b^2/(b^2-a^2)\) per unit length.

Stokes’ first problem (impulsively started plate). A plate at \(y=0\) is suddenly moved with speed \(U\) in a fluid at rest. The velocity obeys the diffusion equation \(u_t=\nu u_{yy}\), with solution

\[u=U\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{y}{2\sqrt{\nu t}}\right),\qquad\tau_w=\frac{\mu U}{\sqrt{\pi\nu t}}.\]

The viscous layer thickens like \(\sqrt{\nu t}\) and the wall stress falls like \(t^{-1/2}\). Trap: this flow has \(\partial u/\partial t\ne0\), so unlike Couette and Poiseuille it is not steady.

Dissipation. Viscous work heats the fluid at the rate \(\Phi=2\mu e_{ij}e_{ij}\) per unit volume; in plane Couette flow \(\Phi=\mu(U/h)^2\).

Formulas and results

  • Stokes stream function: \(q_r=-\dfrac1{r^2\sin\theta}\psi_\theta\), \(q_\theta=\dfrac1{r\sin\theta}\psi_r\); flux between surfaces \(=2\pi\Delta\psi\).
  • Uniform stream: \(\phi=-Ur\cos\theta\), \(\psi=-\tfrac12Ur^2\sin^2\theta\). Source: \(\phi=m/r\), \(\psi=m\cos\theta\). Doublet: \(\phi=\mu\cos\theta/r^2\), \(\psi=-\mu\sin^2\theta/r\).
  • Moving sphere: \(\phi=Ua^3\cos\theta/(2r^2)\); \(T=\dfrac\pi3\rho a^3U^2\); \(M'=\dfrac{2\pi}3\rho a^3=\tfrac12M_{\text{displaced}}\); force \(-M'\dot U\); zero drag at constant \(U\).
  • Fixed sphere: \(\phi=-U\cos\theta(r+a^3/2r^2)\), surface speed \(\tfrac32U\sin\theta\), \(C_p=1-\tfrac94\sin^2\theta\), minimum \(p_\infty-\tfrac58\rho U^2\).
  • Newtonian stress: \(\sigma_{ij}=-p\delta_{ij}+2\mu e_{ij}\) (incompressible); \(\tau=\mu\,du/dy\).
  • Navier-Stokes: \(D\mathbf q/Dt=\mathbf F-\nabla p/\rho+\nu\nabla^2\mathbf q\), \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0\).
  • \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho UL/\mu\); Stokes drag \(6\pi\mu aU\); terminal speed \(2a^2(\sigma-\rho)g/(9\mu)\).
  • Couette: \(u=Uy/h\). Plane Poiseuille: \(u=\dfrac G{2\mu}(h^2-y^2)\), \(\bar u=\tfrac23u_{\max}\).
  • Hagen-Poiseuille: \(w=\dfrac G{4\mu}(a^2-R^2)\), \(Q=\dfrac{\pi Ga^4}{8\mu}\), \(\bar w=\tfrac12w_{\max}\).

Worked examples

Example 1: velocity field for the fixed sphere

Verify from the Stokes stream function \(\psi=-\tfrac12U\sin^2\theta(r^2-a^3/r)\) that the sphere is impermeable and find the speed at the equator.

\(q_r=-\dfrac1{r^2\sin\theta}\psi_\theta=-\dfrac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\left(-U\sin\theta\cos\theta\,(r^2-a^3/r)\right)=U\cos\theta\,(1-a^3/r^3)\), which is \(0\) at \(r=a\). \(q_\theta=\dfrac1{r\sin\theta}\psi_r=-U\sin\theta\,(1+a^3/2r^3)\), which equals \(-\tfrac32U\sin\theta\) at \(r=a\). At the equator the speed is \(\tfrac32U\).

Example 2: pressure on the fixed sphere

Water (\(\rho=1000\) kg/m\(^3\)) flows at \(U=2\) m/s. Pressure at the front stagnation point is \(p_\infty+\tfrac12\rho U^2=p_\infty+2000\) Pa; at the equator \(q=\tfrac32U\), so \(p=p_\infty+\tfrac12\rho\left(U^2-\tfrac94U^2\right)=p_\infty-\tfrac58\rho U^2=p_\infty-2500\) Pa. The pressure difference between stagnation point and equator is \(\tfrac98\rho U^2=4500\) Pa.

Example 3: kinetic energy and added mass

A sphere of radius \(0.1\) m moves at \(2\) m/s in water. \(T=\dfrac\pi3\rho a^3U^2=\dfrac\pi3(1000)(0.001)(4)=4.19\) J. The added mass is \(\dfrac{2\pi}3\rho a^3=2.09\) kg, half the \(4.19\) kg of displaced water. If the sphere’s own mass is \(M\), the force needed to accelerate it at \(\dot U\) is \((M+2.09)\dot U\).

Example 4: sphere released in a perfect liquid

A sphere of density \(3\rho\) is released from rest. \((\sigma+\tfrac12\rho)\dot U=(\sigma-\rho)g\) gives \(\dot U=\dfrac{2\rho g}{3.5\rho}=\dfrac47g\approx0.571\,g\), against \(\tfrac23g\) if added mass is ignored.

Example 5: Hagen-Poiseuille flow

Oil of viscosity \(\mu=0.1\) Pa s and density \(900\) kg/m\(^3\) flows through a pipe of radius \(a=0.01\) m, length \(L=5\) m, at \(Q=10^{-5}\) m\(^3\)/s. Then \(\Delta p=\dfrac{8\mu LQ}{\pi a^4}=\dfrac{8(0.1)(5)(10^{-5})}{\pi(10^{-8})}=1273\) Pa. Mean speed \(=Q/\pi a^2=0.0318\) m/s, maximum speed (axis) \(=0.0637\) m/s, \(G=\Delta p/L=254.6\) Pa/m. \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho\bar w(2a)/\mu=900(0.0318)(0.02)/0.1=5.7\), so the flow is laminar. Wall shear \(\tau_w=Ga/2=1.27\) Pa.

Example 6: Couette and Poiseuille in a channel

(a) Couette: \(\mu=0.002\) Pa s, gap \(1\) mm, upper plate at \(3\) m/s: \(\tau=\mu U/h=0.002\times3/0.001=6\) Pa, uniform across the gap. (b) Plane Poiseuille, plates \(d=0.01\) m apart, \(G=800\) Pa/m, \(\mu=0.1\) Pa s: \(u_{\max}=Gd^2/(8\mu)=0.1\) m/s, \(\bar u=Gd^2/(12\mu)=0.0667\) m/s, \(Q=\bar ud=6.67\times10^{-4}\) m\(^2\)/s per unit width, \(\tau_w=Gd/2=4\) Pa.

Example 7: Stokes terminal velocity

A sphere of radius \(1\) mm and density \(2500\) kg/m\(^3\) falls in a liquid of density \(1000\) kg/m\(^3\) and viscosity \(0.1\) Pa s. \(U=\dfrac{2a^2(\sigma-\rho)g}{9\mu}=\dfrac{2(10^{-6})(1500)(9.8)}{0.9}=0.0327\) m/s. Check: \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho U(2a)/\mu=0.65<1\), so Stokes’ law applies.

Common traps

  • Sphere flows are \(1/r^2\) and \(1/r^3\), not \(1/r\) and \(1/r^2\) as in 2-D. Stokes stream function has a factor \(r^2\sin\theta\), not \(r\).
  • Added mass of a sphere is half the displaced mass (\(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac43\pi a^3\rho\)); for a cylinder it equals the displaced mass.
  • Kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12M'U^2\) with \(M'=\tfrac23\pi\rho a^3\), giving \(\tfrac\pi3\rho a^3U^2\). Using the full displaced mass gives twice the correct value.
  • Max surface speed on a fixed sphere is \(\tfrac32U\), not \(2U\) (cylinder) and not \(U\).
  • Perfect-fluid drag is zero for both a moving and a fixed sphere; this does not survive adding viscosity. Q61 asks exactly this. Correct answer: drag becomes finite, not infinite and not still zero.
  • The Navier-Stokes viscous term is \(\mu\nabla^2\mathbf q\) per unit volume, \(\nu\nabla^2\mathbf q\) per unit mass. Mixing the two breaks the units.
  • Potential flow solves Navier-Stokes but violates no-slip. Irrotational incompressible flow has no viscous force.
  • Reynolds number uses the stated length: diameter for pipes. Radius gives half the value.
  • Hagen-Poiseuille \(Q\propto a^4\) and the mean speed is half (not two-thirds) of the maximum. For plane channels the mean is two-thirds of the maximum.
  • Couette flow is linear; Poiseuille flow is parabolic.
  • Stokes drag is \(6\pi\mu aU\), not \(4\pi\mu aU\) or \(2\pi\mu aU\) (those are the pressure and viscous-stress parts, \(2\pi\mu aU\) and \(4\pi\mu aU\), which add up to \(6\pi\mu aU\)).

2026 Q61 (worked key B; confidence high)

Question: A sphere moves through an inviscid fluid; a small viscosity is now introduced (Navier-Stokes equations). What is the qualitative correction to the drag? (A) remains zero (B) becomes finite (C) becomes infinite (D) oscillatory. Answer: B, becomes finite. Solution: in inviscid potential flow past a sphere the drag is zero (d’Alembert’s paradox). Even a very small viscosity produces a boundary layer, separation and a wake, and a finite drag; at low Reynolds number it is Stokes’ \(6\pi\mu aU\). This is a 2026 worked-key answer (not an official key). The overrides file records only confidence “high” for this question, with no defect note. The worked key mentions the wording could be read as purely conceptual, but B is the standard answer.

Quick revision

  • Stokes flow: \(E^4\psi=0\); \(\psi=-\tfrac12U\sin^2\theta\,(r^2-3ar/2+a^3/2r)\); pressure force \(2\pi\mu aU\) plus viscous \(4\pi\mu aU\) gives \(6\pi\mu aU\).
  • Film on an incline: \(u_{\max}=\rho gh^2\sin\alpha/2\mu\), \(\bar u=\tfrac23u_{\max}\). Rotating cylinders: \(v=Ar+B/r\).
  • Stokes’ first problem: \(u=U\operatorname{erfc}(y/2\sqrt{\nu t})\), layer thickness \(\sim\sqrt{\nu t}\).
  • Axisymmetric: \(q_r=-\psi_\theta/(r^2\sin\theta)\), \(q_\theta=\psi_r/(r\sin\theta)\); flux \(=2\pi\Delta\psi\).
  • Uniform stream \(\psi=-\tfrac12Ur^2\sin^2\theta\); source \(\psi=m\cos\theta\); doublet \(\psi=-\mu\sin^2\theta/r\).
  • Moving sphere: \(\phi=Ua^3\cos\theta/(2r^2)\), \(T=\tfrac\pi3\rho a^3U^2\), added mass \(\tfrac23\pi\rho a^3\) (half the displaced mass).
  • Fixed sphere in stream: surface speed \(\tfrac32U\sin\theta\), max \(\tfrac32U\); \(C_p=1-\tfrac94\sin^2\theta\); drag zero.
  • Newtonian: \(\tau=\mu\,du/dy\); incompressible stress \(-p\delta_{ij}+2\mu e_{ij}\).
  • Navier-Stokes: \(D\mathbf q/Dt=\mathbf F-\nabla p/\rho+\nu\nabla^2\mathbf q\); no-slip at walls.
  • \(\mathrm{Re}=\rho UL/\mu\) is the ratio of inertial to viscous force.
  • Stokes drag \(6\pi\mu aU\); viscosity makes sphere drag finite.
  • Couette: linear, \(u=Uy/h\). Plane Poiseuille: parabola, \(\bar u=\tfrac23u_{\max}\).
  • Hagen-Poiseuille: \(w=\dfrac G{4\mu}(a^2-R^2)\), \(Q=\dfrac{\pi Ga^4}{8\mu}\), \(\bar w=\tfrac12w_{\max}\).
