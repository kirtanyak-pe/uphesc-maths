Why it matters for the exam

Rigid-body questions are rare but direct: the degrees of freedom of a rigid body (2025-Q68, also in the Lagrangian topic) and the stability of torque-free rotation (2026-Q71). Expect one item on moments of inertia (parallel or perpendicular axis theorem), principal axes or Euler’s equations. The syllabus line names “Euler’s dynamical equations”, so know the equations, the symmetric top and the intermediate-axis result.

Core ideas

Degrees of freedom and Euler angles

A rigid body has 6 degrees of freedom: 3 for the centre of mass, 3 for orientation. With one point fixed there are 3; about a fixed axis, 1.

The orientation is fixed by three Euler angles \((\varphi,\theta,\psi)\) (the \(z\)-\(x\)-\(z\) convention): rotate by \(\varphi\) about the space \(z\)-axis, by \(\theta\) about the new \(x\)-axis (the line of nodes), by \(\psi\) about the new \(z\)-axis (the body symmetry axis).

Angle Name Range
\(\varphi\) precession \(0\le\varphi<2\pi\)
\(\theta\) nutation (tilt of the body axis from the space \(z\)-axis) \(0\le\theta\le\pi\)
\(\psi\) spin \(0\le\psi<2\pi\)

Components of the angular velocity along the body axes:

\[\omega_1=\dot\varphi\sin\theta\sin\psi+\dot\theta\cos\psi,\]
\[\omega_2=\dot\varphi\sin\theta\cos\psi-\dot\theta\sin\psi,\]
\[\omega_3=\dot\varphi\cos\theta+\dot\psi.\]

Three angles are used because any rotation matrix is fixed by three parameters. The orientation is the orthogonal matrix \(\mathsf R=\mathsf R_z(\psi)\,\mathsf R_x(\theta)\,\mathsf R_z(\varphi)\) taking space components to body components, with \(\mathsf R^T\mathsf R=\mathbf 1\) and \(\det\mathsf R=+1\), where

\[\mathsf R_z(\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha&\sin\alpha&0\\-\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}.\]

Finite rotations about different axes do not commute, but angular velocities add as vectors; this is why \(\boldsymbol\omega\) is a sum of the three rates \(\dot\varphi,\dot\theta,\dot\psi\) along their own axes. Velocity of a point: \(\mathbf v=\mathbf V+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r\).

Moments and products of inertia, inertia tensor

For a body of mass elements \(m_\alpha\) at \((x_\alpha,y_\alpha,z_\alpha)\) (or a continuous body, replace sums by \(\int\,dm\)):

\[I_{xx}=\sum m(y^2+z^2),\quad I_{yy}=\sum m(z^2+x^2),\quad I_{zz}=\sum m(x^2+y^2),\]
\[I_{xy}=-\sum mxy,\quad I_{yz}=-\sum myz,\quad I_{zx}=-\sum mzx.\]

The first three are moments of inertia; the others are products of inertia (some books omit the minus sign in the definition and put it in the matrix: be careful). They form the symmetric inertia tensor

\[\mathsf I=\begin{pmatrix}I_{xx}&I_{xy}&I_{xz}\\I_{xy}&I_{yy}&I_{yz}\\I_{xz}&I_{yz}&I_{zz}\end{pmatrix}.\]

In vector form \(I_{ij}=\sum m\,(r^2\delta_{ij}-x_ix_j)\). The moment of inertia about an axis through the origin with unit direction \(\mathbf n\) is

\[I_n=\mathbf n^T\mathsf I\,\mathbf n=\sum m\,\rho_\perp^2.\]

The tensor is symmetric and positive definite, so it has three real positive eigenvalues \(I_1,I_2,I_3\) (the principal moments) and three orthogonal eigenvectors (the principal axes). In the principal-axes frame all products of inertia vanish and \(\mathsf I=\mathrm{diag}(I_1,I_2,I_3)\). The principal moments are the roots of \(\det(\mathsf I-\lambda\mathbf 1)=0\).

Properties:

  • Triangle inequality: \(I_1\le I_2+I_3\) (and cyclic). Equality holds for a planar body.
  • Symmetry axes are principal axes. A body with an axis of \(n\)-fold symmetry (\(n\ge3\)) has equal moments about the two perpendicular axes (a symmetric top). A sphere or cube about its centre has \(I_1=I_2=I_3\) (spherical top).
  • Principal axes for a body with a plane of symmetry: the normal to the plane is a principal axis.
  • The moment-of-inertia ellipsoid: \(\mathbf x^T\mathsf I\mathbf x=1\). Its axes are the principal axes, with semi-axes \(1/\sqrt{I_k}\).

Parallel and perpendicular axis theorems

Parallel axis (Huygens-Steiner). If the axis through the centre of mass has moment \(I_{cm}\), a parallel axis at distance \(d\) has

\[I=I_{cm}+Md^2.\]

For the full tensor: \(I_{ij}=I^{cm}_{ij}+M\,(a^2\delta_{ij}-a_ia_j)\), where \(\mathbf a\) is the vector from the centre of mass to the new origin. For a product of inertia, \(I_{xy}=I^{cm}_{xy}-Mab\).

Perpendicular axis. For a planar lamina (flat body) in the \(xy\)-plane,

\[I_z=I_x+I_y.\]

It applies only to plane laminae, with all three axes through the same point. Use it together with symmetry: a uniform disc has \(I_x=I_y\), so \(I_{\mathrm{diameter}}=\tfrac12I_z\).

Angular momentum and kinetic energy

For rotation with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega\) about a fixed point (or about the centre of mass):

\[\mathbf L=\mathsf I\boldsymbol\omega,\qquad T_{\mathrm{rot}}=\tfrac12\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathsf I\boldsymbol\omega=\tfrac12\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf L.\]

In principal axes \(L_k=I_k\omega_k\) and \(T=\tfrac12(I_1\omega_1^2+I_2\omega_2^2+I_3\omega_3^2)\). In general \(\mathbf L\) is not parallel to \(\boldsymbol\omega\): it is parallel only if \(\boldsymbol\omega\) lies along a principal axis, or the body is a spherical top.

For a body moving with centre-of-mass velocity \(\mathbf V\) (König theorem):

\[T=\tfrac12MV^2+\tfrac12\boldsymbol\omega^T\mathsf I_{cm}\boldsymbol\omega,\qquad\mathbf L=\mathbf R_{cm}\times M\mathbf V+\mathsf I_{cm}\boldsymbol\omega.\]

Rolling without slipping: \(v=\omega R\) and \(T=\tfrac12Mv^2\left(1+\dfrac{I_{cm}}{MR^2}\right)\). The acceleration down an incline is \(a=\dfrac{g\sin\alpha}{1+I_{cm}/MR^2}\): sphere \(\tfrac57g\sin\alpha\), solid cylinder \(\tfrac23g\sin\alpha\), hoop \(\tfrac12g\sin\alpha\).

Euler’s dynamical equations

The equation \(d\mathbf L/dt=\mathbf N\) holds in an inertial frame. In the body frame (rotating with \(\boldsymbol\omega\)), \(\left(\dfrac{d\mathbf L}{dt}\right)_{\mathrm{body}}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf L=\mathbf N\). With axes along the principal axes at the fixed point or centre of mass:

\[\boxed{\begin{aligned}I_1\dot\omega_1-(I_2-I_3)\,\omega_2\omega_3&=N_1,\\I_2\dot\omega_2-(I_3-I_1)\,\omega_3\omega_1&=N_2,\\I_3\dot\omega_3-(I_1-I_2)\,\omega_1\omega_2&=N_3.\end{aligned}}\]

Equivalent: \(I_1\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3+N_1\) and cyclic. Torque-free case \(N=0\) has two first integrals:

\[2T=I_1\omega_1^2+I_2\omega_2^2+I_3\omega_3^2=\text{const},\qquad L^2=I_1^2\omega_1^2+I_2^2\omega_2^2+I_3^2\omega_3^2=\text{const}.\]

(Energy ellipsoid and angular-momentum sphere in \(L\)-space; their intersection is the polhode.) Rotation about a principal axis with constant \(\omega\) is a solution: if \(\omega_2=\omega_3=0\), then \(\dot\omega_1=0\).

Torque-free symmetric top (\(I_1=I_2\ne I_3\))

The third equation gives \(\dot\omega_3=0\), so \(\omega_3\) is constant. The first two become

\[\dot\omega_1=-\Omega\,\omega_2,\qquad\dot\omega_2=\Omega\,\omega_1,\qquad\Omega=\frac{I_3-I_1}{I_1}\,\omega_3.\]

So \((\omega_1,\omega_2)\) rotates in the body frame with constant magnitude at angular rate \(\Omega\): \(\boldsymbol\omega\) precesses about the symmetry axis. In the space frame \(\mathbf L\) is fixed, \(\mathbf L\), \(\boldsymbol\omega\) and the symmetry axis are coplanar, the symmetry axis precesses about \(\mathbf L\) at

\[\dot\varphi=\frac{L}{I_1}\quad(\theta\ \text{constant}),\]

with \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{I_3\omega_3}{L}\). Direction of body-frame precession: same sense as the spin for an oblate body (\(I_3>I_1\)), opposite for a prolate body (\(I_3<I_1\)). The Earth is slightly oblate with \((I_3-I_1)/I_1\approx1/305\), which gives Euler’s free-wobble period of about 305 days (the observed Chandler wobble is about 433 days because the Earth is not perfectly rigid).

Stability of torque-free rotation about a principal axis

Take \(I_1<I_2<I_3\) and a rotation of rate \(\Omega\) about one principal axis, with small perturbations of the other two components. Linearising Euler’s equations:

Axis Perturbation equation Result
Axis 1 (smallest \(I\)) \(\ddot\omega_2=-\Omega^2\dfrac{(I_3-I_1)(I_2-I_1)}{I_2I_3}\,\omega_2\) oscillation: stable
Axis 2 (intermediate \(I\)) \(\ddot\omega_1=+\Omega^2\dfrac{(I_3-I_2)(I_2-I_1)}{I_1I_3}\,\omega_1\) exponential growth, rate \(\Omega\sqrt{\dfrac{(I_3-I_2)(I_2-I_1)}{I_1I_3}}\): unstable
Axis 3 (largest \(I\)) \(\ddot\omega_1=-\Omega^2\dfrac{(I_3-I_1)(I_3-I_2)}{I_1I_2}\,\omega_1\) oscillation: stable

Each row has the form \(\ddot\omega=K\omega\): \(K<0\) (oscillation) for axes 1 and 3, \(K>0\) (exponential growth) for axis 2. This is the “tennis racket theorem”: rotation about the intermediate axis is unstable, and the body flips over repeatedly. Rotation about the largest-moment axis is also stable under energy dissipation; rotation about the smallest-moment axis is stable for a rigid body but unstable for a body that dissipates energy (a satellite with internal friction ends up spinning about the largest-moment axis).

Rotating frames and the Coriolis force

For any vector \(\mathbf A\), the rates in the space (inertial) frame and in a frame rotating with angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega\) are related by

\[\left(\frac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\right)_{\text{space}}=\left(\frac{d\mathbf A}{dt}\right)_{\text{rot}}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf A.\]

Applying this twice, a particle’s acceleration seen in the rotating frame satisfies

\[m\mathbf a_{\text{rot}}=\mathbf F-2m\,\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v_{\text{rot}}-m\,\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)-m\dot{\boldsymbol\omega}\times\mathbf r.\]

The second term is the Coriolis force (perpendicular to \(\mathbf v_{\text{rot}}\), does no work), the third is the centrifugal force (directed away from the axis, magnitude \(m\omega^2\rho\)). Euler’s equations are exactly \(\dot{\mathbf L}=\mathbf N\) written in the rotating body frame.

Example: a stone dropped from rest through a height \(h\) at the equator is deflected eastwards by \(\tfrac13\Omega g t^3\) with \(t=\sqrt{2h/g}\) and \(\Omega=7.29\times10^{-5}\) rad/s. For \(h=100\) m, \(t=4.52\) s and the deflection is about \(22\) mm.

Compound pendulum

A rigid body of mass \(M\) swinging about a fixed horizontal axis at distance \(d\) above its centre of mass has \(L=\tfrac12I\dot\theta^2+Mgd\cos\theta\) with \(I=I_{cm}+Md^2\), so for small angles

\[T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{Mgd}}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l_{eq}}g},\qquad l_{eq}=\frac{I}{Md}=d+\frac{k^2}{d},\]

with \(k\) the radius of gyration about the centre of mass. The period is least when \(d=k\). For a uniform rod of length \(l\) pivoted at its end, \(I=\tfrac13Ml^2\), \(d=l/2\), so \(l_{eq}=\tfrac23l\).

Heavy symmetric top (basic)

A top with \(I_1=I_2\) and fixed point at distance \(l\) from the centre of mass has

\[L=\tfrac12I_1(\dot\theta^2+\dot\varphi^2\sin^2\theta)+\tfrac12I_3(\dot\psi+\dot\varphi\cos\theta)^2-Mgl\cos\theta.\]

\(\varphi\) and \(\psi\) are cyclic, so \(p_\varphi\) and \(p_\psi=I_3\omega_3\) are conserved, and so is energy. The steady precession rate for fast spin is \(\dot\varphi\approx\dfrac{Mgl}{I_3\omega_3}\).

Worked examples

Example 1: principal moments of a cube about a corner

A uniform cube of side \(a\) and mass \(M\) about a corner: \(I_{xx}=I_{yy}=I_{zz}=\tfrac23Ma^2\) and \(I_{xy}=I_{yz}=I_{zx}=-\tfrac14Ma^2\). In units of \(Ma^2\) the tensor is \(\begin{pmatrix}2/3&-1/4&-1/4\\-1/4&2/3&-1/4\\-1/4&-1/4&2/3\end{pmatrix}\). The body diagonal \((1,1,1)/\sqrt3\) is a principal axis with

\[I=\tfrac23+2\left(-\tfrac14\right)=\tfrac16.\]

The other two principal moments (any two perpendicular axes orthogonal to the diagonal) are \(\tfrac23+\tfrac14=\tfrac{11}{12}\) each. Check: trace \(=\tfrac16+2\cdot\tfrac{11}{12}=2=3\cdot\tfrac23\). The cube about a corner is a symmetric top.

Example 2: parallel and perpendicular axis theorems

  • Ring, axis tangent and perpendicular to the plane: \(MR^2+MR^2=2MR^2\).
  • Disc, axis tangent in the plane: \(\tfrac14MR^2+MR^2=\tfrac54MR^2\).
  • Square plate of side \(a\) about a diagonal: about the perpendicular axis through the centre \(I_z=\dfrac{M(a^2+a^2)}{12}=\dfrac{Ma^2}{6}\). By symmetry the two diagonals have equal moments, so \(I_{\mathrm{diag}}=\tfrac12I_z=\dfrac{Ma^2}{12}\).

Example 3: stability of rotation about the intermediate axis

\(I_1=1\), \(I_2=2\), \(I_3=3\) (kg m\(^2\)), spinning about axis 2 at \(\Omega=1\) rad/s. The perturbation grows like \(e^{\lambda t}\) with

\[\lambda=\Omega\sqrt{\frac{(3-2)(2-1)}{1\cdot3}}=\frac1{\sqrt3}\approx0.577\ \text{s}^{-1}.\]

(Checked by numerical integration of Euler’s equations.) About axis 1 with \(\Omega=1\) the perturbation oscillates with frequency \(\sqrt{\dfrac{(3-1)(2-1)}{2\cdot3}}=\dfrac1{\sqrt3}\), so axis 1 and 3 are stable.

Example 4: free symmetric top

\(I_1=I_2=2\), \(I_3=3\) (kg m\(^2\)), \(\omega_3=10\) rad/s. The body-frame precession rate is \(\Omega=\dfrac{3-2}{2}\cdot10=5\) rad/s. The component \(\omega_3\) stays \(10\) rad/s. With a small transverse \(\omega_\perp\), the angle \(\theta\) between \(\mathbf L\) and the symmetry axis is small: \(\tan\theta=\dfrac{I_1\omega_\perp}{I_3\omega_3}\). In the space frame the axis precesses at \(\dot\varphi=L/I_1\approx I_3\omega_3/I_1=15\) rad/s.

Example 5: rolling down an incline

A solid sphere, a solid cylinder and a hoop start together at rest on a \(30^\circ\) incline. \(a=\dfrac{g\sin\alpha}{1+k}\) with \(k=\tfrac25,\tfrac12,1\) gives \(a=\tfrac57\cdot\tfrac g2\), \(\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac g2\), \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac g2\). The sphere wins, then the cylinder, then the hoop, independent of mass and radius.

Example 6: principal moments from a tensor

For \(\mathsf I=\begin{pmatrix}3&-1&0\\-1&3&0\\0&0&5\end{pmatrix}\) (units \(ML^2\)) the block \(\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&3\end{pmatrix}\) has eigenvalues \(3\mp1\), so the principal moments are \(2,4,5\) with axes \((1,1,0)/\sqrt2\), \((1,-1,0)/\sqrt2\), \((0,0,1)\). Checks: trace \(=11=2+4+5\) and determinant \(=40=2\cdot4\cdot5\). The moment about the axis \((1,1,0)/\sqrt2\) is \(\mathbf n^T\mathsf I\mathbf n=\tfrac12(3-1-1+3)=2\).

Common traps

  • Free rigid body: 6; one point fixed: 3 (not 6 and not 2).
  • \(\mathbf L\ne I\boldsymbol\omega\) as a scalar equation. \(\mathbf L=\mathsf I\boldsymbol\omega\) is a matrix equation. \(\mathbf L\parallel\boldsymbol\omega\) only along a principal axis.
  • Intermediate axis is unstable; smallest and largest are stable. The 2026 question uses \(I_1<I_2<I_3\) and asks about the middle one: exponential divergence (tumbling), not stable oscillation, uniform precession or no change.
  • Perpendicular axis theorem is for planar laminae only. It fails for a sphere or a cylinder.
  • Parallel axis theorem needs one axis through the centre of mass. You cannot go directly from one off-centre axis to another.
  • Disc about a diameter is \(\tfrac14MR^2\), ring about a diameter is \(\tfrac12MR^2\), solid sphere \(\tfrac25MR^2\), hollow sphere \(\tfrac23MR^2\). Options are built from these swapped.
  • Rod about the end is \(Ml^2/3\), about the centre \(Ml^2/12\): the difference is \(M(l/2)^2\).
  • Signs in Euler’s equations: \(I_1\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3\). Swapping \((I_2-I_3)\) to \((I_3-I_2)\) is the common error.
  • Euler’s angles are not components of \(\boldsymbol\omega\). The \(\omega_k\) are combinations of \(\dot\varphi,\dot\theta,\dot\psi\).
  • Coriolis does no work and is perpendicular to the velocity; centrifugal is outward from the axis, not from the origin.
  • Compound pendulum: use \(I\) about the pivot (\(I_{cm}+Md^2\)), not \(I_{cm}\); the equivalent length is \(I/Md\), not \(d\).
  • Products of inertia have a minus sign in the standard definition.

Previous-year questions

2026-Q71: torque-free rotation about the intermediate axis

Question. A rigid body rotates torque-free with \(I_1<I_2<I_3\). If it is slightly perturbed from rotation about the intermediate axis, the long-term behaviour from Euler’s equations is: (A) stable oscillation (B) exponential divergence (C) uniform precession (D) no change.

Answer: B. Linearising about rotation at \(\Omega\) about axis 2 gives \(\ddot\omega_1=\Omega^2\dfrac{(I_3-I_2)(I_2-I_1)}{I_1I_3}\omega_1\) with a positive coefficient, so the perturbation grows exponentially. (Worked key, unofficial; no defect noted.)

Quick revision

  • Principal moments: eigenvalues of \(\mathsf I\) (trace = sum, det = product). Compound pendulum \(T=2\pi\sqrt{I/Mgd}\). Rotating frames: \(\mathbf a_{rot}=\mathbf a-2\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf v-\boldsymbol\omega\times(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)\).
  • Free rigid body 6 degrees of freedom; one point fixed 3 (Euler angles \(\varphi,\theta,\psi\)).
  • \(I_{xx}=\sum m(y^2+z^2)\), \(I_{xy}=-\sum mxy\); tensor symmetric, positive definite, principal axes orthogonal.
  • Parallel axis \(I=I_{cm}+Md^2\); perpendicular axis \(I_z=I_x+I_y\) (laminae only).
  • Rod \(Ml^2/12\) (centre), \(Ml^2/3\) (end); disc \(\tfrac12MR^2\); ring \(MR^2\); solid sphere \(\tfrac25MR^2\); shell \(\tfrac23MR^2\).
  • \(\mathbf L=\mathsf I\boldsymbol\omega\), \(T=\tfrac12\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf L\); \(\mathbf L\parallel\boldsymbol\omega\) only on a principal axis.
  • Euler: \(I_1\dot\omega_1-(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3=N_1\) and cyclic.
  • Torque-free symmetric top: \(\omega_3\) const, body-frame rate \(\frac{I_3-I_1}{I_1}\omega_3\), space-frame \(\dot\varphi=L/I_1\).
  • Torque-free: \(T\) and \(L^2\) are conserved.
  • Rotation about the smallest or largest principal axis is stable; about the intermediate axis it is unstable (tennis racket).
  • Rolling: \(a=g\sin\alpha/(1+I/MR^2)\); sphere \(\tfrac57\), cylinder \(\tfrac23\), hoop \(\tfrac12\) of \(g\sin\alpha\).
