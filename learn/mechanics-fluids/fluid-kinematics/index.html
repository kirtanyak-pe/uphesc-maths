Why it matters for the exam

Fluid kinematics is the most reliably asked part of the fluid syllabus. In 2025 three of the four fluid questions (Q67, Q70, Q72) came from here: the continuity equation in spherical symmetry, the continuity equation versus incompressibility, and the relations between velocity potential and stream function. Expect direct recall of the equation of continuity in some coordinate system, an assertion-reason on \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0\) versus \(\nabla\times\mathbf q=0\), or a check of whether a given field is a possible flow.

Core ideas

Two descriptions of motion

Lagrangian (material) description. Label each particle by its position \((a,b,c)\) at \(t=0\) and follow it: \(\mathbf r=\mathbf r(a,b,c,t)\). The velocity is \(\partial\mathbf r/\partial t\) with \((a,b,c)\) fixed.

Eulerian (field) description. Sit at a fixed point \(\mathbf r\) and record what passes: \(\mathbf q=\mathbf q(\mathbf r,t)\), \(p(\mathbf r,t)\), \(\rho(\mathbf r,t)\). This is the description used almost everywhere in the exam.

To go from Lagrangian to Eulerian, invert \(\mathbf r=\mathbf r(a,b,c,t)\) to get \((a,b,c)\) in terms of \((\mathbf r,t)\) and substitute. To go back, solve \(dx/dt=u\), \(dy/dt=v\), \(dz/dt=w\) with \(\mathbf r=(a,b,c)\) at \(t=0\).

Material derivative and acceleration

The rate of change of a quantity \(F\) following a fluid particle is \(\frac{DF}{Dt}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}+(\mathbf q\cdot\nabla)F=\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}+u\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}+w\frac{\partial F}{\partial z}.\) The first term is the local change, the second the convective change. The acceleration is \(\mathbf f=\frac{D\mathbf q}{Dt}=\frac{\partial\mathbf q}{\partial t}+(\mathbf q\cdot\nabla)\mathbf q=\frac{\partial\mathbf q}{\partial t}+\nabla\!\left(\tfrac12 q^2\right)-\mathbf q\times\boldsymbol\omega ,\) where \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf q\) (Lamb’s form, from the identity \((\mathbf q\cdot\nabla)\mathbf q=\nabla(\tfrac12q^2)-\mathbf q\times(\nabla\times\mathbf q)\)).

Term Meaning
Steady flow \(\partial/\partial t=0\) at every point; the pattern does not change with time
Uniform flow \(\mathbf q\) is the same at all points at one instant
Local acceleration \(\partial\mathbf q/\partial t\)
Convective acceleration \((\mathbf q\cdot\nabla)\mathbf q\), nonzero even in steady flow if \(\mathbf q\) varies in space

Streamlines, pathlines, streaklines

  • Streamline. A curve whose tangent at each point is the velocity at that instant. At fixed \(t\): \(\dfrac{dx}{u}=\dfrac{dy}{v}=\dfrac{dz}{w}\). Time is a constant parameter while integrating.
  • Pathline. The track of one particle: integrate \(\dfrac{dx}{dt}=u,\ \dfrac{dy}{dt}=v,\ \dfrac{dz}{dt}=w\) and eliminate \(t\).
  • Streakline. The locus, at one instant, of all particles that have passed through a given point (the smoke trail from a fixed nozzle).

In steady flow all three coincide. In unsteady flow they are in general different curves. A stream tube is a bundle of streamlines through a closed curve; no fluid crosses its wall.

Boundary surface

A surface \(F(\mathbf r,t)=0\) moving with the fluid (always made of the same particles) satisfies \(DF/Dt=0\). This is the kinematical boundary condition. At a fixed rigid wall it reduces to \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf n=0\). At a moving boundary the normal component of fluid velocity equals the normal component of the boundary velocity.

Equation of continuity (conservation of mass)

For a fixed volume \(V\) with surface \(S\): the rate of increase of mass inside equals the inflow through \(S\), \(\int_V\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\,dV=-\oint_S\rho\,\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=-\int_V\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf q)\,dV .\) As \(V\) is arbitrary, \(\boxed{\ \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf q)=0\ }\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\frac{D\rho}{Dt}+\rho\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0 .\)

Incompressible fluid. The density of each particle stays constant, \(D\rho/Dt=0\), so \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0\). The fluid need not have uniform density, and the flow need not be irrotational. Incompressible does not mean irrotational.

Steady flow (not nec. incompressible): \(\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf q)=0\).

Lagrangian form. \(\rho\,\dfrac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(a,b,c)}=\rho_0(a,b,c)\). For an incompressible fluid with uniform initial density, the Jacobian equals \(1\).

The divergence \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q\) is the rate of volume expansion per unit volume.

Continuity in coordinates

System Equation of continuity (general \(\rho\))
Cartesian \((x,y,z)\) \(\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\dfrac{\partial(\rho u)}{\partial x}+\dfrac{\partial(\rho v)}{\partial y}+\dfrac{\partial(\rho w)}{\partial z}=0\)
Cylindrical \((R,\theta,z)\) \(\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\dfrac1R\dfrac{\partial(R\rho q_R)}{\partial R}+\dfrac1R\dfrac{\partial(\rho q_\theta)}{\partial\theta}+\dfrac{\partial(\rho q_z)}{\partial z}=0\)
Spherical \((r,\theta,\phi)\) \(\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\dfrac1{r^2}\dfrac{\partial(\rho r^2q_r)}{\partial r}+\dfrac1{r\sin\theta}\dfrac{\partial(\rho\sin\theta\,q_\theta)}{\partial\theta}+\dfrac1{r\sin\theta}\dfrac{\partial(\rho q_\phi)}{\partial\phi}=0\)

Here \(\theta\) is the azimuth in cylindrical coordinates and the polar angle in spherical ones, and \(\phi\) is the azimuth in spherical ones.

Special cases.

  • Spherical symmetry (\(q_\theta=q_\phi=0\), everything depends on \(r,t\) only): \(\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\dfrac1{r^2}\dfrac{\partial(\rho r^2q)}{\partial r}=0\).
  • Cylindrical (2-D radial) symmetry: \(\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\dfrac1R\dfrac{\partial(R\rho q)}{\partial R}=0\).
  • Incompressible, steady, purely radial: \(r^2q=\text{const}\) in 3-D (so \(q\propto1/r^2\)) and \(Rq=\text{const}\) in 2-D (so \(q\propto1/R\)). These are the point source and the line source.

Checking whether a velocity field is a possible incompressible flow

A given \(\mathbf q\) is a possible motion of an incompressible fluid if and only if \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0\) (and the field is single-valued and smooth). Compute the divergence in the coordinate system in which the components are given. If the problem also says “irrotational”, check \(\nabla\times\mathbf q=0\) separately: the two tests are independent.

Field Divergence Curl Verdict
\(u=x,\ v=y\) \(2\) \(0\) irrotational, not incompressible
\(u=-y,\ v=x\) \(0\) \(2\) incompressible, rotational
\(u=x^2-y^2,\ v=-2xy\) \(0\) \(0\) both

Rotation, vorticity, circulation

The vorticity is \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf q\); a fluid element rotates with angular velocity \(\tfrac12\boldsymbol\omega\). In Cartesian components \(\boldsymbol\omega=\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial v}{\partial z},\ \frac{\partial u}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial w}{\partial x},\ \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right).\) In cylindrical coordinates the axial component is \(\omega_z=\dfrac1R\left[\dfrac{\partial(Rq_\theta)}{\partial R}-\dfrac{\partial q_R}{\partial\theta}\right]\). Always \(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\omega=0\). A vortex line is a curve tangent to \(\boldsymbol\omega\); a vortex tube is a bundle of vortex lines.

Two standard flows:

  • Rigid rotation \(q_\theta=\Omega R\): \(\omega=2\Omega\) (forced vortex).
  • Free (line) vortex \(q_\theta=\kappa/R\): \(\omega=0\) everywhere except the axis; circulation \(2\pi\kappa\) about the axis.

Circulation about a closed curve \(C\) is \(\Gamma=\oint_C\mathbf q\cdot d\mathbf r\). By Stokes’ theorem \(\Gamma=\int_S\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf n\,dS\) for any surface \(S\) spanning \(C\) inside the region of flow.

Kelvin’s circulation theorem

For an inviscid fluid with conservative body forces and density depending on pressure only (barotropic, which includes incompressible), the circulation about any closed curve moving with the fluid is constant in time: \(D\Gamma/Dt=0\).

Proof idea: \(\dfrac{D\Gamma}{Dt}=\oint\dfrac{D\mathbf q}{Dt}\cdot d\mathbf r+\oint\mathbf q\cdot d\mathbf q\). The second integral is \(\oint d(\tfrac12q^2)=0\). By Euler’s equation \(D\mathbf q/Dt=-\nabla\Omega-\nabla p/\rho\) is a gradient of a single-valued function, so its closed integral is \(0\).

Consequences (Lagrange and Helmholtz): vortex lines move with the fluid and their strength stays constant; a flow that is irrotational at one instant stays irrotational; a flow started from rest is irrotational. The theorem needs the curve to be a material curve, and it fails if viscosity or a non-barotropic density is present.

Velocity potential

If \(\nabla\times\mathbf q=0\) in a simply connected region, a scalar \(\phi\) exists with \(\mathbf q=-\nabla\phi\qquad(\text{many Indian texts; modern texts write }\mathbf q=+\nabla\phi).\) Surfaces \(\phi=\text{const}\) are equipotentials and cut the streamlines at right angles. If also \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0\) then \(\nabla^2\phi=0\) (Laplace’s equation).

In a multiply connected region (for example the plane with a point or a cylinder removed) an irrotational flow can have nonzero circulation about a hole, and then \(\phi\) is many-valued: it increases by \(-\Gamma\) (with \(\mathbf q=-\nabla\phi\)) on each circuit. Such flow is cyclic; zero circulation gives acyclic flow.

Stream function and Cauchy-Riemann relations

For a 2-D incompressible flow \(u_x+v_y=0\), so a stream function exists: \(u=-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y},\qquad v=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}.\) If the flow is also irrotational, \(\phi\) and \(\psi\) satisfy \(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y},\qquad\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y}=-\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x},\) the Cauchy-Riemann equations, so \(\phi+i\psi\) is analytic in \(z=x+iy\). In polar form \(\partial\phi/\partial r=\tfrac1r\partial\psi/\partial\theta\) and \(\tfrac1r\partial\phi/\partial\theta=-\partial\psi/\partial r\). With the opposite sign pairing (\(\mathbf q=+\nabla\phi\), \(u=\psi_y\), \(v=-\psi_x\)) the same two relations hold. That is why the exam item on these relations has the same answer in both conventions.

Sign conventions in one place

Convention \(\mathbf q\) \(u,\ v\) from \(\psi\) \(dw/dz\)
Used in these notes (Chorlton, Raisinghania) \(-\nabla\phi\) \(-\psi_y,\ \psi_x\) \(-u+iv\)
Modern \(+\nabla\phi\) \(\psi_y,\ -\psi_x\) \(u-iv\)

Speeds, streamline shapes and pressures do not depend on the convention; only the sign of \(\phi\), \(\psi\) and of source strength written in \(w\) do.

Formulas and results

  • \(\dfrac{D}{Dt}=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}+\mathbf q\cdot\nabla\); acceleration \(=\dfrac{\partial\mathbf q}{\partial t}+\nabla(\tfrac12q^2)-\mathbf q\times\boldsymbol\omega\).
  • Streamline \(dx/u=dy/v=dz/w\) (\(t\) fixed); pathline \(dx/dt=u,\dots\); coincide if steady.
  • Continuity: \(\partial\rho/\partial t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf q)=0\); incompressible: \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0\).
  • Spherical symmetry: \(\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\dfrac1{r^2}\dfrac{\partial(\rho r^2q)}{\partial r}=0\).
  • Cylindrical: \(\dfrac1R\dfrac{\partial(Rq_R)}{\partial R}+\dfrac1R\dfrac{\partial q_\theta}{\partial\theta}+\dfrac{\partial q_z}{\partial z}=0\) (incompressible).
  • Spherical: \(\dfrac1{r^2}\dfrac{\partial(r^2q_r)}{\partial r}+\dfrac1{r\sin\theta}\dfrac{\partial(\sin\theta\,q_\theta)}{\partial\theta}+\dfrac1{r\sin\theta}\dfrac{\partial q_\phi}{\partial\phi}=0\) (incompressible).
  • \(\boldsymbol\omega=\nabla\times\mathbf q\), element spin \(=\tfrac12\boldsymbol\omega\); rigid rotation: \(\omega=2\Omega\).
  • \(\Gamma=\oint\mathbf q\cdot d\mathbf r=\int\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf n\,dS\).
  • Kelvin: \(D\Gamma/Dt=0\) (inviscid, barotropic, conservative forces).
  • Irrotational: \(\mathbf q=-\nabla\phi\); plus incompressible: \(\nabla^2\phi=0\).
  • 2-D: \(u=-\psi_y,\ v=\psi_x\); \(\phi_x=\psi_y,\ \phi_y=-\psi_x\).

Worked examples

Example 1: Lagrangian to Eulerian

A flow is \(x=ae^{-kt},\ y=be^{kt},\ z=c\). Find the Eulerian velocity, the acceleration and the streamlines.

Differentiate at fixed \((a,b,c)\): \(u=-kae^{-kt}=-kx\), \(v=kbe^{kt}=ky\), \(w=0\). This is steady. Convective acceleration: \(f_x=u\,\partial u/\partial x=(-kx)(-k)=k^2x\), \(f_y=k^2y\). The Lagrangian check \(\partial^2x/\partial t^2=k^2ae^{-kt}=k^2x\) agrees. Streamlines: \(dx/(-kx)=dy/(ky)\) gives \(\ln x+\ln y=\text{const}\), i.e. \(xy=\text{const}\) (rectangular hyperbolas). Also \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=-k+k=0\): possible incompressible flow.

Example 2: acceleration in an unsteady field

\(u=x+t,\ v=y-t,\ w=0\). Find \(f_x,f_y\) at \((1,2)\), \(t=1\).

\(f_x=\partial u/\partial t+u\,u_x+v\,u_y=1+(x+t)(1)+0=x+t+1=3\). \(f_y=\partial v/\partial t+u\,v_x+v\,v_y=-1+0+(y-t)=-1+1=0\). So \(\mathbf f=(3,0)\). (Local part alone gives only \(1\) for \(f_x\): a typical wrong option.)

Example 3: streamline, pathline, streakline

\(u=1,\ v=t\). Compare the three curves through the origin.

  • Streamline at \(t=2\): \(dy/dx=v/u=2\), so \(y=2x\).
  • Pathline of the particle at the origin at \(t=0\): \(x=t,\ y=t^2/2\), so \(y=x^2/2\).
  • Streakline at \(t=2\) (particles released at the origin at times \(\tau\in[0,2]\)): position at time \(2\) is \(x=2-\tau\), \(y=(4-\tau^2)/2\); with \(\tau=2-x\) this is \(y=2x-\tfrac12x^2\), \(0\le x\le2\).

At \(x=1\) the three give \(y=2,\ \tfrac12,\ \tfrac32\): all different, because the flow is unsteady.

Example 4: incompressibility in polar coordinates

Show that \(q_R=U\cos\theta\,(1-a^2/R^2)\), \(q_\theta=-U\sin\theta\,(1+a^2/R^2)\) satisfies continuity.

\(\dfrac1R\dfrac{\partial(Rq_R)}{\partial R}=\dfrac{U\cos\theta}{R}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial R}\!\left(R-\dfrac{a^2}R\right)=\dfrac{U\cos\theta}{R}\left(1+\dfrac{a^2}{R^2}\right)\), and \(\dfrac1R\dfrac{\partial q_\theta}{\partial\theta}=-\dfrac{U\cos\theta}{R}\left(1+\dfrac{a^2}{R^2}\right)\). The sum is \(0\). (This is the flow past a circular cylinder, see the next topic.)

Example 5: finding constants and the missing component

(a) \(u=2x+4y,\ v=bx+cy\) is a possible incompressible irrotational flow. Divergence: \(2+c=0\), so \(c=-2\). Curl: \(v_x-u_y=b-4=0\), so \(b=4\). (b) \(u=3x^2y\) with \(w=0\): \(v_y=-u_x=-6xy\), so \(v=-3xy^2+g(x)\) for any function \(g(x)\).

Example 6: circulation and vorticity

For \(\mathbf q=(-\Omega y,\Omega x,0)\) the vorticity is \(\omega_z=\Omega-(-\Omega)=2\Omega\). Around a circle of radius \(a\), \(\Gamma=\oint q_\theta\,a\,d\theta=2\pi a^2\Omega\), which equals \(\omega\times\text{area}=2\Omega\cdot\pi a^2\). For \(\Omega=3,\ a=2\): \(\Gamma=24\pi\). For the free vortex \(q_\theta=\kappa/R\), \(\Gamma=2\pi\kappa\) on every circle about the axis, although \(\omega=0\) in the fluid; the circulation comes entirely from the singular axis.

Common traps

  • Incompressible \(\ne\) irrotational. The 2025 Q70 reason says incompressibility is \(\nabla\times\mathbf q=0\). It is \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0\). The curl condition is irrotationality.
  • Steady \(\ne\) uniform, and steady flow still has convective acceleration.
  • Streamlines, pathlines, streaklines agree only for steady flow. Streamlines use \(t\) as a fixed parameter; pathlines do not.
  • Spherical symmetry factor. It is \(r^2q\) inside the \(r\)-derivative with a \(1/r^2\) in front. A \(1/r\) in front of \(\partial(\rho qr)/\partial r\) is the cylindrical form.
  • The stream function exists for every 2-D incompressible flow; the velocity potential exists only for irrotational flow. Do not swap them.
  • Cauchy-Riemann signs. \(\phi_x=\psi_y\) and \(\phi_y=-\psi_x\). The other combination \(\phi_x=\psi_y\) and \(\phi_y=+\psi_x\) is false.
  • Kelvin’s theorem is about a closed curve moving with the fluid, not a fixed curve, and requires inviscid, barotropic and conservative forces.
  • A free vortex is irrotational but has circulation. Zero vorticity does not imply zero circulation in a multiply connected region.

Previous-year questions

2025 Q67 (official key B)

Question: In the case of spherical symmetry the equation of continuity is… (four forms of \(\partial\rho/\partial t\) and \(\partial(\rho qr^2)/\partial r\); one option is “none of these”). The correct form is \(\dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\dfrac1{r^2}\dfrac{\partial(\rho qr^2)}{\partial r}=0\). Solution: set \(q_\theta=q_\phi=0\) and no \(\theta,\phi\) dependence in the spherical continuity equation. Option B is the one with \(1/r^2\) outside and \(r^2\) inside.

2025 Q70 (official key C)

Question (assertion-reason). A: the equation of continuity for a fluid is \(\partial\rho/\partial t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf q)=0\). R: the equation of continuity for an incompressible fluid is \(\nabla\times\mathbf q=0\). Answer: A is true, R is false. Solution: R should read \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0\). \(\nabla\times\mathbf q=0\) is irrotationality.

2025 Q72 (official key D)

Question: The relations between velocity potential \(\phi\) and stream function \(\psi\) are (a) \(\phi_x=\psi_y\), (b) \(\phi_y=\psi_x\), (c) \(\phi_x=-\psi_y\), (d) \(\phi_y=-\psi_x\). Which are correct? Answer: a, d only. Solution: These are the Cauchy-Riemann equations of \(\phi+i\psi\): \(\phi_x=\psi_y\) and \(\phi_y=-\psi_x\). Options b and c have the wrong sign.

Quick revision

  • Eulerian: fields at fixed points. Lagrangian: follow particle \((a,b,c)\).
  • \(D/Dt=\partial/\partial t+\mathbf q\cdot\nabla\); acceleration has local + convective parts.
  • Steady flow: streamline = pathline = streakline.
  • Continuity: \(\partial\rho/\partial t+\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf q)=0\); incompressible means \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf q=0\).
  • Spherical symmetry: \(\partial\rho/\partial t+r^{-2}\,\partial(\rho r^2q)/\partial r=0\).
  • Radial incompressible flow: \(q\propto1/r^2\) in 3-D, \(q\propto1/R\) in 2-D.
  • Vorticity \(=\nabla\times\mathbf q\); element spin is half of it; rigid rotation has \(\omega=2\Omega\).
  • \(\Gamma=\oint\mathbf q\cdot d\mathbf r=\int\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf n\,dS\).
  • Kelvin: circulation about a material curve is constant (inviscid, barotropic, conservative).
  • Irrotational: \(\mathbf q=-\nabla\phi\); with incompressible, \(\nabla^2\phi=0\).
  • \(\psi\) exists for 2-D incompressible; \(\phi\) only if irrotational; \(\phi_x=\psi_y\), \(\phi_y=-\psi_x\).
