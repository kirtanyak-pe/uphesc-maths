Why it matters for the exam

This unit (mechanics and fluids together) gave 7 of the 70 maths questions in each of the 2025 and 2026 papers; the exam of 2026 has 90 maths questions, so expect about 9. Lagrangian mechanics is the base for every other topic in the unit: degrees of freedom, generalized momentum, Hamiltonian, normal modes. Direct questions ask for the number of generalized coordinates or degrees of freedom (2025-Q68), but the Lagrangian itself appears inside questions on momenta, the Hamiltonian and coupled oscillators (2025-Q71, 2025-Q73). You must be able to write \(T\), \(V\), \(L\) for a standard system and get the equation of motion in under two minutes.

Core ideas

Generalized coordinates and degrees of freedom

A system of \(N\) particles has \(3N\) Cartesian coordinates. If there are \(k\) independent holonomic constraints, the number of independent coordinates is

\[n = 3N - k,\]

and any set \(q_1,\dots,q_n\) that fixes the configuration is a set of generalized coordinates. They need not be lengths: angles, areas, charges all qualify. The space with coordinates \((q_1,\dots,q_n)\) is the configuration space. Generalized velocities are \(\dot q_j\).

For holonomic constraints the number of degrees of freedom equals the number of generalized coordinates. For non-holonomic constraints the system has more generalized coordinates than degrees of freedom: the coordinates are not all independent in their differentials. A disc rolling without slipping on a plane has 5 coordinates but only 3 independent velocities.

System Degrees of freedom
Free particle in space \(3\)
Particle on a sphere or on a fixed surface \(2\)
Particle on a fixed curve (bead on a wire) \(1\)
\(N\) particles in a plane \(2N\)
Free rigid body \(6\) (3 translation + 3 rotation)
Rigid body with one point fixed \(3\) (the three Euler angles)
Rigid body rotating about a fixed axis \(1\)
Two particles joined by a rigid rod, free in space \(5\)
Planar double pendulum \(2\)
Spherical pendulum \(2\)
Atwood machine (inextensible string) \(1\)
Rigid body, \(N \ge 3\) non-collinear particles \(3N - (3N-6) = 6\)

Why a rigid body has 6: three non-collinear particles have 9 coordinates and 3 fixed mutual distances, so \(9-3=6\). Every further particle is fixed by its distances to three already-placed particles, so adding it adds nothing.

Types of constraint

A constraint is holonomic if it can be written as an equation between coordinates (and possibly time):

\[f(\mathbf r_1,\dots,\mathbf r_N,t)=0.\]

It is non-holonomic otherwise. Two kinds occur:

  • an inequality, such as a particle on or outside a sphere, \(r\ge a\), or gas molecules inside a closed box;
  • a velocity relation that cannot be integrated to a coordinate relation, such as a disc or sphere rolling without slipping on a plane (when it may also turn about the vertical axis). The condition \(\dot x=a\dot\varphi\cos\theta\), \(\dot y=a\dot\varphi\sin\theta\) cannot be integrated without knowing the whole path.

A rolling wheel confined to a straight line is holonomic: the constraint \(x=a\varphi\) integrates.

A constraint is scleronomic if time does not appear explicitly, and rheonomic if it does.

Time-independent Explicit time dependence
Holonomic Scleronomic: simple pendulum with fixed length Rheonomic: pendulum whose length is \(l(t)\), bead on a wire rotating at \(\omega\)
Non-holonomic Disc rolling on a plane Rare in exams

Scleronomic and rheonomic describe time dependence, holonomic and non-holonomic describe integrability. These are different axes, and examiners mix them in options.

Virtual displacement, virtual work, D’Alembert’s principle

A virtual displacement \(\delta\mathbf r_i\) is an infinitesimal displacement consistent with the constraints at a fixed instant: time does not change, forces do not change. Real displacements \(d\mathbf r_i\) take place in time \(dt\). They coincide only for scleronomic constraints.

Split the force on particle \(i\) into the applied force \(\mathbf F_i\) and the constraint force \(\mathbf f_i\). For ideal constraints (smooth surfaces, rigid rods, rolling without slipping) the constraint forces do no virtual work: \(\sum_i \mathbf f_i\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0\).

Principle of virtual work (statics): the system is in equilibrium iff

\[\sum_i \mathbf F_i\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i = 0\]

for every virtual displacement. In generalized coordinates this says \(Q_j=0\) for every \(j\) (independent \(q_j\)).

D’Alembert’s principle (dynamics): replace \(\mathbf F_i\) by \(\mathbf F_i-\dot{\mathbf p}_i\),

\[\sum_i(\mathbf F_i-\dot{\mathbf p}_i)\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0.\]

The constraint forces have disappeared. This is the starting point for Lagrange’s equations.

Generalized force and Lagrange’s equations

With \(\mathbf r_i=\mathbf r_i(q_1,\dots,q_n,t)\), the generalized force is

\[Q_j=\sum_i \mathbf F_i\cdot\frac{\partial \mathbf r_i}{\partial q_j}.\]

If the forces come from a potential \(V(\mathbf r_i)\), then \(Q_j=-\partial V/\partial q_j\). Substituting into D’Alembert’s principle, with independent \(\delta q_j\), gives

\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot q_j}\right)-\frac{\partial T}{\partial q_j}=Q_j .\]

For conservative forces define the Lagrangian \(L=T-V\):

\[\boxed{\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_j}\right)-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j}=0,\qquad j=1,\dots,n.}\]

This is \(n\) second-order equations. Conditions: holonomic constraints (so the \(q_j\) are independent), ideal constraints, and forces derivable from a potential \(V\) that may depend on \(q\), \(t\) (and on \(\dot q\) for the generalized potential below).

If some forces are not conservative, write \(Q_j'\) for those: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_j}-\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial q_j}=Q_j'\). For viscous friction \(F_i=-c\,v_i\) there is a Rayleigh dissipation function \(\mathcal F=\tfrac12\sum c\,\dot q^2\) and \(Q_j'=-\partial\mathcal F/\partial\dot q_j\).

Generalized (velocity-dependent) potential. If \(Q_j=-\dfrac{\partial U}{\partial q_j}+\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial U}{\partial\dot q_j}\), Lagrange’s equations hold with \(L=T-U\). A charged particle in an electromagnetic field has \(U=q\phi-q\,\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf v\), so

\[L=\tfrac12 mv^2-q\phi+q\,\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf v.\]

Facts about \(L\).

  • \(L\) is not unique: replacing \(L\) by \(L'=L+\dfrac{dF(q,t)}{dt}\) gives the same equations of motion.
  • \(L\) and \(\lambda L\) (\(\lambda\) constant) give the same equations.
  • The equations keep the same form under any point transformation \(q_j\to q_j'(q,t)\): they are covariant.
  • The number of equations equals the number of degrees of freedom, and the constraint forces do not appear. To find a constraint force (a tension, a normal reaction) use a Lagrange multiplier (below).

Kinetic energy structure

For \(\mathbf r_i=\mathbf r_i(q,t)\), \(T=T_2+T_1+T_0\), where \(T_2=\tfrac12\sum_{jk}m_{jk}\dot q_j\dot q_k\) is quadratic in the velocities, \(T_1=\sum_j a_j\dot q_j\) linear and \(T_0\) independent of \(\dot q\). For scleronomic constraints \(T=T_2\) only, and Euler’s theorem gives \(\sum_j\dot q_j\,\partial T/\partial\dot q_j=2T\). This is the reason \(H=T+V\) in the next topic.

Generalized momentum, cyclic coordinates, conservation laws

The generalized momentum conjugate to \(q_j\) is

\[p_j=\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_j}.\]

It need not have the dimension of momentum: for an angle \(\theta\) it is angular momentum. A coordinate \(q_k\) is cyclic (ignorable) if \(L\) does not contain \(q_k\) (though it may contain \(\dot q_k\)). Then

\[\frac{d p_k}{dt}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad p_k=\text{const}.\]

Symmetry and conservation (Noether, at exam level):

Symmetry of \(L\) Conserved quantity
Invariant under translation along \(x\) \(p_x\) (linear momentum)
Invariant under rotation about \(z\) \(p_\varphi=L_z\) (angular momentum)
No explicit time dependence energy function \(h\)

The energy function (Jacobi integral) is

\[h=\sum_j\dot q_j\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_j}-L,\qquad \frac{dh}{dt}=-\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}.\]

So \(h\) is conserved when \(\partial L/\partial t=0\). It equals \(T+V\) only if the constraints are scleronomic and \(V\) does not depend on velocities. For a bead on a rotating wire, \(h\) is conserved but it is not the energy: the wire does work.

Lagrange multipliers (to find constraint forces)

Keep an extra coordinate and a constraint \(f(q,t)=0\). Then

\[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_j}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j}=\lambda\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_j},\]

and \(\lambda\,\partial f/\partial q_j\) is the generalized constraint force. Example: for a pendulum with coordinates \((r,\theta)\) and constraint \(r-l=0\), \(\lambda\) is minus the tension.

Standard Lagrangians

1. Simple pendulum (length \(l\), angle \(\theta\) from the downward vertical): \(T=\tfrac12 ml^2\dot\theta^2\), \(V=-mgl\cos\theta\),

\[L=\tfrac12 ml^2\dot\theta^2+mgl\cos\theta,\qquad \ddot\theta+\frac gl\sin\theta=0.\]

Small angles: \(\omega=\sqrt{g/l}\).

2. Particle in a central force (plane polar \((r,\theta)\)):

\[L=\tfrac12 m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)-V(r).\]

\(\theta\) is cyclic, so \(p_\theta=mr^2\dot\theta=\ell\) is constant (angular momentum, equivalently Kepler’s second law: areal velocity \(\tfrac12 r^2\dot\theta=\ell/2m\)). The radial equation is

\[m\ddot r-\frac{\ell^2}{mr^3}=-V'(r),\]

i.e. one-dimensional motion in the effective potential \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}=V+\dfrac{\ell^2}{2mr^2}\). In three dimensions, with spherical coordinates, \(L=\tfrac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2+r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2)-V(r)\): \(\varphi\) is cyclic and \(p_\varphi\) is the \(z\)-component of angular momentum.

3. Bead on a wire.

  • Smooth fixed wire \(y=f(x)\) in a vertical plane: \(L=\tfrac12m(1+f'^2)\dot x^2-mgf(x)\). For the parabola \(y=ax^2\): \(L=\tfrac12m(1+4a^2x^2)\dot x^2-mgax^2\), small-oscillation frequency \(\omega^2=2ag\).
  • Bead on a horizontal straight wire rotating at constant \(\omega\) about a vertical axis through one point: \(L=\tfrac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\omega^2)\), so \(\ddot r=\omega^2r\). Here \(h=\tfrac12m(\dot r^2-r^2\omega^2)\) is conserved, but \(T=\tfrac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\omega^2)\) is not: the constraint is rheonomic.
  • Bead on a smooth vertical hoop of radius \(R\) rotating at constant \(\omega\) about a vertical diameter: with \(\theta\) measured from the downward vertical,
\[L=\tfrac12mR^2\dot\theta^2+\tfrac12mR^2\omega^2\sin^2\theta+mgR\cos\theta,\]
\[\ddot\theta=\sin\theta\left(\omega^2\cos\theta-\frac gR\right).\]

Equilibria: \(\theta=0\) always, and \(\cos\theta_0=g/(R\omega^2)\) only when \(\omega^2>g/R\). The bottom point is stable for \(\omega^2<g/R\) and unstable for \(\omega^2>g/R\) (pitchfork bifurcation).

4. Atwood machine (masses \(m_1,m_2\), light frictionless pulley, \(x\) = downward displacement of \(m_1\), so \(m_2\) goes up by \(x\)): \(T=\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)\dot x^2\), \(V=-(m_1-m_2)gx\) (up to a constant),

\[L=\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)\dot x^2+(m_1-m_2)gx,\qquad \ddot x=\frac{m_1-m_2}{m_1+m_2}\,g.\]

String tension \(T_s=\dfrac{2m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}\,g\) (needs a multiplier or Newton’s law). If the pulley is a disc of moment of inertia \(I\) and radius \(r\), add \(\tfrac12 I\dot x^2/r^2\) to \(T\): \(\ddot x=\dfrac{(m_1-m_2)g}{m_1+m_2+I/r^2}\).

5. Free particle in plane polar coordinates: \(L=\tfrac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)\) gives \(m\ddot r=mr\dot\theta^2\) (centrifugal term) and \(\dfrac{d}{dt}(mr^2\dot\theta)=0\). The term \(mr\dot\theta^2\) is a coordinate effect of polar coordinates, not a force.

6. Two-dimensional oscillator / coupled system. \(L=\tfrac12m(\dot x^2+\dot y^2)-\tfrac12k(x^2+y^2)\) separates into two oscillators; cross terms in \(T\) or \(V\) lead to coupled equations (small-oscillations topic).

7. Spherical pendulum (length \(l\), \(\theta\) from the downward vertical, \(\varphi\) azimuth): two degrees of freedom,

\[L=\tfrac12ml^2\left(\dot\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2\right)+mgl\cos\theta.\]

\(\varphi\) is cyclic, so \(p_\varphi=ml^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi\) is conserved, and the \(\theta\) equation is \(\ddot\theta=\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\varphi^2-\dfrac gl\sin\theta\). A steady conical pendulum (\(\ddot\theta=0\), \(\theta=\theta_0\)) needs \(\dot\varphi^2=\dfrac{g}{l\cos\theta_0}\), so the period is \(2\pi\sqrt{l\cos\theta_0/g}\), shorter than \(2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\).

8. Two-body problem and reduced mass. For \(L=\tfrac12m_1\dot{\mathbf r}_1^2+\tfrac12m_2\dot{\mathbf r}_2^2-V(\lvert\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2\rvert)\) use the centre of mass \(\mathbf R\) and the relative vector \(\mathbf r=\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2\):

\[L=\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)\dot{\mathbf R}^2+\tfrac12\mu\dot{\mathbf r}^2-V(r),\qquad \mu=\frac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}.\]

\(\mathbf R\) is cyclic (free motion of the centre of mass) and the relative motion is a one-body central-force problem with mass \(\mu\). For two masses joined by a spring \(k\), the relative vibration has \(\omega=\sqrt{k/\mu}\).

9. Pendulum on a movable support. A bob \(m\) on a light rod of length \(l\) hangs from a block \(M\) that slides freely on a horizontal rail (\(x\) = position of the block):

\[L=\tfrac12(M+m)\dot x^2+ml\,\dot x\dot\theta\cos\theta+\tfrac12ml^2\dot\theta^2+mgl\cos\theta.\]

\(x\) is cyclic, so the horizontal momentum \((M+m)\dot x+ml\dot\theta\cos\theta\) is conserved. For small oscillations, eliminating \(\dot x\) gives \(\omega^2=\dfrac gl\cdot\dfrac{M+m}{M}\), which tends to \(g/l\) as \(M\to\infty\). Note that \(T\) contains a cross term \(\dot x\dot\theta\), so the generalized momenta are not simply \(M\dot x\) and \(ml^2\dot\theta\).

Worked examples

Example 1: counting degrees of freedom

A planar double pendulum has two rods, so 4 Cartesian coordinates and 2 length constraints: \(n=4-2=2\). Two particles joined by a rigid rod in space have \(6-1=5\). A particle on the surface of a cylinder of fixed radius: \(3-1=2\). A rigid body with one point fixed: three coordinates are removed from 6, leaving 3 (the three Euler angles).

Example 2: Atwood machine with a massive pulley

Masses \(m_1=3\) kg and \(m_2=1\) kg hang over a uniform disc pulley of mass \(M=2\) kg, radius \(r\). The disc has \(I=\tfrac12Mr^2\), so \(I/r^2=1\) kg. With \(x\) the downward displacement of \(m_1\):

\[L=\tfrac12(m_1+m_2+I/r^2)\dot x^2+(m_1-m_2)gx.\]

Lagrange’s equation gives \((m_1+m_2+1)\ddot x=(m_1-m_2)g\), so

\[\ddot x=\frac{2g}{5}=0.4g\approx3.92\ \text{m/s}^2.\]

(The same system with a light pulley would give \(0.5g\).)

Example 3: central force, circular orbits

For \(V=-k/r\), \(L=\tfrac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)+k/r\). The coordinate \(\theta\) is cyclic, so \(\ell=mr^2\dot\theta\) is constant. The radial equation is \(m\ddot r=\ell^2/(mr^3)-k/r^2\). A circular orbit has \(\ddot r=0\):

\[r_0=\frac{\ell^2}{mk}.\]

Small radial oscillations about \(r_0\) have \(\omega_r^2=V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(r_0)/m=\dfrac{3\ell^2}{m^2r_0^4}-\dfrac{2k}{mr_0^3}=\dfrac{k}{mr_0^3}\), equal to the orbital angular frequency squared \(\dot\theta^2\). That is why bound Kepler orbits close.

Example 4: bead on a rotating hoop

Take \(R=0.5\) m, \(\omega^2=2g/R\). Then \(\omega^2>g/R\), so the off-centre equilibrium exists at \(\cos\theta_0=g/(R\omega^2)=\tfrac12\), i.e. \(\theta_0=60^\circ\). Write \(\ddot\theta=F(\theta)\). At \(\theta_0\), \(F'(\theta_0)=\omega^2\cos2\theta_0-\dfrac gR\cos\theta_0=-\omega^2\sin^2\theta_0<0\), a restoring force, so this equilibrium is stable. The bottom point \(\theta=0\) has \(F'(0)=\omega^2-g/R>0\): unstable.

Example 5: cyclic coordinates and conservation

\(L=\tfrac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2+\dot z^2)-\tfrac12 k r^2-mgz\). The coordinate \(\theta\) is cyclic: \(mr^2\dot\theta\) is conserved. Nothing depends on \(t\), so \(h=T+V\) is conserved. The \(z\) equation is \(\ddot z=-g\): free fall, independent of the rest. The \(r\) equation is \(m\ddot r=mr\dot\theta^2-kr\).

Example 6: block on a rail with a pendulum

Take \(M=3m\). With zero initial total momentum, small oscillations of the bob have \(\omega^2=\dfrac gl\cdot\dfrac{3m+m}{3m}=\dfrac{4g}{3l}\), so the bob swings faster than the same pendulum on a fixed support (\(\omega^2=g/l\)). The block moves opposite to the bob so that the centre of mass of the system stays at rest horizontally.

Common traps

  • Non-holonomic means non-integrable, not “has velocity in it”. Rolling on a line is holonomic. Rolling on a plane with free steering is non-holonomic.
  • Scleronomic vs holonomic are independent properties. A bead on a rotating wire is holonomic and rheonomic.
  • Rigid body with one point fixed has 3 degrees of freedom, a free rigid body 6. The options 8, 6, 3, 2 in 2025-Q68 are built on this confusion.
  • \(h=T+V\) is not automatic. It needs scleronomic constraints and a velocity-independent \(V\). A rotating-frame or rotating-wire problem gives \(h\ne T+V\) and \(h\) is still conserved if \(L\) has no explicit time.
  • Conserved \(h\) vs conserved energy. Explicit time dependence of the constraint spoils energy conservation but not necessarily \(h\) conservation.
  • Sign of \(V\) in \(L=T-V\), e.g. \(L=\dots+mgl\cos\theta\) for a pendulum. \(L=T+V\) is wrong.
  • Generalized momentum is \(\partial L/\partial\dot q\), not \(m\dot q\). For a charged particle, \(p=m\dot x+qA\).
  • Different \(L\), same motion. Adding a total time derivative, or multiplying by a constant, does not change the equations. A question asking “which \(L\) gives the same equation” has such options.
  • Lagrange’s equations have as many equations as degrees of freedom, so constraint forces do not appear unless multipliers are used.
  • Reduced mass. In the two-body problem the relative coordinate has mass \(\mu=m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)\), not \(m_1+m_2\) and not \(m_1\).
  • A moving support adds a cross term \(\dot x\dot\theta\cos\theta\) to \(T\); dropping it gives the fixed-support frequency, which is wrong.
  • Virtual work is zero only for ideal constraints: sliding friction is not ideal.

Previous-year questions

2025-Q68: generalized coordinates of a rigid body

Question. The number of generalized coordinates required to describe the motion of a rigid body with one of its points fixed is: (A) 8 (B) 6 (C) 3 (D) 2.

Answer: C (3). A rigid body has 6 degrees of freedom. Fixing one point removes the 3 translational ones, leaving the orientation, described by three Euler angles \((\varphi,\theta,\psi)\). (Official key.)

Quick revision

  • Degrees of freedom \(=3N-k\) for \(k\) holonomic constraints; free rigid body 6, one point fixed 3, fixed axis 1.
  • Holonomic: \(f(\mathbf r,t)=0\). Non-holonomic: inequality or non-integrable velocity relation (rolling on a plane).
  • Scleronomic: no explicit \(t\). Rheonomic: explicit \(t\) (moving or rotating support).
  • D’Alembert: \(\sum(\mathbf F_i-\dot{\mathbf p}_i)\cdot\delta\mathbf r_i=0\); ideal constraints do no virtual work.
  • Lagrange: \(\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}-\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial q}=0\) with \(L=T-V\).
  • \(p_j=\partial L/\partial\dot q_j\); cyclic coordinate \(\Rightarrow\) \(p_j\) conserved.
  • \(h=\sum\dot q\,\partial L/\partial\dot q-L\), conserved if \(\partial L/\partial t=0\); equals \(T+V\) only if scleronomic and \(V\) velocity-independent.
  • Pendulum: \(\ddot\theta+(g/l)\sin\theta=0\). Atwood: \(a=\dfrac{m_1-m_2}{m_1+m_2}g\).
  • Central force: \(mr^2\dot\theta=\) const; radial motion in \(V+\ell^2/2mr^2\).
  • \(L\to L+dF/dt\) does not change the equations of motion.
  • Spherical pendulum: \(p_\varphi=ml^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi\) conserved; two-body: \(\mu=m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)\); pendulum on a free block: \(\omega^2=\frac gl\frac{M+m}{M}\).
