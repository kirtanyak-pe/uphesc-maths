Why it matters for the exam

Linear programming is the most frequently asked part of Unit 9. In 2025, four of the eight Unit 9 questions (Q80, Q81, Q83, Q84) came from it, and in 2026 two more (Q35, Q78). The questions are short: decide whether an LPP is infeasible, unbounded, has alternative optima, or compute the optimum from a few constraints, then pick the non-convex set, or read what a two-phase ending means. Every one is answerable in under two minutes if the classification of outcomes is clear.

Core ideas

Formulation

An LPP has a linear objective, linear constraints and non-negativity restrictions. Formulation means choosing decision variables, writing the objective and turning each limit into a constraint.

Checklist: (1) name the decision variables, with units; (2) write the objective as total profit (maximise) or total cost (minimise); (3) write one constraint per limited resource or requirement; “at most” and “not more than” give \(\le\), “at least” and “minimum” give \(\ge\), “exactly” gives \(=\); (4) add \(x\ge0\).

Example (paint problem). Exterior paint earns 5 and interior paint 4 per unit. Raw material M1 gives \(6x_1+4x_2\le 24\), raw material M2 gives \(x_1+2x_2\le 6\), interior output cannot exceed exterior by more than 1 (\(-x_1+x_2\le 1\)), and interior demand is at most 2 (\(x_2\le 2\)).

\[\max Z=5x_1+4x_2\quad\text{s.t. }6x_1+4x_2\le 24,\ x_1+2x_2\le 6,\ -x_1+x_2\le 1,\ x_2\le 2,\ x_1,x_2\ge 0.\]

More models of the same type:

  • Diet (minimise cost). Food 1 costs 4 per kg and gives 2 units of protein and 1 of iron per kg; food 2 costs 3 per kg and gives 1 protein and 3 iron. The daily need is at least 8 protein and 9 iron. Model: \(\min Z=4x_1+3x_2\) s.t. \(2x_1+x_2\ge8\), \(x_1+3x_2\ge9\), \(x_1,x_2\ge0\). The corners \((0,8),(3,2),(9,0)\) cost \(24,18,36\), so the best diet is \((3,2)\) at cost \(18\).
  • Blending (a percentage condition). Make 100 kg of feed from grain 1 (6 per kg, 20% protein) and grain 2 (4 per kg, 10% protein) with protein at least 14%. Multiply the percentage out: \(0.2x_1+0.1x_2\ge0.14(x_1+x_2)\), i.e. \(0.06x_1-0.04x_2\ge0\), with \(x_1+x_2=100\). Minimising \(6x_1+4x_2\) gives \(x_1=40\), \(x_2=60\), cost \(480\).
  • Investment (maximise return). Rs 10 lakh is split between bonds (6% return) and shares (10%), with shares at most Rs 6 lakh and bonds at least Rs 2 lakh. Model: \(\max Z=0.06x_1+0.10x_2\) s.t. \(x_1+x_2\le10\), \(x_2\le6\), \(x_1\ge2\). The optimum is \((4,6)\) with return \(0.84\) lakh.

General form: optimise \(Z=c^{T}x\) subject to \(Ax\ (\le,=,\ge)\ b\), \(x\ge 0\).

  • Standard form: maximise, every constraint an equation, \(b\ge 0\), \(x\ge 0\).
  • Canonical form: maximise with all constraints \(\le\) (or minimise with all \(\ge\)), \(x\ge 0\).
  • Minimising \(Z\) is the same as maximising \(-Z\): \(\min Z=-\max(-Z)\).
  • A variable unrestricted in sign is written \(x=x'-x''\) with \(x',x''\ge 0\).
  • If a right-hand side is negative, multiply the constraint by \(-1\) and reverse the inequality.

Slack, surplus and artificial variables

Constraint Add Role in the starting basis
\(\le\) slack \(s\ge 0\) (coefficient \(+1\)) \(s\) can be basic
\(\ge\) surplus \(s\ge 0\) (coefficient \(-1\)) and artificial \(R\ge 0\) (\(+1\)) \(R\) is basic, \(s\) is not
\(=\) artificial \(R\ge 0\) (\(+1\)) \(R\) is basic

Slack and surplus variables have cost 0. Artificial variables are not part of the real problem; they only give a starting identity matrix and must leave the basis.

Convex sets (syllabus: convex set theory)

  • A set \(S\subseteq\mathbb R^n\) is convex if \(x,y\in S\) and \(0\le\lambda\le 1\) imply \(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\in S\). The whole segment joining two points of \(S\) lies in \(S\).
  • A convex combination of \(x_1,\dots,x_k\) is \(\sum\lambda_i x_i\) with \(\lambda_i\ge 0\), \(\sum\lambda_i=1\).
  • Hyperplane: \(\{x: a^{T}x=b\}\). Closed half-space: \(\{x: a^{T}x\le b\}\). Both are convex.
  • Intersection of any family of convex sets is convex. A union of convex sets need not be convex.
  • A convex polyhedron is an intersection of finitely many half-spaces. A bounded polyhedron is a convex polytope, and it is the convex hull of its extreme points.
  • Extreme point (vertex) of a convex set \(S\): a point of \(S\) that is not an interior point of any line segment in \(S\). Equivalently, \(x=\lambda y+(1-\lambda)z\) with \(y,z\in S\), \(0<\lambda<1\) forces \(y=z=x\). A boundary point need not be extreme.
  • Convex hull \(\operatorname{conv}(S)\): all convex combinations of points of \(S\), the smallest convex set containing \(S\). Carathéodory: in \(\mathbb R^n\) at most \(n+1\) points are needed (three in the plane).
  • Cone: a set \(C\) with \(\lambda x\in C\) for all \(x\in C\), \(\lambda\ge0\); a convex cone is also closed under addition. A direction of \(S=\{x: Ax=b,\ x\ge0\}\) is a vector \(d\ne0\) with \(Ad=0\), \(d\ge0\); an extreme direction (ray) is not a sum of two non-parallel directions.
  • Supporting hyperplane of \(S\) at a boundary point \(x_0\): \(a^{T}x=a^{T}x_0\) with \(S\) entirely on one side, \(a^{T}x\le a^{T}x_0\) on \(S\). A closed convex set has one at every boundary point.
  • Separating hyperplane theorem: disjoint nonempty convex sets \(S,T\) admit \(a\ne0\), \(\beta\) with \(a^{T}x\le\beta\le a^{T}y\) for \(x\in S\), \(y\in T\). A point outside a closed convex set is strictly separated from it. Convexity is essential: no line separates the centre of an annulus from the ring.
  • Minkowski theorem: every point of a nonempty polyhedron \(S=\{x: Ax=b,\ x\ge0\}\) is \(x=\sum\lambda_iv_i+\sum\mu_jd_j\) with \(v_i\) extreme points, \(d_j\) extreme directions, \(\lambda_i\ge0\), \(\sum\lambda_i=1\), \(\mu_j\ge0\). A bounded \(S\) is the hull of its vertices. So \(\max c^{T}x\) is unbounded exactly when \(c^{T}d>0\) for some direction \(d\); otherwise the maximum is at an extreme point.
  • Convex function: \(f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\le\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y)\). Its epigraph is convex exactly when \(f\) is convex, and every set \(\{f\le c\}\) of a convex \(f\) is convex (the converse is false). A linear function is both convex and concave, hence an LPP optimum sits at a vertex.

Extreme points of standard sets:

Set Extreme points
closed disc every point of the boundary circle (infinitely many)
open disc none
closed triangle or polygon its vertices only
\(\{\lvert x_1\rvert+\lvert x_2\rvert\le1\}\) the four points \((\pm1,0),(0,\pm1)\)
quadrant \(x_1,x_2\ge0\) the origin only
half-plane, strip, line, whole plane none

Standard convex and non-convex sets in \(\mathbb R^2\):

Convex Not convex
disc \(x_1^2+x_2^2\le r^2\) annulus \(1\le x_1^2+x_2^2\le 4\)
half-plane, strip, line, single point exterior of a disc \(x_1^2+x_2^2\ge 1\)
\(\{x_2\ge x_1^2\}\) (epigraph of a convex function) \(\{x_2\le x_1^2\}\)
the empty set (by convention) union of two disjoint discs

The set above a convex function is convex; the set below it is not.

Theorems of linear programming

For \(S=\{x: Ax=b,\ x\ge 0\}\) with \(A\) an \(m\times n\) matrix of rank \(m\):

  1. \(S\) is a closed convex set (an intersection of hyperplanes and half-spaces). The feasible region of every LPP is convex.
  2. The set of optimal solutions of an LPP is convex. If two points are optimal, every point of the segment joining them is optimal.
  3. \(x\) is an extreme point of \(S\) if and only if \(x\) is a basic feasible solution (BFS).
  4. Fundamental theorem: if a feasible solution exists, a BFS exists. If an optimal solution exists, an optimal BFS exists, so an optimum is attained at an extreme point.
  5. If the optimum is attained at several extreme points, it is attained at every convex combination of them.
  6. If the feasible region is nonempty and bounded it is a polytope and the optimum always exists (Weierstrass). If it is unbounded, the optimum may be finite or infinite.

Basic solutions

Write the constraints as \(Ax=b\) with \(n\) variables (including slack and surplus) and \(m\) independent equations. Choose \(m\) columns forming a nonsingular matrix \(B\); set the other \(n-m\) variables to zero and solve \(Bx_B=b\). That is a basic solution.

  • The number of basic solutions is at most \(\binom{n}{m}=\dfrac{n!}{m!\,(n-m)!}\).
  • A basic solution with \(x_B\ge 0\) is a BFS. A BFS is nondegenerate if all \(m\) basic variables are positive and degenerate if at least one basic variable is zero.
  • Geometrically, in \(\mathbb R^2\) a degenerate vertex is one where more than two constraint lines meet.
  • Adjacent BFS differ in exactly one basic variable. The simplex method moves along edges from one BFS to an adjacent one, never decreasing \(Z\) (maximisation).

Graphical method (two variables)

  1. Draw each constraint line and shade the feasible side. Intersect all.
  2. Mark the corner points of the feasible region.
  3. Evaluate \(Z\) at each corner. The best value is the optimum.
  4. For an unbounded region, push the iso-profit line \(Z=k\) in the improving direction. If it never leaves the region, \(Z\) is unbounded.

Outcomes read from the picture:

Picture Outcome
Empty feasible region infeasible, no solution
Optimal iso-profit line touches one corner unique optimum
Optimal iso-profit line coincides with an edge (objective parallel to a constraint) alternative optima, infinitely many on that edge
Unbounded region and \(Z\) improves forever unbounded solution
Unbounded region but \(Z\) is bounded in its improving direction finite optimum

Simplex method

Setup (maximisation, all constraints \(\le\), \(b\ge 0\)): add a slack to each constraint, take the slacks as the starting basis.

The tableau holds the constraint rows, the RHS, and a bottom row \(Z_j-C_j\) where \(Z_j=\sum c_{B_i}\,a_{ij}\).

  1. Optimality test: all \(Z_j-C_j\ge 0\) means optimal (maximisation).
  2. Entering variable: the column with the most negative \(Z_j-C_j\).
  3. Unboundedness test: if that column has no positive entry, the problem is unbounded.
  4. Leaving variable: minimum ratio \(\min\{b_i/a_{ik}: a_{ik}>0\}\).
  5. Pivot: divide the pivot row by the pivot element, then clear the pivot column.
  6. Repeat.

For a minimisation problem, either convert to \(\max(-Z)\) or use the mirror rule: optimal when all \(Z_j-C_j\le 0\), enter the most positive.

Big-M (M-technique)

Give every artificial variable a huge penalty: coefficient \(-M\) in a maximisation, \(+M\) in a minimisation, where \(M\) exceeds every other number in the problem. Then run the simplex method, treating \(M\) as a symbol. In \(Z_j-C_j\), compare first by the coefficient of \(M\), then by the constant part.

  • An artificial variable that leaves the basis never needs to return; its column can be dropped.
  • If optimality is reached with an artificial variable still basic at a positive level, the problem is infeasible.
  • If an artificial variable is basic at zero level, the solution is feasible but degenerate.

Two-phase method

Phase I: ignore the real objective. Minimise \(W=\sum R_i\) (sum of artificial variables) subject to the constraints, starting from the artificial basis.

  • \(\min W>0\): no feasible solution to the original problem.
  • \(\min W=0\) and no artificial variable in the basis: drop the artificial columns, put the original objective back, and run Phase II from the Phase I final tableau.
  • \(\min W=0\) but an artificial variable is basic at zero level: the original problem is feasible and the starting BFS is degenerate. Either pivot that variable out on any nonzero entry of its row (on a real column, even a negative entry, since the RHS is 0), or, if all real entries of the row are 0, the row is a redundant constraint and can be deleted.

Compared with Big-M, two-phase avoids using a numeric \(M\) and is preferred in computer codes.

Reading special cases from the tableau

Case What the final or current tableau shows
Unbounded entering column (\(Z_j-C_j<0\)) has all entries \(\le 0\)
Alternative optima optimal tableau with \(Z_j-C_j=0\) for a nonbasic variable and a minimum ratio \(\theta>0\) in its column (if \(\theta=0\) the pivot only changes the basis, not the point)
Degenerate tie in the minimum ratio, so one basic variable is \(0\) in the next tableau
Infeasible (Big-M) optimal tableau with an artificial variable in the basis at a positive level
Infeasible (two-phase) Phase I optimum \(W>0\)
Redundant constraint artificial variable basic at zero with zero entries in all real columns of its row
Cycling the same basis repeats after degenerate pivots; rare; Bland’s rule (smallest index) prevents it

Degeneracy does not imply that the optimal solution is non-unique, and non-uniqueness does not imply degeneracy.

Formulas and results

  • Basic solutions \(\le\binom{n}{m}\) (\(n\) counts slack and surplus variables); extreme point \(\Longleftrightarrow\) BFS.
  • The feasible region and the set of optimal solutions of an LPP are convex; an optimum over a polytope is at a vertex.
  • \(\min Z=-\max(-Z)\).
  • Simplex (max): enter the most negative \(Z_j-C_j\), leave by minimum ratio over positive entries; optimal when all \(Z_j-C_j\ge0\); unbounded when an entering column is \(\le0\); alternative optima when a nonbasic \(Z_j-C_j=0\) at the optimum with \(\theta>0\).
  • Big-M penalty: \(-M\) (max), \(+M\) (min). Phase I: \(\min W=\sum\) artificials; infeasible iff \(\min W>0\).
  • Minkowski: \(x=\sum\lambda_iv_i+\sum\mu_jd_j\) (\(\sum\lambda_i=1\), \(\lambda,\mu\ge0\)); \(\max c^{T}x\) unbounded iff \(c^{T}d>0\) for some direction \(d\).
  • At most \(\Rightarrow\le\); at least \(\Rightarrow\ge\); exactly \(\Rightarrow=\).

Worked examples

Example 1: graphical and simplex on the paint problem

Corner points of the region of \(\max Z=5x_1+4x_2\) s.t. \(6x_1+4x_2\le24\), \(x_1+2x_2\le6\), \(-x_1+x_2\le1\), \(x_2\le2\):

Corner \((x_1,x_2)\) \(Z\)
\((0,0)\) 0
\((4,0)\) 20
\((3,\tfrac32)\) 21
\((2,2)\) 18
\((1,2)\) 13
\((0,1)\) 4

The best corner is \((3,\tfrac32)\) with \(Z=21\), where the first two constraints meet.

Simplex confirms it. Add slacks \(s_1,\dots,s_4\) (starting basis) with \(Z_j-C_j=(-5,-4,0,0,0,0)\). \(x_1\) enters, ratios \(24/6=4\), \(6/1=6\), so \(s_1\) leaves. Next \(x_2\) enters (its \(Z_j-C_j=-\tfrac23\)); the ratios are \(6,\ \tfrac32,\ 3,\ 2\) for the four rows, the smallest is \(\tfrac32\) in the \(s_2\) row, so \(s_2\) leaves. Final tableau:

Basis \(x_1\) \(x_2\) \(s_1\) \(s_2\) \(s_3\) \(s_4\) RHS
\(x_1\) 1 0 1/4 -1/2 0 0 3
\(x_2\) 0 1 -1/8 3/4 0 0 3/2
\(s_3\) 0 0 3/8 -5/4 1 0 5/2
\(s_4\) 0 0 1/8 -3/4 0 1 1/2
\(Z_j-C_j\) 0 0 3/4 1/2 0 0 21

All \(Z_j-C_j\ge0\), so \(x_1=3\), \(x_2=\tfrac32\), \(Z=21\). The entries \(\tfrac34\) and \(\tfrac12\) under \(s_1,s_2\) are the shadow prices of M1 and M2 (see Duality).

Example 2: Big-M

\(\min Z=4x_1+x_2\) s.t. \(3x_1+x_2=3\), \(4x_1+3x_2\ge6\), \(x_1+2x_2\le4\), \(x_1,x_2\ge0\).

Add surplus \(s_2\) (constraint 2), slack \(s_3\) (constraint 3), artificials \(R_1\) (constraint 1) and \(R_2\) (constraint 2). Work with \(Z'=-Z\), so maximise \(Z'=-4x_1-x_2-MR_1-MR_2\). Starting basis \(R_1,R_2,s_3\).

Initial row: \(Z_j-C_j=(4-7M,\ 1-4M,\ M,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0)\), \(Z'=-9M\). The most negative is \(x_1\) (\(-7M\)). Ratios \(3/3=1\), \(6/4\), \(4/1\): \(R_1\) leaves.

Next: \(x_2\) has \(Z_j-C_j=-\tfrac13-\tfrac53M\), the most negative; ratios \(1/(1/3)=3\), \(2/(5/3)=\tfrac65\), \(3/(5/3)=\tfrac95\): \(R_2\) leaves. Then \(s_2\) enters (its \(Z_j-C_j=-\tfrac15\)); ratio test gives \(s_3\) leaving. The final basis is \(x_1=\tfrac25\), \(x_2=\tfrac95\), \(s_2=1\), with row \(Z_j-C_j=(0,0,0,\tfrac15,\ -\tfrac75+M,\ M)\) and value \(-\tfrac{17}{5}\) (the columns are \(x_1,x_2,s_2,s_3,R_1,R_2\)).

Every entry is \(\ge0\) for large \(M\) and no artificial is basic, so \(\min Z=\tfrac{17}{5}\) at \((\tfrac25,\tfrac95)\). Check: \(3(0.4)+1.8=3\) and \(1.6+5.4=7\ge6\).

Example 3: the same problem by two-phase

Phase I: \(\max W'=-R_1-R_2\), starting row \((-7,-4,1,0,0,0)\). \(x_1\) enters (\(R_1\) leaves), then \(x_2\) (\(R_2\) leaves), reaching basis \(x_1=\tfrac35\), \(x_2=\tfrac65\), \(s_3=1\) with \(W=0\) and no artificial basic. The problem is feasible.

Phase II: delete the \(R\) columns and use the cost \(-4x_1-x_2\) (maximisation form). With basis \(x_1,x_2,s_3\) the entry under \(s_2\) is \(-\tfrac15<0\), so \(s_2\) enters; ratio test: only the \(s_3\) row has a positive entry (\(1\)), so \(s_3\) leaves. New basis \(x_1=\tfrac25\), \(x_2=\tfrac95\), \(s_2=1\) and every \(Z_j-C_j\ge0\). Same answer \(\min Z=\tfrac{17}{5}\).

Example 4: special cases from tableaus

  1. Unbounded. \(\max 3x_1+5x_2\) s.t. \(x_1-2x_2\le6\), \(x_1\le10\). First tableau: \(Z_j-C_j=(-3,-5,0,0)\). \(x_2\) enters, its column is \((-2,0)\), no positive entry, so unbounded.
  2. Alternative optima. \(\max 2x_1+4x_2\) s.t. \(x_1+2x_2\le5\), \(x_1+x_2\le4\). One pivot gives \(x_2=\tfrac52\), \(s_2=\tfrac32\), \(Z=10\), with \(Z_j-C_j=(0,0,2,0)\). The nonbasic \(x_1\) has \(Z_j-C_j=0\). Pivoting it in gives another optimal vertex \((3,1)\) with \(Z=10\). Optima: every point of the segment from \((0,\tfrac52)\) to \((3,1)\).
  3. Degenerate. \(\max 3x_1+9x_2\) s.t. \(x_1+4x_2\le8\), \(x_1+2x_2\le4\). For entering \(x_2\) the ratios are \(8/4=2\) and \(4/2=2\), a tie. Choosing \(s_1\) to leave gives a tableau with \(x_2=2\), \(s_2=0\) basic (a degenerate BFS), where \(x_1\) still has \(Z_j-C_j=-\tfrac34\). Entering \(x_1\) gives ratio \(0\) in the \(s_2\) row, so a second pivot with \(\theta=0\) follows; it does not move the point. The final row is \(Z_j-C_j=\tfrac32,\tfrac32\) for \(s_1,s_2\), so the optimum is \((0,2)\), \(Z=18\), unique although degenerate.
  4. Infeasible. \(\max 3x_1+2x_2\) s.t. \(2x_1+x_2\le2\), \(3x_1+4x_2\ge12\). Big-M ends with \(x_2=2\) and \(R=4>0\) in the basis. Infeasible: \(2x_1+x_2\le2\) forces \(x_1\le1\) and \(x_2\le2\), so \(3x_1+4x_2\le11<12\).

Example 5: counting basic solutions

\(x_1+2x_2+x_3=4\), \(2x_1+x_2+x_4=5\) (\(m=2\), \(n=4\)). At most \(\binom42=6\) basic solutions:

Choosing the two nonbasic variables gives: nonbasic \(x_3,x_4\): \((2,1,0,0)\), feasible; \(x_2,x_4\): \((\tfrac52,0,\tfrac32,0)\), feasible; \(x_1,x_3\): \((0,2,0,3)\), feasible; \(x_1,x_2\): \((0,0,4,5)\), feasible; \(x_2,x_3\): \((4,0,0,-3)\), infeasible; \(x_1,x_4\): \((0,5,-6,0)\), infeasible.

Four BFS, matching the four corners \((2,1)\), \((\tfrac52,0)\), \((0,2)\), \((0,0)\) of the region \(x_1+2x_2\le4\), \(2x_1+x_2\le5\). The other two are basic but infeasible (intersections outside the region).

Example 6: hull, extreme points and Minkowski form

(a) Take the points \((0,0),(2,0),(0,2),(1,1)\). The point \((1,1)\) is the midpoint of \((2,0)\) and \((0,2)\), so it is a convex combination of the others. The hull is the triangle with vertices \((0,0),(2,0),(0,2)\): three extreme points. The point \((1,1)\) lies on the boundary but is not extreme.

(b) \(S=\{x_1,x_2\ge0,\ x_1+x_2\ge1\}\). Extreme points: \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\). Extreme directions: \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\). For instance \((2,3)=1\cdot(0,1)+2(1,0)+2(0,1)\) (weight \(1\) on the vertex \((0,1)\), weights \(2,2\) on the directions). Then \(\max x_1+x_2\) is unbounded since \(c^{T}d=1>0\) for a direction, while \(\min x_1+x_2=1\) is attained at both vertices, so on the whole edge between them.

Common traps

  • Basic solutions number \(\binom nm\) with \(n\) counting slack and surplus variables; BFS are fewer. “Basic solution” alone does not mean feasible.
  • Union of convex sets is not convex; intersection is. The annulus \(1\le x_1^2+x_2^2\le4\) is the standard non-convex option.
  • A boundary point need not be an extreme point (the midpoint of an edge is not). A closed disc has infinitely many extreme points, an open disc none.
  • Unbounded feasible region does not mean unbounded objective. Unbounded objective needs an improving direction inside a nonempty region; an empty region is infeasible, not unbounded (2025 Q80 versus Q84).
  • Alternative optima needs a nonbasic \(Z_j-C_j=0\) at optimality and a positive minimum ratio, not just any zero in the row.
  • Infeasible means an artificial positive at the end (Big-M) or \(W>0\) (Phase I); a zero-level artificial means feasible but degenerate.
  • The \(Z_j-C_j\) sign test flips for minimisation. The ratio test uses positive entries only.
  • Make the right-hand side non-negative before adding artificials (multiply the row by \(-1\), flipping the inequality).
  • In a formulation, a percentage condition is not a constraint on one variable; write it against the total and multiply out.

2025 Q80

LPP: maximise \(z=x_1+x_2\) subject to \(x_1-x_2\ge0\), \(-3x_1+x_2\ge3\), \(x_1,x_2\ge0\). The solution is (A) feasible, (B) bounded, (C) infinitely many, (D) none of the above. Answer: D.

Solution: the constraints give \(x_2\le x_1\) and \(x_2\ge3+3x_1\), so \(3+3x_1\le x_1\), i.e. \(x_1\le-\tfrac32\), contradicting \(x_1\ge0\). The feasible region is empty, so there is no feasible, bounded or multiple solution; only “none of the above” holds.

2025 Q81

Which of the following sets in \(\mathbb R^2\) is not convex? (A) \(x_1^2+x_2^2\le1\); (B) \(x_1^2+x_2^2\ge1\) and \(x_1^2+x_2^2\le4\); (C) \(x_1^2+x_2^2\le4\); (D) \(x_1\ge0\). Answer: B.

Solution: A and C are discs, D is a half-plane, all convex. B is the ring (annulus) between radii 1 and 2: the points \((-\tfrac32,0)\) and \((\tfrac32,0)\) are in it, but their midpoint \((0,0)\) is not.

2025 Q83

Maximum value of \(z=5x_1+7x_2\) subject to \(x_1+x_2\le4\), \(3x_1+4x_2\le24\), \(10x_1+7x_2\le35\), \(x_1,x_2\ge0\) is (A) 0, (B) 17.5, (C) 28, (D) 34.8. Answer: C.

Solution: the corners are \((0,0)\) with \(z=0\), \((3.5,0)\) with \(17.5\), \((\tfrac73,\tfrac53)\) (intersection of constraints 1 and 3) with \(\tfrac{70}{3}\approx23.3\), and \((0,4)\) with \(z=28\). 2025 Q84

LPP: maximise \(z=2x_1-x_2\) subject to \(x_1-x_2\le1\), \(2x_1+x_2\ge6\), \(x_1,x_2\ge0\) has (A) no solution, (B) unbounded solution, (C) several alternative optimal solutions, (D) only one optimal solution. Answer: B.

Solution: the region is nonempty, e.g. \((3,0)\) satisfies both constraints. Along \(x_2=x_1-1\) (which satisfies \(x_1-x_2\le1\) with equality, and \(2x_1+x_2=3x_1-1\ge6\) for \(x_1\ge\tfrac73\)) we get \(z=2x_1-(x_1-1)=x_1+1\to\infty\). So the objective is unbounded.

2026 Q35

Constraints \(x+2y\le6\), \(2x+y\le6\), \(x,y\ge0\). The objective \(ax+by\) attains its maximum along the edge joining two vertices of the region. Find the ratio \(a:b\). No official key is available; the worked key (unofficial) gives A (\(1:2\)). Defect note: the question is flawed.

Solution: the vertices are \((0,0)\), \((3,0)\), \((2,2)\), \((0,3)\). Alternative optima need the objective parallel to an edge. The edge from \((2,2)\) to \((0,3)\) lies on \(x+2y=6\), giving \(a:b=1:2\); the edge from \((3,0)\) to \((2,2)\) lies on \(2x+y=6\), giving \(a:b=2:1\). By the symmetry \(x\leftrightarrow y\) of the region both fit, so the question has two defensible answers; the worked key picks \(1:2\).

2026 Q78

Two-phase method: Phase I ends with objective value 0, with artificial variables still in the basis at zero level, and the final Phase I tableau is dual feasible. What follows? Answer (worked key): D, the original problem is feasible and the basic feasible solution obtained is degenerate. The option wording is not available, so only the conclusion is given here.

Solution: \(W=0\) means the original constraints are satisfiable. An artificial variable that stays basic at value 0 is a basic variable at zero level, which is exactly a degenerate BFS. It is then pivoted out (or its row is removed if redundant) before Phase II.

Quick revision

  • Basic solutions of \(m\) equations in \(n\) unknowns: at most \(\binom nm\); BFS \(\Longleftrightarrow\) extreme point.
  • Feasible region and optimal set are convex; intersections are convex, unions not; annulus is non-convex.
  • Extreme points: closed disc infinitely many, open disc none, polygon its vertices, half-plane none.
  • Minkowski form = vertices plus directions; unbounded objective iff \(c^{T}d>0\) for a direction.
  • Formulation: variables, objective, one constraint per limit; percentages are multiplied out.
  • Simplex (max): enter the most negative \(Z_j-C_j\), leave by minimum ratio; stop when all \(Z_j-C_j\ge0\).
  • Unbounded: entering column with no positive entry. Alternative optima: nonbasic \(Z_j-C_j=0\) at the optimum. Degenerate: ratio tie.
  • Big-M: artificial positive at the end means infeasible. Two-phase: \(W>0\) infeasible; \(W=0\) with an artificial basic at 0 means feasible but degenerate.
  • \(\ge\): surplus plus artificial; \(=\): artificial; \(\le\): slack.
