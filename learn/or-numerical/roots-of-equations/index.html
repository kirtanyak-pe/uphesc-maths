Why it matters for the exam

Newton-Raphson is the most reliable numerical-analysis topic in this paper: the 2025 paper asked both its formula (Q82) and its order of convergence (Q87). Expect one direct question per paper: the formula, the order of a method, or the condition \(\lvert g'(x)\rvert<1\) for fixed-point iteration. Bisection counts, the secant order \(1.618\) and the square-root / reciprocal iterations are the usual follow-ups.

Core ideas

Setting and locating roots

We solve \(f(x)=0\) where \(f\) is algebraic (a polynomial) or transcendental (\(e^x\), \(\sin x\), \(\log x\) …). Direct formulas do not exist in general, so we build a sequence \(x_0,x_1,x_2,\dots\) converging to a root \(\alpha\).

Locating a root first:

  • Intermediate value theorem: if \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(f(a)f(b)<0\), then \(f\) has at least one root in \((a,b)\).
  • Tabulation: evaluate \(f\) at \(0,1,2,\dots\) until the sign changes. For \(x^3-x-1\): \(f(1)=-1\), \(f(2)=5\), so a root lies in \((1,2)\).
  • Cauchy bound: every root of \(a_0x^n+a_1x^{n-1}+\dots+a_n\) satisfies \(\lvert x\rvert<1+\max_i\lvert a_i/a_0\rvert\).
  • Descartes’ rule of signs: the number of positive roots is the number of sign changes in the coefficients, or less by an even number. Negative roots: apply it to \(f(-x)\).

Order and rate of convergence

Let \(e_n=x_n-\alpha\). A method has order \(p\) and asymptotic error constant \(C\) if

\[\lvert e_{n+1}\rvert\approx C\,\lvert e_n\rvert^{p}.\]
  • \(p=1\): linear (needs \(C<1\)). Each step gains a fixed number of correct digits.
  • \(p=2\): quadratic. The number of correct digits roughly doubles each step.
  • \(1<p<2\): superlinear.

Orders of the standard methods (for a simple root):

Method Order \(p\) Needs derivative? Keeps bracket?
Bisection \(1\) (error halves each step) no yes
Regula falsi (false position) \(1\) (linear, usually slower in practice than secant) no yes
Secant \(\dfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\approx1.618\) no no
Fixed-point iteration \(1\) if \(g'(\alpha)\neq0\) no no
Newton-Raphson \(2\) yes (\(f'\)) no
Halley / Chebyshev \(3\) yes (\(f',f''\)) no

Bisection method

Start with \([a,b]\), \(f(a)f(b)<0\). Repeat: \(c=\dfrac{a+b}{2}\); keep the half where the sign change remains.

After \(n\) steps the interval length is \((b-a)/2^n\), so the error of the midpoint is at most \((b-a)/2^{n+1}\) (or \((b-a)/2^n\) if the last interval is used). To get error below \(\varepsilon\):

\[n\ \ge\ \frac{\ln\big((b-a)/\varepsilon\big)}{\ln 2}.\]

Always converges (for continuous \(f\) with a sign change), but only linearly with ratio \(\tfrac12\). It finds a root in the bracket, not a chosen one.

Regula falsi (false position)

Also needs a bracket. Instead of the midpoint, use the point where the chord joining \((a,f(a))\) and \((b,f(b))\) cuts the \(x\)-axis:

\[c=\frac{a\,f(b)-b\,f(a)}{f(b)-f(a)}.\]

Replace \(a\) or \(b\) by \(c\) so that the sign change is kept. It always converges, order \(1\). A known defect: when \(f\) is convex or concave on the bracket, one endpoint stays fixed forever, so convergence can be slow.

Secant method

Same chord formula, but no bracket is kept: use the last two iterates.

\[x_{n+1}=\frac{x_{n-1}f(x_n)-x_nf(x_{n-1})}{f(x_n)-f(x_{n-1})}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{\dfrac{f(x_n)-f(x_{n-1})}{x_n-x_{n-1}}}.\]

It replaces \(f'(x_n)\) in Newton’s formula by a divided difference. The error satisfies \(e_{n+1}\approx\dfrac{f''(\alpha)}{2f'(\alpha)}\,e_ne_{n-1}\). Trying \(\lvert e_{n+1}\rvert=C\lvert e_n\rvert^p\) gives \(p^2=p+1\), hence \(p=\dfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\approx1.618\) (the golden ratio). Needs two starting values; may diverge if they are poor. One new function value per step, so per function evaluation it is often cheaper than Newton.

Fixed-point iteration

Rewrite \(f(x)=0\) as \(x=g(x)\) and iterate \(x_{n+1}=g(x_n)\).

Convergence theorem (contraction mapping). Let \(g\) be continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\) with

  1. \(g(x)\in[a,b]\) for all \(x\in[a,b]\), and
  2. \(\lvert g'(x)\rvert\le k<1\) for all \(x\in(a,b)\).

Then \(g\) has a unique fixed point \(\alpha\in[a,b]\) and \(x_{n+1}=g(x_n)\) converges to \(\alpha\) for every \(x_0\in[a,b]\). Error bounds:

\[\lvert x_n-\alpha\rvert\le k^n\lvert x_0-\alpha\rvert,\qquad \lvert x_n-\alpha\rvert\le\frac{k^n}{1-k}\lvert x_1-x_0\rvert.\]

Local version: if \(g'\) is continuous and \(\lvert g'(\alpha)\rvert<1\), convergence holds for \(x_0\) close enough to \(\alpha\).

Why: \(e_{n+1}=g(x_n)-g(\alpha)=g'(\xi_n)e_n\). So the order is \(1\) with constant \(\lvert g'(\alpha)\rvert\) when \(g'(\alpha)\ne0\). The smaller \(\lvert g'\rvert\), the faster the convergence. If \(\lvert g'(\alpha)\rvert>1\) the iteration diverges from every nearby start. If \(g'(\alpha)=0\) and \(g''(\alpha)\neq0\), the order is \(2\).

The same equation gives different iterations. For \(x^2-x-2=0\) (root \(2\)):

Rearrangement \(g'(2)\) Result
\(x=x^2-2\) \(4\) diverges
\(x=\sqrt{x+2}\) \(\tfrac14\) converges
\(x=1+\dfrac2x\) \(-\tfrac12\) converges (oscillating)

The sign of \(g'\) tells the pattern: \(g'>0\) gives monotone convergence, \(g'<0\) gives alternating (zig-zag) convergence.

Newton-Raphson method

Formula.

\[\boxed{x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}}\]

Geometric meaning. Draw the tangent to \(y=f(x)\) at \((x_n,f(x_n))\). It meets the \(x\)-axis at \(x_{n+1}\). The tangent is \(y-f(x_n)=f'(x_n)(x-x_n)\); setting \(y=0\) gives the formula.

Derivation from Taylor. \(0=f(\alpha)\approx f(x_n)+f'(x_n)(\alpha-x_n)\) gives \(\alpha\approx x_n-f/f'\).

As a fixed-point method. \(g(x)=x-\dfrac{f(x)}{f'(x)}\), so

\[g'(x)=\frac{f(x)f''(x)}{[f'(x)]^2}.\]

At a simple root (\(f(\alpha)=0\), \(f'(\alpha)\ne0\)) we get \(g'(\alpha)=0\), so convergence is at least quadratic.

Quadratic convergence. Taylor expansion about \(x_n\) evaluated at \(\alpha\), with \(f(\alpha)=0\):

\[e_{n+1}\approx\frac{f''(\alpha)}{2f'(\alpha)}\,e_n^{2}.\]

The order is \(2\) for a simple root.

Sufficient condition for convergence on \([a,b]\): \(f',f''\) continuous and nonzero (do not change sign) on \([a,b]\), \(f(a)f(b)<0\), and \(\lvert f f''\rvert<(f')^2\) on \([a,b]\). A common practical rule: start at the end where \(f(x_0)f''(x_0)>0\) (the Fourier condition); the iterates then move monotonically to the root.

Failure cases. \(f'(x_n)=0\) or very small (tangent is horizontal, step is huge); a poor starting value can lead to another root, a cycle, or divergence (for example \(f(x)=x^{1/3}\) diverges from every \(x_0\ne0\)).

Multiple root. If \(\alpha\) is a root of multiplicity \(m>1\) then \(g'(\alpha)=1-\tfrac1m\neq0\) and Newton’s method becomes linear, with ratio \(\dfrac{m-1}{m}\) (ratio \(\tfrac12\) for a double root). The modified iteration \(x_{n+1}=x_n-m\dfrac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}\) restores order \(2\).

Newton iterations for roots and reciprocals

These are very common in exams. They need no division (reciprocal) or only one.

Quantity Apply Newton to Iteration
\(\sqrt N\) \(f(x)=x^2-N\) \(x_{n+1}=\dfrac12\left(x_n+\dfrac N{x_n}\right)\)
\(\sqrt[k]{N}\) \(f(x)=x^k-N\) \(x_{n+1}=\dfrac1k\left((k-1)x_n+\dfrac N{x_n^{k-1}}\right)\)
\(\sqrt[3]{N}\) \(f(x)=x^3-N\) \(x_{n+1}=\dfrac{2x_n+N/x_n^2}{3}\)
\(1/N\) \(f(x)=\dfrac1x-N\) \(x_{n+1}=x_n(2-Nx_n)\)
\(1/\sqrt N\) \(f(x)=\dfrac1{x^2}-N\) \(x_{n+1}=\dfrac{x_n}{2}\left(3-Nx_n^2\right)\)

For \(\sqrt N\) the error obeys exactly \(e_{n+1}=\dfrac{e_n^2}{2x_n}\), and for any \(x_0>0\) the iterates after the first step decrease monotonically to \(\sqrt N\) from above. For \(1/N\): the relative error obeys \(1-Nx_{n+1}=(1-Nx_n)^2\), so the iteration converges iff \(0<x_0<2/N\).

Aitken’s \(\Delta^2\) process and Steffensen’s method

For a linearly convergent sequence \(x_n\to\alpha\) with \(e_{n+1}\approx Ke_n\), three consecutive terms give a better estimate

\[\hat x=x_n-\frac{(\Delta x_n)^2}{\Delta^2x_n}=x_n-\frac{(x_{n+1}-x_n)^2}{x_{n+2}-2x_{n+1}+x_n}.\]

Example: for \(g(x)=\sqrt{x+2}\) from \(x_0=1\), the iterates are \(1,\ 1.7321,\ 1.9319\) and the formula gives \(\hat x=2.0069\), much nearer the root \(2\) (errors \(0.0069\) against \(0.068\)).

Steffensen’s method applies Aitken after every two iterations: from \(x_n\) compute \(y=g(x_n)\), \(z=g(y)\) and then

\[x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{(y-x_n)^2}{z-2y+x_n}.\]

It needs no derivative yet is quadratically convergent (order \(2\)), even though the underlying \(g\) gives only order \(1\). For \(x=\cos x\) from \(x_0=0.5\): \(x_1=0.7314\), \(x_2=0.73908\), \(x_3=0.7390851\) (root \(0.7390851\)). Trap: Aitken is an acceleration, not a new root-finder, and it fails when \(\Delta^2x_n=0\).

Muller’s method

Muller’s method fits a parabola through the last three points \((x_{n-2},f)\), \((x_{n-1},f)\), \((x_n,f)\) and takes the root of the parabola nearest \(x_n\). With \(h_1=x_{n-1}-x_{n-2}\), \(h_2=x_n-x_{n-1}\), \(\delta_1=\dfrac{f(x_{n-1})-f(x_{n-2})}{h_1}\), \(\delta_2=\dfrac{f(x_n)-f(x_{n-1})}{h_2}\):

\[a=\frac{\delta_2-\delta_1}{h_2+h_1},\quad b=a h_2+\delta_2,\quad c=f(x_n),\qquad x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{2c}{b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}},\]

with the sign chosen to make the denominator larger in modulus. Its order is about \(1.84\) (root of \(p^3=p^2+p+1\)), between secant (\(1.618\)) and Newton (\(2\)), and it needs no derivative. Because the square root may be negative, it can reach complex roots from real starting values. Example: \(f=x^3-x-1\) with \(0,1,2\): \(a=3\), \(b=9\), \(c=5\), \(x_3=2-\dfrac{10}{9+\sqrt{41}}=1.2638\).

Multiple roots and Birge-Vieta

If the multiplicity \(m\) is unknown, apply Newton to \(u(x)=f(x)/f'(x)\), which has a simple root at \(\alpha\) whatever \(m\) is:

\[x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)f'(x_n)}{[f'(x_n)]^2-f(x_n)f''(x_n)}.\]

This is quadratic for any multiplicity. Check with \(f=(x-1)^2\) from \(x_0=2\): \(f=1\), \(f'=2\), \(f''=2\), so \(x_1=2-\dfrac{2}{4-2}=1\) at once. Trap: \(x-mf/f'\) needs \(m\) known, and it is not Newton’s plain formula.

Birge-Vieta is Newton’s method for a polynomial \(p\), with \(p(x_n)\) and \(p'(x_n)\) obtained by two rounds of synthetic division. For \(p=x^3-x-1\) at \(x_n=1.5\): dividing \(1,0,-1,-1\) by \(x-1.5\) gives \(1,\ 1.5,\ 1.25,\ 0.875\), so \(p(1.5)=0.875\); dividing \(1,1.5,1.25\) again gives \(1,\ 3,\ 5.75\), so \(p'(1.5)=5.75\). Then \(x_{n+1}=1.5-\dfrac{0.875}{5.75}=1.3478\).

Graeffe’s root-squaring method (basic level)

Graeffe’s method finds all roots of a polynomial (real or complex) without a starting guess. Idea: build a polynomial whose roots are the squares of the roots of \(f\), and repeat so the roots separate in size.

If \(f(x)=\prod(x-\alpha_i)\), define

\[g(y)=(-1)^nf(\sqrt y)\,f(-\sqrt y),\qquad\text{roots of }g\text{ are }\alpha_i^2.\]

Coefficient rule for \(f=a_0x^n+a_1x^{n-1}+\dots+a_n\): the coefficients of the squared polynomial (up to sign) are

\[b_k=a_k^2-2a_{k-1}a_{k+1}+2a_{k-2}a_{k+2}-\cdots\]

After \(m\) squarings the roots are \(\alpha_i^{2^m}\). If they are real and well separated in modulus, Vieta’s relations give \(\lvert\alpha_k^{2^m}\rvert\approx\left\lvert\dfrac{b_k}{b_{k-1}}\right\rvert\), so

\[\lvert\alpha_k\rvert\approx\left\lvert\frac{b_k}{b_{k-1}}\right\rvert^{1/2^m}.\]

The signs of the roots are found by substituting \(\pm\) values in the original \(f\). Its advantage is that it needs no initial approximation and finds complex roots too; its weakness is rapid growth of coefficients and trouble with equal-modulus roots.

Formulas and results

  • Bisection: \(c=\tfrac{a+b}2\); steps needed \(n\ge\log_2\dfrac{b-a}{\varepsilon}\); order \(1\), ratio \(\tfrac12\).
  • Regula falsi: \(c=\dfrac{af(b)-bf(a)}{f(b)-f(a)}\); order \(1\); bracket kept.
  • Secant: \(x_{n+1}=\dfrac{x_{n-1}f(x_n)-x_nf(x_{n-1})}{f(x_n)-f(x_{n-1})}\); order \(\dfrac{1+\sqrt5}2\).
  • Fixed point: \(x_{n+1}=g(x_n)\) converges if \(\lvert g'\rvert\le k<1\) on an interval mapped into itself; \(e_{n+1}\approx g'(\alpha)e_n\).
  • Newton: \(x_{n+1}=x_n-\dfrac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}\); \(e_{n+1}\approx\dfrac{f''(\alpha)}{2f'(\alpha)}e_n^2\); order \(2\).
  • Newton on a root of multiplicity \(m\): linear, ratio \(1-\tfrac1m\); fix with \(x-m f/f'\).
  • \(\sqrt N\): \(\tfrac12(x+N/x)\). \(1/N\): \(x(2-Nx)\). \(\sqrt[3]N\): \(\tfrac13(2x+N/x^2)\).
  • Newton’s \(g'(x)=\dfrac{ff''}{f'^2}\).
  • Graeffe: roots of the new polynomial are \(\alpha_i^2\); \(\lvert\alpha_k\rvert\approx\lvert b_k/b_{k-1}\rvert^{1/2^m}\).
  • Aitken: \(\hat x=x_n-\dfrac{(\Delta x_n)^2}{\Delta^2x_n}\); Steffensen (Aitken on \(g\)) has order \(2\) with no derivative.
  • Muller: parabola through three points, order \(\approx1.84\), can give complex roots.
  • Unknown multiplicity: Newton on \(f/f'\), \(x-\dfrac{ff'}{f'^2-ff''}\), quadratic. Birge-Vieta: Newton with synthetic division.

Worked examples

Example 1: bisection for \(x^3-x-1=0\) on \([1,2]\)

\(f(1)=-1<0\), \(f(2)=5>0\).

Step \([a,b]\) \(c\) \(f(c)\) New interval
1 \([1,2]\) \(1.5\) \(0.875>0\) \([1,1.5]\)
2 \([1,1.5]\) \(1.25\) \(-0.2969<0\) \([1.25,1.5]\)
3 \([1.25,1.5]\) \(1.375\) \(0.2246>0\) \([1.25,1.375]\)
4 \([1.25,1.375]\) \(1.3125\) \(-0.0515<0\) \([1.3125,1.375]\)

The root is \(1.3247\ldots\). To reach error \(<10^{-3}\) on an interval of length \(1\): \(n\ge\log_2 1000\approx9.97\), so \(10\) iterations.

Example 2: Newton for \(\sqrt2\)

\(f=x^2-2\), \(x_{n+1}=\tfrac12(x_n+2/x_n)\), \(x_0=1.5\).

\(x_1=1.416667\), \(x_2=1.414216\), \(x_3=1.414214\) (true \(\sqrt2=1.41421356\)). The correct digits go \(1,3,6\): roughly doubling. Also, for \(\sqrt{10}\) starting at \(x_0=3\): \(x_1=\tfrac12(3+\tfrac{10}3)=\tfrac{19}6\approx3.1667\).

Example 3: reciprocal of 7 without division

\(x_{n+1}=x_n(2-7x_n)\), \(x_0=0.1\) (valid since \(0<0.1<2/7\)).

\(x_1=0.13\), \(x_2=0.1417\), \(x_3=0.142848\), \(x_4=0.14285714\) (\(1/7=0.142857142857\ldots\)). The error \(1-7x_n\) goes \(0.3,\,0.09,\,0.0081,\,\dots\) (squared each time).

Example 4: which rearrangement converges?

Solve \(x^2-x-2=0\) near \(x=2\) with \(g(x)=\sqrt{x+2}\): \(g'(x)=\dfrac1{2\sqrt{x+2}}\), \(g'(2)=\tfrac14<1\). Starting \(x_0=1\): \(1.7321,\,1.9319,\,1.9829,\,1.9957,\,1.9989,\,1.9997\ldots\). The error shrinks by about \(\tfrac14\) each step, matching \(\lvert g'(2)\rvert=0.25\). With \(g(x)=x^2-2\), \(g'(2)=4>1\), so iteration moves away from \(2\).

Another: \(x=\cos x\) has \(g'=-\sin x\), \(\lvert g'(0.739)\rvert=0.674<1\). From \(0.5\): \(0.8776,\,0.6390,\,0.8027,\,0.6948,\,0.7682,\dots\to0.739085\); alternating because \(g'<0\), slow because \(0.674\) is close to \(1\).

Example 5: regula falsi and secant for \(x^3-x-1\), start \(1,2\)

Chord through \((1,-1)\) and \((2,5)\): \(c=\dfrac{1\cdot5-2\cdot(-1)}{5+1}=\dfrac76=1.1667\), \(f=-0.5787\).

Regula falsi keeps \(b=2\) forever: \(1.1667,\ 1.2531,\ 1.2934,\ 1.3113,\dots\) (slow, one-sided). Secant uses the last two points: \(1.1667,\ 1.2531,\ 1.3372,\ 1.32385,\ 1.32471\ (\text{true }1.324718)\). It is much faster.

Example 6: Newton on a double root

\(f(x)=(x-1)^2\), \(x_{n+1}=x_n-\dfrac{(x_n-1)^2}{2(x_n-1)}=\dfrac{x_n+1}2\). From \(x_0=2\) the errors are \(0.5,\,0.25,\,0.125,\dots\): only linear with ratio \(\tfrac12=\tfrac{m-1}m\). Using \(x-2f/f'\) gives \(x_1=1\) at once.

Common traps

  • Formula sign: Newton is \(x_n-f/f'\), not \(x_n+f/f'\) and not \(x_n-f'/f\). Options in exams differ only by this.
  • Bisection vs regula falsi order: both are linear (order \(1\)). The secant method is \(1.618\), not \(2\).
  • Newton is order \(2\) only for a simple root. For a multiple root it is linear. The phrase “quadratic convergence” always hides this hypothesis.
  • Fixed point: the condition is \(\lvert g'\rvert<1\) near the root (on an interval mapped into itself), not \(\lvert f'\rvert<1\), and not \(g'<1\) (negative large values diverge).
  • \(\lvert g'\rvert<1\) is sufficient for convergence; it is not necessary globally (the condition at \(\alpha\) is \(\lvert g'(\alpha)\rvert<1\), and for \(\lvert g'(\alpha)\rvert=1\) the test is inconclusive).
  • Rate of convergence of Newton is \(2\), not \(1\) and not \(0\). “Rate” in some books means “order”.
  • Regula falsi keeps a bracket but is not faster than the secant method.
  • Bisection needs \(f(a)f(b)<0\); it fails to detect a root of even multiplicity where \(f\) touches zero without changing sign (for example \((x-1)^2\)).
  • Graeffe’s method squares the roots, not the coefficients.
  • Secant method needs two initial values but no derivative; Newton needs one initial value and \(f'\).

2025-Q82 (official key: A)

Question. The Newton-Raphson formula of successive approximation to find the value of a root of \(f(x)=0\) is: (A) \(x_{n+1}=x_n-\dfrac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}\) (the other options are variations with \(f\) and \(f'\) interchanged or with a wrong sign). Answer: A. The tangent at \(x_n\) meets the axis at \(x_n-f(x_n)/f'(x_n)\).

2025-Q87 (official key: A)

Question. The rate (order) of convergence of the Newton-Raphson method is (A) \(2\) (B) \(1\) (C) \(0\) (D) \(3\). Answer: A, order \(2\). At a simple root \(g(x)=x-f/f'\) has \(g'(\alpha)=0\), and \(e_{n+1}\approx\dfrac{f''(\alpha)}{2f'(\alpha)}e_n^2\). Quadratic convergence.

Quick revision

  • Newton: \(x_{n+1}=x_n-f(x_n)/f'(x_n)\); tangent meets the \(x\)-axis; order \(2\) (simple root).
  • Secant: order \(\tfrac{1+\sqrt5}2\approx1.618\); no derivative, no bracket.
  • Bisection: order \(1\), error halves; \(n\ge\log_2((b-a)/\varepsilon)\) steps.
  • Regula falsi: order \(1\), always brackets, one endpoint can get stuck.
  • Fixed point converges if \(\lvert g'\rvert<1\) near the root; order \(1\) with constant \(\lvert g'(\alpha)\rvert\).
  • Newton’s \(g'=ff''/f'^2\), so \(g'(\alpha)=0\) at a simple root.
  • Multiple root of multiplicity \(m\): Newton is linear, ratio \(1-1/m\); use \(x-mf/f'\).
  • \(\sqrt N\): \(\tfrac12(x+N/x)\); \(1/N\): \(x(2-Nx)\) (needs \(0<x_0<2/N\)).
  • Graeffe: repeated root-squaring; \(\lvert\alpha_k\rvert\approx\lvert b_k/b_{k-1}\rvert^{1/2^m}\).
  • Aitken \(\Delta^2\) speeds up a linear sequence; Steffensen is order \(2\) without \(f'\); Muller (order \(1.84\)) uses a parabola.
  • \(g'>0\) gives monotone, \(g'<0\) gives zig-zag convergence.
