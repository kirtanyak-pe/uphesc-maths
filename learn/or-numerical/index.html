What this unit covers

Two blocks that are separate in method but share one exam style: short computations done by hand in two or three minutes.

Operations research (four topics).

  1. Linear programming: formulation, convex sets and extreme points, basic feasible solutions, the graphical method, the simplex tableau, Big-M and two-phase methods, and recognising unbounded, infeasible, degenerate and alternative-optima cases.
  2. Duality: how to write the dual, weak and strong duality, complementary slackness, reading the dual solution from a tableau, the dual simplex method.
  3. Transportation and assignment: balanced and unbalanced problems, initial solutions (NWCR, least cost, VAM), the MODI test, degeneracy, and the Hungarian method.
  4. Game theory: saddle point, mixed strategies, the \(2\times2\) formula, dominance, the graphical method and the LP form.

Numerical analysis (five topics).

  1. Roots of equations: bisection, regula falsi, secant, fixed point iteration, Newton-Raphson and their orders of convergence.
  2. Linear systems: Gauss elimination, pivoting, LU, and the Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel iterations with convergence conditions.
  3. Finite differences and interpolation: the operators, Newton forward and backward formulas, Lagrange, divided differences, Hermite and cubic splines.
  4. Numerical differentiation and integration: difference formulas, trapezoid, Simpson, Weddle, Gauss-Legendre and Romberg, with error terms.
  5. ODEs: Picard, Taylor, Euler, modified Euler, Runge-Kutta, and the multistep predictor-corrector formulas.

The syllabus line has a typo (“ordinary differential equitation”); it is corrected above and means “equation”.

How it is asked

  • Volume. The unit gave 8 questions in 2025 (4 OR, 4 numerical) and 7 in 2026 (4 OR, 3 numerical). In the partial 2019 paper only 1 was recovered. This is one of the steadier units: expect 6 to 8 questions.
  • Operations research. Mostly “what does this situation imply” questions on LP: the nature of a solution (none, unbounded, alternative, unique), a set that is not convex, the dual of a given LP, what primal alternative optima mean for the dual, what a zero Phase I objective with artificials still in the basis means, and what multiple optima after MODI mean for a transportation problem. Numeric ones are short: the maximum of an objective over a small region, or the ratio \(a:b\) that makes an edge optimal. A long hand-pivoted tableau has not appeared in the recovered papers.
  • Numerical analysis. Statements about methods and short calculations: the Newton-Raphson iteration and its order, a divided difference of \(\frac1x\), an interpolating polynomial from a small table, Gauss-Seidel versus Jacobi speed, stability of Euler and Runge-Kutta, and which formula is more accurate for smooth functions.
  • Traps. Options usually differ in one detail: a sign in a dual constraint, \(m+n-1\) versus \(m+n\), order \(2\) versus \(1.618\), \(h^2\) versus \(h^4\) in an error term. Learn the exact statements.
  • Defects to expect. In the recovered 2026 papers, Q35 (LP) and Q65 (duality) have defective keys or options, and the options of Q78 and Q94 could not be recovered. The topic notes mark these. Do not memorise those four answers as facts.

High-yield list

  1. \(m+n-1\) basic cells in a transportation BFS (not \(m+n\)); fewer positive cells means degenerate.
  2. Duality sign table and the three outcomes (both optimal, one unbounded and the other infeasible, both infeasible).
  3. Complementary slackness and how to read the dual from the final tableau.
  4. Which tableau signals unbounded, infeasible (artificial in the basis), alternative optima and degenerate cases.
  5. MODI: \(\Delta_{ij}=c_{ij}-u_i-v_j\); all \(\ge0\) is optimal in a minimisation; a zero means another optimum.
  6. Hungarian method: minimum number of lines equals \(n\) at the optimum.
  7. Game theory: check for a saddle point before using the \(2\times2\) formula; dominance direction for the column player.
  8. Newton-Raphson is quadratic with \(e_{n+1}\approx\frac{f''}{2f'}e_n^2\); fixed point iteration is linear with ratio \(g'(\alpha)\); secant has order \(1.618\).
  9. Gauss-Seidel converges for strictly diagonally dominant matrices; \(\rho(T)<1\) is the exact criterion.
  10. Divided difference identities, in particular \(f[a,b,c,d]=-\frac1{abcd}\) for \(f=\frac1x\).
  11. Degree of the Hermite interpolating polynomial (\(2n+1\) on \(n+1\) nodes) and the natural spline end conditions.
  12. Trapezoid error \(\propto h^2f''\); Simpson error \(\propto h^4f^{(4)}\) and exact for cubics.
  13. Euler is \(O(h)\) globally, modified Euler \(O(h^2)\), RK4 \(O(h^4)\).
  14. Picard iteration: write the integral form and iterate; it converges to the unique solution under a Lipschitz condition.

Official syllabus

Linear programming problem, solution of linear programming problem by graphical and simplex methods, M-technique, Two-phase method, Dual problem and duality theorem, convex set theory, balanced and unbalanced transportation problems, Hungarian method for solving assignment problems, Game theory. Numerical solutions of algebraic equations, Fixed point iteration and Newton-Raphson methods, Solution of systems of linear algebraic equations using Gauss elimination and Gauss-Seidel methods, Finite differences, Lagrange, Hermite and spline interpolations, Numerical differentiation and integration, Numerical solutions of ordinary differential equation using Picard, Euler, modified Euler and Runge-Kutta methods.
