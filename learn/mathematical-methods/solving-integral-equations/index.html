Why it matters for the exam

This is the highest-yield page of Unit 7. The separable-kernel method is examined directly (2025 Q76, 2026 Q39) and through its theory: the number of eigenvalues, orthogonality of eigenfunctions, the consistency (rank) condition and the Fredholm determinant (2026 Q32, Q82, Q97). Successive approximations and Volterra equations (2026 Q89) complete the syllabus line.

Core ideas

Fredholm equation with a separable kernel

Solve \(\varphi(x)=f(x)+\lambda\int_a^bK(x,t)\varphi(t)\,dt\) with \(K(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^na_i(x)b_i(t)\).

Step 1. Define the constants \(c_i=\int_a^bb_i(t)\varphi(t)\,dt\). Then

\(\varphi(x)=f(x)+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^nc_i\,a_i(x).\)

Step 2. Multiply by \(b_k(x)\) and integrate over \([a,b]\):

\(c_k=f_k+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^nA_{ki}c_i,\qquad f_k=\int b_kf\,dx,\quad A_{ki}=\int b_ka_i\,dx .\)

In matrix form \((I-\lambda A)\,\mathbf c=\mathbf f\).

Step 3. Solve the \(n\times n\) system. Substitute \(c_i\) back into \(\varphi\).

The polynomial \(D(\lambda)=\det(I-\lambda A)\) (degree \(\le n\), \(D(0)=1\)) is the Fredholm determinant of the separable kernel. Its roots are the eigenvalues (characteristic values) \(\lambda\). They are the reciprocals of the non-zero eigenvalues of the matrix \(A\).

Eigenvalues and eigenfunctions (homogeneous case \(f=0\)). Solve \((I-\lambda A)\mathbf c=0\) for \(\lambda\) with \(\det(I-\lambda A)=0\). For each such \(\lambda\) the eigenfunction is \(\varphi(x)=\sum c_ia_i(x)\) (up to a constant factor).

Facts for a separable kernel of rank \(n\):

  • there are at most \(n\) eigenvalues (counted by multiplicity of roots of \(D\));
  • every eigenfunction lies in \(\operatorname{span}\{a_1,\dots,a_n\}\);
  • if \(K\) is symmetric of rank \(n\), there are exactly \(n\) eigenvalues counted with multiplicity, all real, and eigenfunctions of distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

Fredholm alternative

For the second-kind equation with \(\lambda\) fixed, exactly one of the following holds.

(a) \(\lambda\) is not an eigenvalue (\(D(\lambda)\ne0\)). The homogeneous equation has only the trivial solution, and the inhomogeneous equation has a unique solution for every \(f\).

(b) \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue (\(D(\lambda)=0\)). The homogeneous equation has non-trivial solutions (finitely many independent ones). The inhomogeneous equation has a solution iff \(f\) is orthogonal to every solution \(\psi\) of the adjoint homogeneous equation \(\psi(x)=\lambda\int_a^bK(t,x)\psi(t)\,dt\):

\(\int_a^bf(x)\psi(x)\,dx=0 .\)

When this holds the solution is not unique: add any eigenfunction. For a symmetric kernel the adjoint equation is the same equation, so \(f\) must be orthogonal to the eigenfunctions of \(\lambda\) itself.

For a separable kernel the same statement is the rank condition: \((I-\lambda A)\mathbf c=\mathbf f\) is consistent iff \(\operatorname{rank}(I-\lambda A)=\operatorname{rank}\,[\,I-\lambda A\mid\mathbf f\,]\). If \(D(\lambda)=0\) and the rank condition fails, the equation has no solution.

Three further facts (Fredholm’s theorems):

  • the number of independent eigenfunctions of \(\lambda\) equals the number of independent solutions of the adjoint equation;
  • eigenvalues (of a nonzero \(L^2\) kernel) form a set with no finite accumulation point, so they are isolated;
  • the eigenvalue sets of \(K(x,t)\) and \(\overline{K(t,x)}\) coincide (conjugates).

Method of successive substitutions (Neumann series)

Write \(\varphi=f+\lambda T\varphi\) with \((T\varphi)(x)=\int K(x,t)\varphi(t)\,dt\) and substitute repeatedly:

\(\varphi=f+\lambda Tf+\lambda^2T^2f+\cdots+\lambda^nT^nf+\cdots\)

In kernel form:

\(\varphi(x)=f(x)+\sum_{n\ge1}\lambda^n\int_a^bK_n(x,t)f(t)\,dt,\)

with iterated kernels

\(K_1=K,\qquad K_{n+1}(x,t)=\int_a^bK(x,s)K_n(s,t)\,ds .\)

The resolvent kernel \(R(x,t;\lambda)=\sum_{n\ge0}\lambda^nK_{n+1}(x,t)\) gives

\(\varphi(x)=f(x)+\lambda\int_a^bR(x,t;\lambda)f(t)\,dt .\)

\(R\) satisfies \(R(x,t;\lambda)=K(x,t)+\lambda\int K(x,s)R(s,t;\lambda)\,ds\). Also \(K_{m+n}(x,t)=\int K_m(x,s)K_n(s,t)\,ds\).

Convergence (Fredholm). If \(K\) is bounded, \(\lvert K\rvert\le M\), the series converges for

\(\lvert\lambda\rvert\,M\,(b-a)<1 .\)

For an \(L^2\) kernel the condition is \(\lvert\lambda\rvert<1/B\) with \(B=\sqrt{\iint K^2\,dx\,dt}\). These are sufficient, not necessary: the true radius of convergence is the smallest \(\lvert\lambda_1\rvert\) of the eigenvalues (the pole of the resolvent nearest \(0\)). For \(K=xt\) on \([0,1]\) the \(L^2\) bound is \(1/B=3\) (since \(B^2=\iint x^2t^2=\tfrac19\)), which happens to be exactly the eigenvalue; the cruder \(M\)-bound (\(M=1\), \(b-a=1\)) gives only \(\lvert\lambda\rvert<1\).

Convergence (Volterra). \(\lvert K_n(x,t)\rvert\le M^n(x-t)^{n-1}/(n-1)!\), so the series converges for every \(\lambda\). A Volterra equation of the second kind (continuous kernel and \(f\)) has a unique solution for every \(\lambda\) and every \(f\). It has no eigenvalues: the homogeneous Volterra equation has only \(\varphi=0\).

Successive approximations (Picard iteration)

Start with \(\varphi_0(x)\) (usually \(f\) or \(0\)) and iterate

\(\varphi_{n+1}(x)=f(x)+\lambda\int K(x,t)\varphi_n(t)\,dt .\)

This produces the partial sums of the Neumann series (starting from \(\varphi_0=f\), \(\varphi_n=\sum_{k\le n}\lambda^kT^kf\)). The iterates converge uniformly to the unique solution for Volterra equations always, and for Fredholm equations when \(\lvert\lambda\rvert\) is small enough (as above). In the syllabus, “substitutions” and “approximations” are the same idea: one writes out the series, the other computes the sequence.

Fredholm determinant and the resolvent

For an \(L^2\) kernel the resolvent is a ratio \(R(x,t;\lambda)=\dfrac{D(x,t;\lambda)}{D(\lambda)}\) of entire functions. \(D(\lambda)=\det(I-\lambda T)\) is the Fredholm determinant. With \(A_n=\int K_n(s,s)\,ds\) (traces),

\(D(\lambda)=\exp\Big(-\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\lambda^nA_n}{n}\Big).\)

Facts:

  • \(D(\lambda)=0\) exactly at the eigenvalues.
  • For \(\lambda\) with \(D(\lambda)\ne0\) the resolvent exists and the equation is uniquely solvable.
  • \(\varphi=\lambda T\varphi\) has a non-trivial solution iff \(D(\lambda)=0\) (2026 Q97).
  • For \(K=xt\) on \([0,1]\): \(A_n=3^{-n}\), \(D(\lambda)=1-\lambda/3\).

Symmetric kernels and Hilbert-Schmidt theory

Let \(K\) be real, symmetric and in \(L^2\) (and not identically zero).

  1. All eigenvalues are real.
  2. Eigenfunctions belonging to different eigenvalues are orthogonal: \(\int\varphi_m\varphi_n\,dx=0\).
  3. There is at least one eigenvalue, and the eigenvalues form a countable set with no finite limit point. Each has finite multiplicity.
  4. Orthonormal eigenfunctions \(\{\varphi_n\}\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda_n\) (\(\lambda_n\to\infty\) in modulus if there are infinitely many) satisfy

\(\sum_n\frac1{\lambda_n^2}=\iint K(x,t)^2\,dx\,dt,\qquad \sum_n\frac1{\lambda_n}=\int K(x,x)\,dx\ \ (\text{continuous, symmetric, positive semidefinite (Mercer) kernel}).\)

  1. Hilbert-Schmidt theorem. If \(g(x)=\int K(x,t)h(t)\,dt\) with \(h\) square-integrable, then \(g\) has the absolutely and uniformly convergent expansion \(g=\sum_n(g,\varphi_n)\varphi_n\) in the orthonormal eigenfunctions.
  2. Solution by eigenfunction expansion. For \(\lambda\) not an eigenvalue,

\(\varphi(x)=f(x)+\lambda\sum_n\frac{(f,\varphi_n)}{\lambda_n-\lambda}\,\varphi_n(x),\qquad(f,\varphi_n)=\int f\varphi_n\,dx .\)

  1. Resolvent expansion. \(R(x,t;\lambda)=\displaystyle\sum_n\frac{\varphi_n(x)\varphi_n(t)}{\lambda_n-\lambda}\), and \(K(x,t)=\sum_n\varphi_n(x)\varphi_n(t)/\lambda_n\).
  2. At an eigenvalue \(\lambda=\lambda_m\) the equation is solvable iff \((f,\varphi)=0\) for all eigenfunctions \(\varphi\) of \(\lambda_m\) (Fredholm alternative).

For the Dirichlet Green’s function \(\mathcal G=x_<(1-x_>)\): \(\lambda_n=n^2\pi^2\), \(\varphi_n=\sqrt2\sin n\pi x\). Checks: \(\sum1/(n\pi)^4=1/90=\iint\mathcal G^2\) and \(\sum1/(n\pi)^2=\frac16=\int\mathcal G(x,x)\,dx\).

Volterra equations by differentiation, and via the Laplace transform

Reduction to an ODE. Differentiate a second-kind Volterra equation with a separable or polynomial-in-\((x-t)\) kernel until the integral disappears (Leibniz rule). Initial conditions come from setting \(x=0\).

Convolution kernel and the Laplace transform. If \(K(x,t)=k(x-t)\), the integral is a convolution \(k*\varphi\) and \(\mathscr L\{k*\varphi\}=\mathscr L\{k\}\,\mathscr L\{\varphi\}=\kappa(s)\,\Phi(s)\). Write \(F=\mathscr L\{f\}\).

  • Second kind: \(\varphi=f+\lambda\,k*\varphi\) gives \(\Phi(s)=\frac{F(s)}{1-\lambda\kappa(s)},\qquad\varphi=\mathscr L^{-1}\{\Phi\}.\)
  • First kind: \(f=k*\varphi\) gives \(\Phi(s)=F(s)/\kappa(s)\) (the inverse exists only if \(F/\kappa\) is a Laplace transform).
  • Systems of Volterra equations and integro-differential equations: same idea, \(\mathscr L\{\varphi'\}=s\Phi-\varphi(0)\).

The resolvent for a convolution kernel is itself a convolution: \(\mathscr L\{R\}=\dfrac{\kappa(s)}{1-\lambda\kappa(s)}\).

Kernel not of convolution type (2026 Q89). The Laplace transform of \(\int_0^xK(x,t)\varphi(t)\,dt\) does not factor. Options: differentiate to an ODE (if the kernel is separable), or change variables so that the kernel becomes a function of a difference. For a Mellin-type kernel \(K(x,t)=h(x/t)\) the substitution \(x=e^u\), \(t=e^v\) turns it into a function of \(u-v\). The key for 2026 Q89 chooses the variable-transformation option.

Abel’s equation is itself a convolution equation: \(\varphi*x^{-\alpha}=f\) and \(\mathscr L\{x^{-\alpha}\}=\Gamma(1-\alpha)s^{\alpha-1}\).

Equations of the first kind (Fredholm)

\(f(x)=\lambda\int_a^bK(x,t)\varphi(t)\,dt\) is more delicate. For a separable kernel the right side is a combination of the \(a_i(x)\), so a solution exists only if \(f\in\operatorname{span}\{a_i\}\), and then it is not unique: add any function orthogonal to all \(b_i\). In general first-kind equations are ill-posed.

Worked examples

Example 1: separable kernel of rank 2

Solve \(\varphi(x)=1+\lambda\int_0^1(x+t)\varphi(t)\,dt\).

Write \(K=x\cdot1+1\cdot t\). Put \(c_1=\int_0^1\varphi\,dt\) and \(c_2=\int_0^1t\varphi\,dt\). Then \(\varphi=1+\lambda(c_1x+c_2)\). Integrate \(\varphi\) and \(t\varphi\):

\(c_1=1+\lambda\Big(\frac{c_1}2+c_2\Big),\qquad c_2=\frac12+\lambda\Big(\frac{c_1}3+\frac{c_2}2\Big).\)

So \(\Big(1-\frac\lambda2\Big)c_1-\lambda c_2=1\) and \(-\frac\lambda3c_1+\Big(1-\frac\lambda2\Big)c_2=\frac12\). The determinant is

\(D(\lambda)=\Big(1-\frac\lambda2\Big)^2-\frac{\lambda^2}3=1-\lambda-\frac{\lambda^2}{12}.\)

Cramer’s rule: \(c_1=1/D\), \(c_2=\big(\tfrac12+\tfrac\lambda{12}\big)/D\). Hence

\(\boxed{\varphi(x)=1+\frac{\lambda\big(x+\tfrac12+\tfrac\lambda{12}\big)}{1-\lambda-\lambda^2/12}}\qquad(\lambda\ne-6\pm4\sqrt3).\)

The eigenvalues are the roots of \(\lambda^2+12\lambda-12=0\), namely \(\lambda=-6\pm4\sqrt3\). Numerical check at \(\lambda=\frac12\): \(\varphi(0.2)=1.77391\), and the integral equation gives the same value.

Example 2: eigenvalues and eigenfunctions

Find them for \(\varphi(x)=\lambda\int_0^{2\pi}\sin(x+t)\varphi(t)\,dt\).

\(\sin(x+t)=\sin x\cos t+\cos x\sin t\), so \(\varphi=\lambda(c_1\sin x+c_2\cos x)\) with \(c_1=\int\varphi\cos t\), \(c_2=\int\varphi\sin t\). Substituting: \(c_1=\lambda\pi c_2\) and \(c_2=\lambda\pi c_1\). Non-trivial solutions require \(1-\lambda^2\pi^2=0\), so \(\lambda=\pm\frac1\pi\).

  • \(\lambda=\frac1\pi\): \(c_1=c_2\), \(\varphi=\sin x+\cos x\).
  • \(\lambda=-\frac1\pi\): \(c_1=-c_2\), \(\varphi=\sin x-\cos x\).

These are orthogonal on \([0,2\pi]\): \(\int(\sin^2x-\cos^2x)\,dx=0\). ✓ (symmetric kernel, distinct eigenvalues). Two eigenvalues for a rank-2 kernel.

Example 3: Fredholm alternative

For \(K=xt\) on \([0,1]\): for which \(f\) does \(\varphi=f+3\int_0^1xt\varphi(t)\,dt\) have a solution?

Put \(c=\int t\varphi\,dt\). Then \(\varphi=f+3cx\). Multiply by \(t\) and integrate: \(c=\int tf\,dt+3c\int t^2dt=\int tf\,dt+c\). So we need \(\int_0^1tf(t)\,dt=0\). Here \(\lambda=3\) is the eigenvalue (the homogeneous equation \(\varphi=3\int xt\varphi\) has solution \(\varphi=x\)), and the condition is \(f\perp x\).

If it holds, \(c\) is free and \(\varphi=f+3cx\). Example: \(f=3x-2\) has \(\int t(3t-2)dt=0\), and \(\varphi=3x-2+3cx\) solves the equation for every \(c\) (checked numerically). For \(f=x\), \(\int t\cdot t\,dt=\frac13\ne0\): no solution.

Example 4: resolvent and Neumann series (Volterra)

Solve \(\varphi(x)=1+\lambda\int_0^x(x-t)\varphi(t)\,dt\) using iterated kernels.

\(K_1=x-t\), \(K_2=\int_t^x(x-s)(s-t)\,ds=\frac{(x-t)^3}{3!}\), and \(K_n=\frac{(x-t)^{2n-1}}{(2n-1)!}\). So \(R(x,t;\lambda)=\sum_{n\ge1}\lambda^{n-1}\frac{(x-t)^{2n-1}}{(2n-1)!}=\frac{\sinh\sqrt\lambda(x-t)}{\sqrt\lambda}\). Then

\(\varphi=1+\lambda\int_0^x\frac{\sinh\sqrt\lambda(x-t)}{\sqrt\lambda}\,dt=1+\big(\cosh\sqrt\lambda\,x-1\big)=\cosh\sqrt\lambda\,x .\)

(Check: \(\varphi''=\lambda\varphi\), \(\varphi(0)=1\), \(\varphi'(0)=0\). ✓)

Example 5: successive approximations

Solve \(\varphi(x)=x-\int_0^x(x-t)\varphi(t)\,dt\) by Picard iteration with \(\varphi_0=x\).

\(\varphi_1=x-\int_0^x(x-t)t\,dt=x-\frac{x^3}6\). \(\varphi_2=x-\int_0^x(x-t)\Big(t-\frac{t^3}6\Big)dt=x-\frac{x^3}{6}+\frac{x^5}{120}\). The pattern is the partial sum of \(\sin x\), which is the exact solution (\(\varphi''=-\varphi\), \(\varphi(0)=0\), \(\varphi'(0)=1\)).

Example 6: Volterra by Laplace transform

(a) Solve \(\varphi(t)=t+\int_0^t(t-\tau)\varphi(\tau)\,d\tau\). Here \(k(t)=t\), \(\kappa=1/s^2\), \(F=1/s^2\). \(\Phi=\dfrac{1/s^2}{1-1/s^2}=\dfrac1{s^2-1}\), so \(\varphi=\sinh t\). (Check: \(t+(\sinh t-t)=\sinh t\). ✓)

(b) Solve \(\sin t=\int_0^te^{t-\tau}\varphi(\tau)\,d\tau\) (first kind). \(F=\frac1{s^2+1}\), \(\kappa=\frac1{s-1}\). \(\Phi=\frac{s-1}{s^2+1}\), so \(\varphi=\cos t-\sin t\). (Check: \(e^t\int_0^te^{-\tau}(\cos\tau-\sin\tau)d\tau=e^t\cdot e^{-t}\sin t=\sin t\). ✓)

Example 7: Hilbert-Schmidt expansion

Solve \(\varphi(x)=\sin\pi x+\lambda\int_0^1\mathcal G(x,t)\varphi(t)\,dt\), \(\mathcal G=x_<(1-x_>)\), \(\lambda\ne n^2\pi^2\).

The eigenfunction \(\sin\pi x\) with \(\lambda_1=\pi^2\) is exactly \(f\). So \(\varphi=c\sin\pi x\) with \(c=1+\lambda c/\pi^2\), giving \(c=\dfrac{\pi^2}{\pi^2-\lambda}\). The expansion formula agrees: only the \(n=1\) term survives.

Common traps

  • \(c_i\) definition. \(c_i=\int b_i\varphi\) uses the \(t\)-part of the kernel. Using the \(x\)-part (\(a_i\)) is the commonest error. Also \(A_{ki}=\int b_ka_i\) (not \(\int a_ka_i\)).
  • Eigenvalue vs reciprocal. The equation \(\det(I-\lambda A)=0\) gives \(\lambda=1/\mu\) where \(\mu\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\). If an option lists \(\mu\), it is the wrong one.
  • Fredholm alternative direction. If \(\lambda\) is not an eigenvalue the solution exists for every \(f\) and is unique. If \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue and \(f\not\perp\) the adjoint solutions, no solution; if \(f\perp\), infinitely many. “Non-trivial solution of the inhomogeneous equation when \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue” is not automatic.
  • Orthogonality holds for different eigenvalues of a symmetric kernel (with weight \(1\)). Non-symmetric kernels need the adjoint equation. Eigenfunctions of one repeated eigenvalue need not be orthogonal until chosen so.
  • Rank counts eigenvalues. A rank-2 kernel has at most 2 non-zero eigenvalues (counting multiplicity); a rank-2 symmetric kernel has exactly 2 (2026 Q32). It has infinitely many only if rank is infinite.
  • Volterra has no eigenvalues. The Neumann series converges for all \(\lambda\); do not look for characteristic values.
  • Neumann series radius. \(\lvert\lambda\rvert<1/(M(b-a))\) is sufficient. Failing it does not mean the equation has no solution.
  • Laplace on Volterra needs a convolution kernel \(k(x-t)\). \(K=xt\) or \(K=x/t\) is not one (2026 Q89).
  • First-kind Volterra needs \(f(0)=0\); a Laplace solution may not exist as a function if \(F/\kappa\) does not decay.
  • Do not confuse the Fredholm determinant \(D(\lambda)=\det(I-\lambda A)\) with \(\det A\).

Previous-year questions

2025 Q76 (official key A)

Question. The solution of the Fredholm integral equation \(\varphi(x)=1+\lambda\int_0^1xt\,\varphi(t)\,dt\) is (the options were printed in a form that does not survive the scan).

Answer. A, which is \(\varphi(x)=1+\dfrac{3\lambda}{2(3-\lambda)}\,x\).

Solution. Put \(c=\int_0^1t\varphi\,dt\). Then \(\varphi=1+\lambda cx\) and \(c=\frac12+\frac{\lambda c}{3}\), so \(c=\dfrac{3}{2(3-\lambda)}\). Hence \(\varphi=1+\dfrac{3\lambda x}{2(3-\lambda)}\), valid for \(\lambda\ne3\).

2026 Q32 (worked key D, unofficial)

Question (restated). For a symmetric degenerate kernel of rank 2, what is the maximum number of non-zero eigenvalues, and what is true of the eigenfunctions of distinct eigenvalues?

Answer. D: at most 2 eigenvalues, and the eigenfunctions are orthogonal.

Solution. A degenerate kernel \(\sum_{i=1}^2a_i(x)b_i(t)\) reduces to a \(2\times2\) system, whose determinant is a polynomial of degree \(\le2\): at most 2 eigenvalues. For a symmetric kernel, eigenfunctions of distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

2026 Q39 (worked key D, unofficial)

Question (restated). For \(y(x)=1+\lambda\int_0^1xt\,y(t)\,dt\), find the non-zero value of \(\lambda\) at which the consistency condition fails (the characteristic value).

Answer. D, \(\lambda=3\).

Solution. With \(c=\int ty\,dt\), \(y=1+\lambda cx\) and \(c=\frac12+\frac{\lambda c}{3}\), so \(c\big(1-\frac\lambda3\big)=\frac12\). This has no solution when \(\lambda=3\) (left side zero, right side \(\frac12\)). So \(\lambda=3\) is the characteristic value and the equation is unsolvable there.

2026 Q82 (worked key B, unofficial; the key says wording is vague)

Question (restated). A degenerate kernel leads to a singular linear system \(A\mathbf c=\mathbf b\). What is the condition for the integral equation to have a solution?

Answer. B: the rank condition, \(\operatorname{rank}A=\operatorname{rank}[A\mid\mathbf b]\) (consistency).

Solution. When the system matrix \(I-\lambda A\) is singular (\(D(\lambda)=0\)), a solution exists iff the augmented matrix has the same rank, equivalently \(f\) is orthogonal to the adjoint homogeneous solutions (Fredholm alternative). The solution is then not unique.

2026 Q89 (worked key B, unofficial; the key marks low confidence)

Question (restated). A Volterra equation has a kernel that is not of convolution type. How can the Laplace transform be applied?

Answer. B: after a suitable change of variable that turns the kernel into convolution form.

Solution. The Laplace transform turns a convolution into a product only for \(k(x-t)\). For a kernel like \(h(x/t)\) the substitution \(x=e^u\), \(t=e^v\) gives a convolution in \(u,v\). Direct transformation, ignoring the kernel, or converting to a PDE are not valid methods. The key rates this as a best guess because the question is poorly posed.

2026 Q97 (worked key D, unofficial)

Question (restated). \(y(x)=\lambda\int G(x,t)\,y(t)\,dt\). What is the condition on \(\lambda\) for a non-trivial solution?

Answer. D: the Fredholm determinant \(D(\lambda)=0\), i.e. \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue.

Solution. The homogeneous equation \(y=\lambda Ty\) has a non-trivial solution iff \(I-\lambda T\) is not invertible, iff \(D(\lambda)=\det(I-\lambda T)=0\). The question text is OCR-uncertain in the key, but the answer is the standard theorem.

Quick revision

  • Separable kernel: set \(c_i=\int b_i\varphi\), get \((I-\lambda A)\mathbf c=\mathbf f\) with \(A_{ki}=\int b_ka_i\).
  • Eigenvalues of a separable kernel: roots of \(\det(I-\lambda A)=0\); eigenfunction \(\sum c_ia_i\).
  • Rank-\(n\) kernel: at most \(n\) eigenvalues; symmetric rank \(n\): exactly \(n\).
  • Fredholm alternative: \(\lambda\) not an eigenvalue: unique solution for every \(f\).
  • \(\lambda\) an eigenvalue: solvable iff \(f\perp\) adjoint eigenfunctions; then infinitely many solutions.
  • Singular system solvable iff \(\operatorname{rank}A=\operatorname{rank}[A\mid b]\).
  • Neumann series: \(\varphi=f+\sum\lambda^n\int K_nf\); resolvent \(R=\sum\lambda^nK_{n+1}\).
  • Volterra 2nd kind: unique solution for all \(\lambda\); no eigenvalues.
  • Convolution Volterra: \(\Phi=F/(1-\lambda\kappa)\) (2nd kind), \(\Phi=F/\kappa\) (1st kind).
  • Symmetric kernels: real eigenvalues; orthogonal eigenfunctions; Hilbert-Schmidt expansion.
  • \(\varphi=\lambda T\varphi\) non-trivial iff \(D(\lambda)=0\).
  • \(K=xt\) on \([0,1]\): \(\lambda=3\). \(\varphi=1+\lambda\int xt\varphi\) gives \(\varphi=1+\frac{3\lambda x}{2(3-\lambda)}\).
