Why it matters for the exam

Calculus of variations has appeared in every paper we have: one question in 2019, two in 2025 (Q74, Q78) and one in 2026 (Q76). The usual form is “find the extremal of this functional with these end conditions”, plus one conceptual question on the Euler-Lagrange equation (its order and linearity). Isoperimetric problems (Lagrange multiplier) came in 2026. The same ideas return in Unit 8 (Hamilton’s principle, Lagrange’s equations).

Core ideas

Functionals and the basic problem

A functional is a map from a class of functions to the real numbers. The standard one is

\(J[y]=\int_a^b F(x,y,y')\,dx .\)

The basic problem: among all \(y\in C^1[a,b]\) with \(y(a)=y_a\), \(y(b)=y_b\), find the \(y\) that makes \(J\) a maximum or minimum.

A linear functional \(L\) satisfies \(L[\alpha y+\beta z]=\alpha L[y]+\beta L[z]\). Examples: \(y\mapsto y(0)\), \(y\mapsto \int_a^b y\,dx\). A linear functional is continuous iff it is bounded: \(\lvert L[y]\rvert\le M\lVert y\rVert\). The \(J\) above is not linear in general, but its first variation (the part linear in the increment) is.

Increment and variation. For an admissible \(y\) and a function \(\eta\) with \(\eta(a)=\eta(b)=0\):

\(\Delta J=J[y+\varepsilon\eta]-J[y]=\varepsilon\,\delta J+\tfrac12\varepsilon^2\,\delta^2J+\cdots,\qquad \delta J=\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}J[y+\varepsilon\eta]\Big\vert_{\varepsilon=0}.\)

\(\delta y=\varepsilon\eta\) is the variation of \(y\). A functional has an extremum at \(y\) only if \(\delta J=0\) for every admissible \(\eta\) (necessary condition). Such a \(y\) is called an extremal (a stationary function).

Weak and strong extrema. \(y_0\) is a weak minimum if \(J[y]\ge J[y_0]\) for all \(y\) close to \(y_0\) in both \(y\) and \(y'\) (norm \(\max\lvert y\rvert+\max\lvert y'\rvert\)). It is a strong minimum if only closeness in \(y\) is required. Every strong extremum is weak, not conversely. The Euler-Lagrange equation and Legendre’s condition are for weak extrema.

Fundamental lemma

If \(g\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(\int_a^b g(x)\eta(x)\,dx=0\) for every \(\eta\in C^1\) with \(\eta(a)=\eta(b)=0\), then \(g\equiv 0\).

The Euler-Lagrange equation

Differentiate under the integral and integrate \(F_{y'}\eta'\) by parts:

\(\delta J=\int_a^b\Big(F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}\Big)\eta\,dx+\Big[F_{y'}\eta\Big]_a^b .\)

The boundary term vanishes because \(\eta(a)=\eta(b)=0\). By the fundamental lemma:

\(\boxed{\ \frac{\partial F}{\partial y}-\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\Big)=0\ }\)

(equivalently \(\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}-F_y=0\), as printed in the 2025 paper). Expanding the total derivative:

\(F_{y'y'}\,y''+F_{y'y}\,y'+F_{y'x}-F_y=0 .\)

This is a second-order ordinary differential equation. It is non-linear in general (it is linear only for special \(F\), for example \(F\) quadratic in \(y,y'\)). When \(F_{y'y'}=0\) the equation drops to first order or becomes algebraic. The two integration constants are fixed by \(y(a)=y_a\), \(y(b)=y_b\).

Special cases (use these first)

Form of \(F\) Consequence
\(F\) free of \(y\) \(F_y=0\), so \(F_{y'}=C\) (first integral)
\(F\) free of \(x\) \(F-y'F_{y'}=C\) (Beltrami identity)
\(F\) free of \(y'\) \(F_y=0\), an algebraic equation, no differential equation
\(F=M(x,y)+N(x,y)\,y'\) E-L becomes \(M_y=N_x\): no differential equation. Either an identity (the integral is path independent, any curve is extremal) or it defines a curve
\(F=f(x,y)\sqrt{1+y'^2}\) arc-length type problem, weighted length

Why Beltrami works. If \(F=F(y,y')\), then \(\frac{d}{dx}\big(F-y'F_{y'}\big)=y'\big(F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}\big)\), which is zero on an extremal. So \(F-y'F_{y'}\) is constant. It is a first integral and saves one differentiation.

Several unknown functions and higher derivatives

Several functions. For \(J=\int F(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,y_1',\dots,y_n')\,dx\), vary each \(y_i\) separately. One E-L equation per function:

\(F_{y_i}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y_i'}=0,\qquad i=1,\dots,n .\)

Higher derivatives (Euler-Poisson equation). For \(J=\int F(x,y,y',y'',\dots,y^{(n)})\,dx\):

\(F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}+\frac{d^2}{dx^2}F_{y''}-\cdots+(-1)^n\frac{d^n}{dx^n}F_{y^{(n)}}=0 .\)

It has order \(2n\). You need \(2n\) end conditions (for \(n=2\): \(y\) and \(y'\) at both ends).

Several independent variables. For \(J=\iint F(x,y,u,u_x,u_y)\,dx\,dy\) (Ostrogradsky):

\(F_u-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}F_{u_x}-\frac{\partial}{\partial y}F_{u_y}=0 .\)

Classical problems

Shortest distance in the plane. \(F=\sqrt{1+y'^2}\) is free of \(y\), so \(y'/\sqrt{1+y'^2}=C\), hence \(y'\) is constant: straight lines. On a sphere the extremals of arc length (geodesics) are great circles; on a cylinder, helices.

Brachistochrone. A bead slides from rest under gravity from \((0,0)\) to \((x_1,y_1)\) (\(y\) measured downward). Speed \(v=\sqrt{2gy}\), so

\(T=\int_0^{x_1}\sqrt{\frac{1+y'^2}{2gy}}\,dx .\)

\(F\) is free of \(x\), so Beltrami gives \(y(1+y'^2)=c\). Put \(y'=\cot(\theta/2)\) to get the cycloid

\(x=\frac c2(\theta-\sin\theta),\qquad y=\frac c2(1-\cos\theta).\)

The cycloid is the curve of quickest descent (not the straight line).

Minimal surface of revolution. Rotating \(y(x)\) about the \(x\)-axis gives area \(A=2\pi\int y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx\). \(F\) is free of \(x\): \(F-y'F_{y'}=\dfrac{y}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}=c\). Solution: the catenary

\(y=c\cosh\frac{x-x_0}{c},\)

and the surface is a catenoid (soap film between two rings).

Hyperbolic-type length. \(F=\sqrt{1+y'^2}/x\) is free of \(y\): \(\dfrac{y'}{x\sqrt{1+y'^2}}=C\), which integrates to circles with centre on the \(y\)-axis: \(x^2+(y-k)^2=R^2\). This is the 2025 Q74 pattern. It is the geodesic problem for metric \(ds=\sqrt{dx^2+dy^2}/x\).

Fermat’s principle. Light follows the path minimising \(\int n(x,y)\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx\).

Isoperimetric problems (constraint by an integral)

Extremise \(J=\int_a^b F\,dx\) subject to \(K=\int_a^b G\,dx=\ell\) (fixed) and fixed end values.

Rule (Lagrange multiplier). Form \(H=F+\lambda G\) and write the E-L equation for \(H\):

\(H_y-\frac{d}{dx}H_{y'}=0 .\)

The general solution has constants \(c_1,c_2\) and the multiplier \(\lambda\). Use the two end conditions and the constraint \(K=\ell\) to find all three. If \(G\)’s own E-L equation is not satisfied by the extremal, the multiplier rule applies with the form above.

Classical examples.

  • Dido’s problem: closed curve of given length enclosing maximum area is a circle.
  • Hanging chain of given length: minimise \(\int y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\) subject to \(\int\sqrt{1+y'^2}=L\). Solution is a catenary.

Several constraints. Use \(H=F+\lambda_1G_1+\lambda_2G_2\).

Finite (holonomic) constraint \(g(x,y,z)=0\) (for example, a geodesic on a surface): \(H=F+\lambda(x)\,g\), with \(\lambda\) a function of \(x\).

Duality. A curve that maximises area for a given length also minimises length for that area.

Natural and variable end-point conditions

Free end. If \(y(b)\) is not prescribed, the boundary term \([F_{y'}\eta]_a^b\) must vanish for \(\eta(b)\ne0\):

\(F_{y'}\Big\vert_{x=b}=0\qquad(\text{natural boundary condition}).\)

Similarly, \(F_{y'}\vert_{x=a}=0\) if \(y(a)\) is free. Natural conditions apply after the E-L equation is solved: E-L (2 constants) plus one prescribed end plus one natural condition.

Free end with a boundary term. For \(J=\int_a^bF\,dx+\varphi(y(b))\), the condition at \(b\) is \(F_{y'}+\varphi'(y(b))=0\).

End point on a given curve. If the right end \((x_1,y_1)\) may slide on \(y=\varphi(x)\) (both \(x_1,y_1\) unknown), the transversality condition is

\(\Big[F+(\varphi'-y')F_{y'}\Big]_{x=x_1}=0,\)

plus \(y(x_1)=\varphi(x_1)\). If the end moves on a vertical line (\(x_1\) fixed, \(y_1\) free) this reduces to \(F_{y'}=0\). For \(F=f(x,y)\sqrt{1+y'^2}\) transversality is orthogonality: the extremal meets the boundary curve at a right angle.

Higher-order functionals (\(F\) contains \(y''\)): a free end with \(y'\) unspecified gives \(F_{y''}=0\); a free end with \(y\) unspecified gives \(F_{y'}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y''}=0\) at that end.

Sufficient conditions (basic level)

\(\delta J=0\) is only necessary. Look at the second variation

\(\delta^2J=\int_a^b\big(P\eta'^2+Q\eta^2\big)\,dx,\qquad P=F_{y'y'},\quad Q=F_{yy}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{yy'} .\)

  • Legendre condition (necessary). For a minimum, \(F_{y'y'}\ge0\) along the extremal; for a maximum, \(F_{y'y'}\le0\).
  • Strengthened Legendre condition. \(F_{y'y'}>0\) along the extremal.
  • Jacobi condition. The solution of the Jacobi equation \(\frac{d}{dx}(P u')-Qu=0\) with \(u(a)=0\), \(u'(a)=1\) must not vanish again on \((a,b]\) (no conjugate point).

Sufficient for a weak minimum: the extremal satisfies the E-L equation, the strengthened Legendre condition and the Jacobi condition.

Convexity shortcut. If \(F(x,y,y')\) is convex in \((y,y')\) for each \(x\) (that is, \(F_{y'y'}\ge0\), \(F_{yy}\ge0\), \(F_{yy}F_{y'y'}-F_{yy'}^2\ge0\)), every extremal satisfying the end conditions gives the global minimum. Example: \(\int(y'^2+y^2)\,dx\).

Weierstrass condition (for strong extrema): the excess function \(E(x,y,y',p)=F(x,y,p)-F(x,y,y')-(p-y')F_{y'}(x,y,y')\ge0\) for all \(p\). You only need to know its name and purpose.

Worked examples

Example 1: Euler-Lagrange with fixed ends

Find the extremal of \(J=\int_0^{\pi/2}(y'^2-y^2)\,dx\), \(y(0)=0\), \(y(\pi/2)=1\).

\(F=y'^2-y^2\): \(F_y=-2y\), \(F_{y'}=2y'\). E-L: \(-2y-2y''=0\), so \(y''+y=0\), \(y=A\cos x+B\sin x\). \(y(0)=0\Rightarrow A=0\); \(y(\pi/2)=1\Rightarrow B=1\). Extremal: \(y=\sin x\), with \(J=\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos2x\,dx=0\).

Check: \(J[y+\varepsilon\sin2x]-J[y]=\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\varepsilon^2>0\) for both \(\varepsilon=\pm0.1\) (numerically \(0.02356\) for each sign), consistent with a local minimum. Note \(F_{y'y'}=2>0\), and the interval length \(\pi/2<\pi\) is before the first conjugate point.

Example 2: Beltrami (F free of x)

Find the curve through \((-1,\cosh 1)\) and \((1,\cosh 1)\) minimising the area of revolution \(\int y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx\).

Beltrami: \(\dfrac{y}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}=c\). Solving \(y'=\sqrt{y^2/c^2-1}\) gives \(y=c\cosh\frac{x-x_0}{c}\). Symmetry gives \(x_0=0\). Then \(y(1)=c\cosh(1/c)=\cosh1\) is satisfied by \(c=1\). The extremal is \(y=\cosh x\).

(The equation \(c\cosh(1/c)=\cosh1\) also has a second root \(c\approx0.70\). Both catenaries pass through the end points; \(c=1\) gives the smaller area, \(2.8134\) against \(2.8224\). For end points far apart relative to their height there may be no catenary at all.)

Example 3: Isoperimetric problem

Extremise \(\int_0^1y'^2\,dx\) subject to \(\int_0^1y\,dx=1\) and \(y(0)=y(1)=0\).

\(H=y'^2+\lambda y\). E-L: \(\lambda-2y''=0\), so \(y=\frac\lambda4x^2+c_1x+c_2\). \(y(0)=0\Rightarrow c_2=0\); \(y(1)=0\Rightarrow c_1=-\lambda/4\). So \(y=\frac\lambda4(x^2-x)\) and \(\int_0^1y\,dx=-\frac\lambda{24}=1\), \(\lambda=-24\). Extremal: \(y=6x(1-x)\).

Check: \(\int_0^16x(1-x)dx=1\) and \(J=12\). Another admissible curve, \(y=\frac\pi2\sin\pi x\), has \(\int y=1\) and \(J\approx12.18>12\).

Example 4: Natural boundary condition

Minimise \(J=\int_0^1(y'^2+4y)\,dx\) with \(y(0)=0\) and \(y(1)\) free.

E-L: \(4-2y''=0\), so \(y=x^2+c_1x+c_2\); \(y(0)=0\Rightarrow c_2=0\). Natural condition at \(x=1\): \(F_{y'}=2y'(1)=0\), so \(2+c_1=0\), \(c_1=-2\). Extremal: \(y=x^2-2x\), \(J=-4/3\).

Check: adding the perturbation \(\varepsilon x\) (which moves the free end) gives \(J=-1.3233\) for \(\varepsilon=\pm0.1\), larger than \(-1.3333\).

Example 5: Euler-Poisson

Extremise \(J=\int_0^1(1+y''^2)\,dx\) with \(y(0)=y'(0)=0\), \(y(1)=1\), \(y'(1)=0\).

\(F=1+y''^2\): Euler-Poisson: \(\frac{d^2}{dx^2}(2y'')=0\), so \(y''''=0\) and \(y=a+bx+cx^2+dx^3\). \(y(0)=0\Rightarrow a=0\); \(y'(0)=0\Rightarrow b=0\); \(y(1)=1\Rightarrow c+d=1\); \(y'(1)=0\Rightarrow2c+3d=0\). So \(d=-2\), \(c=3\): \(y=3x^2-2x^3\). \(J=\int_0^1\big(1+(6-12x)^2\big)dx=13\).

Example 6: Two unknown functions

Extremise \(\int_0^{\pi/2}(y'^2+z'^2+2yz)\,dx\) with \(y(0)=0\), \(y(\pi/2)=1\), \(z(0)=0\), \(z(\pi/2)=-1\).

E-L: \(y''=z\), \(z''=y\). Add and subtract: \(u=y+z\) gives \(u''=u\) and \(v=y-z\) gives \(v''=-v\). End values: \(u(0)=u(\pi/2)=0\) forces \(u\equiv0\). \(v(0)=0\), \(v(\pi/2)=2\) gives \(v=2\sin x\). So \(y=\sin x\), \(z=-\sin x\).

Common traps

  • Sign in E-L. The equation is \(F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}=0\). Writing \(F_y+\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}\) gives wrong extremals. Check on \(F=y'^2+y^2\): the extremal equation is \(y''=y\), not \(y''=-y\).
  • Order and linearity (2025 Q78). The E-L equation is second order and generally non-linear. First order is wrong. “Second order linear” is true only for special \(F\).
  • Beltrami needs \(F\) free of \(x\). It does not apply when \(x\) appears explicitly. Also, for \(F\) free of \(y\) the first integral is \(F_{y'}=C\), not \(F-y'F_{y'}=C\).
  • Brachistochrone is a cycloid, not a straight line or circle. Geodesics on the plane are lines, on the sphere great circles.
  • Natural conditions only at free ends. Do not apply \(F_{y'}=0\) at an end where \(y\) is prescribed.
  • Isoperimetric constraint. The multiplier is part of the unknowns. Forgetting to impose the constraint \(\int G=\ell\) leaves one constant undetermined.
  • Stationary is not minimal. \(\delta J=0\) does not tell max, min or neither. \(F_{y'y'}\ge0\) is necessary for a min, not sufficient.
  • Euler-Poisson signs. The signs alternate: \(F_y-\frac d{dx}F_{y'}+\frac{d^2}{dx^2}F_{y''}\). A second derivative term has a plus sign.
  • Functional vs function. \(J[y]\) depends on the whole curve; “\(\delta J=0\)” is the first-order condition on the curve, not \(dJ/dx\).

Previous-year questions

2019 (unnumbered, Q-14 in the recovered list)

Question. The extremal of \(\int_0^1\big[(y')^2+12xy\big]dx\) with \(y(0)=0\), \(y(1)=1\) is: \(y=x^4\), \(x^3\), \(x^2\) or \(x\).

Answer. \(y=x^3\) (the recovered video shows the working reaching \(y''=6x\); the final tick was not visible, but the calculation is unambiguous).

Solution. \(F=y'^2+12xy\): \(F_y=12x\), \(F_{y'}=2y'\). E-L: \(12x-2y''=0\), so \(y''=6x\), \(y=x^3+c_1x+c_2\). \(y(0)=0\Rightarrow c_2=0\); \(y(1)=1\Rightarrow c_1=0\).

2025 Q74 (official key C)

Question. The extremal of the functional \(\int_1^2\dfrac{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}{x}\,dx\) subject to \(y(1)=0\), \(y(2)=1\) is: (A) \(x^2+y^2=5\), (B) \((x-2)^2+y^2=5\), (C) \(x^2+(y-2)^2=5\), (D) \((x-1)^2+(y-2)^2=5\).

Answer. C.

Solution. \(F\) is free of \(y\), so \(\dfrac{y'}{x\sqrt{1+y'^2}}=c\). This integrates to circles \(x^2+(y-k)^2=R^2\). Through \((1,0)\): \(1+k^2=R^2\). Through \((2,1)\): \(4+(1-k)^2=R^2\). Subtract: \(k=2\), \(R^2=5\). (Fast method: test the options on the two points; only C passes both.)

2025 Q78 (official key C)

Question. The Euler-Lagrange equation \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\Big(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y'}\Big)-\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y}=0\) is a: (A) first order linear differential equation, (B) second order linear differential equation, (C) second order non-linear differential equation, (D) third order non-linear differential equation.

Answer. C.

Solution. Expanded, it is \(F_{y'y'}y''+F_{y'y}y'+F_{y'x}-F_y=0\): second order. The coefficients depend on \(y\) and \(y'\) through \(F\), so in general it is non-linear. (The key’s own note: B is true only for special \(F\).)

2026 Q76 (worked key C, unofficial)

Question. The extremal of \(\int(y'^2+y^2)\,dx\) subject to the constraint \(\int y\,dx=1\) (via the Lagrange multiplier) has which form? The options are not recoverable from the photo.

Answer. C in the worked key, which takes the extremal to be a constant.

Solution. \(H=y'^2+y^2+\lambda y\): E-L gives \(y''-y=\lambda/2\), so \(y=-\frac\lambda2+c_1e^x+c_2e^{-x}\) (hyperbolic form). No end conditions are printed; with natural conditions \(y'=0\) at both ends, \(c_1=c_2=0\), \(y\) is constant, and \(\int_0^1y\,dx=1\) gives \(y=1\). Quick revision

  • Basic problem: \(J=\int F(x,y,y')dx\); extremal means \(\delta J=0\).
  • E-L: \(F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}=0\). Second order, in general non-linear.
  • \(F\) free of \(y\): \(F_{y'}=C\). \(F\) free of \(x\): \(F-y'F_{y'}=C\).
  • \(F\) free of \(y'\): just the algebraic equation \(F_y=0\).
  • Higher derivatives: \(F_y-(F_{y'})'+(F_{y''})''=0\) (Euler-Poisson), order \(2n\).
  • Several functions: one E-L equation per function.
  • Isoperimetric: E-L for \(F+\lambda G\), then fix \(\lambda\) with the constraint.
  • Free end: \(F_{y'}=0\) there. End on \(y=\varphi(x)\): \(F+(\varphi'-y')F_{y'}=0\).
  • Brachistochrone: cycloid. Plane geodesic: line. Minimal surface of revolution: catenary.
  • Legendre: min needs \(F_{y'y'}\ge0\); Jacobi: no conjugate point. Convex \(F\) gives global min.
  • Linear functional: \(L[\alpha y+\beta z]=\alpha L[y]+\beta L[z]\).
