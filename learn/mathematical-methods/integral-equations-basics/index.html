Why it matters for the exam

Integral equations are the most asked part of Unit 7: one question in 2025 (Q76) and six in 2026 (Q32, Q39, Q45, Q82, Q89, Q97). Most need the vocabulary on this page (Fredholm or Volterra, kinds, kernels, eigenvalues) and the conversion between differential and integral equations. 2026 Q45 used the Green’s function to turn a boundary value problem into a Fredholm equation.

Core ideas

Linear integral equations: the general form

An integral equation has the unknown function \(\varphi\) under an integral sign. The general linear equation is

\(h(x)\,\varphi(x)=f(x)+\lambda\int_a^{b(x)}K(x,t)\,\varphi(t)\,dt .\)

Here \(K(x,t)\) is the kernel, \(f\) is the free (forcing) term, and \(\lambda\) is a parameter.

Feature Types
Limits Fredholm: both limits constant, \(\int_a^b\). Volterra: upper limit variable, \(\int_a^x\)
Position of \(\varphi\) First kind: \(\varphi\) only under the integral (\(h=0\)). Second kind: \(h=1\), \(\varphi\) also outside. Third kind: \(h(x)\) a general function
Free term Homogeneous if \(f\equiv0\), otherwise inhomogeneous

So the four standard equations are:

  • Fredholm, first kind: \(\;f(x)=\lambda\int_a^bK(x,t)\varphi(t)\,dt\)
  • Fredholm, second kind: \(\;\varphi(x)=f(x)+\lambda\int_a^bK(x,t)\varphi(t)\,dt\)
  • Volterra, first kind: \(\;f(x)=\lambda\int_a^xK(x,t)\varphi(t)\,dt\)
  • Volterra, second kind: \(\;\varphi(x)=f(x)+\lambda\int_a^xK(x,t)\varphi(t)\,dt\)

Volterra is a special Fredholm. A Volterra equation is a Fredholm equation with kernel \(K(x,t)=0\) for \(t>x\). This is why a Volterra kernel is “triangular”.

Other names.

  • Singular integral equation: infinite limits or a kernel that is not bounded. Example: Abel’s equation \(\int_0^x\frac{\varphi(t)}{(x-t)^\alpha}dt=f(x)\), \(0<\alpha<1\).
  • Non-linear: \(\varphi\) appears non-linearly, for example \(\varphi(x)=f(x)+\int K(x,t)\varphi(t)^2dt\).
  • Integro-differential: derivatives of \(\varphi\) as well as an integral.
  • Eigenvalue (characteristic) problem: the homogeneous equation \(\varphi=\lambda\int K\varphi\); the values of \(\lambda\) with nonzero solutions are eigenvalues (characteristic values) and the solutions are eigenfunctions.

Convention in this unit: \(\lambda\) multiplies the integral. The eigenvalue \(\lambda\) here is the reciprocal of the eigenvalue \(\mu\) of the operator \((T\varphi)(x)=\int K(x,t)\varphi(t)\,dt\), because \(\varphi=\lambda T\varphi\) means \(T\varphi=\frac1\lambda\varphi\).

Kernels

Kernel Definition Remark
Symmetric \(K(x,t)=K(t,x)\) (complex case: \(K(x,t)=\overline{K(t,x)}\), Hermitian) real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenfunctions
Separable (degenerate) \(K(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^na_i(x)b_i(t)\) with \(\{a_i\}\) linearly independent and \(\{b_i\}\) linearly independent equation reduces to an \(n\times n\) linear system; \(n\) is the rank
Difference (convolution) \(K(x,t)=k(x-t)\) Laplace transform works for Volterra form
Iterated \(K_1=K\), \(K_{n+1}(x,t)=\int K(x,s)K_n(s,t)\,ds\) used in the Neumann series
Resolvent \(R(x,t;\lambda)=\sum_{n\ge0}\lambda^nK_{n+1}(x,t)\) gives the solution formula
\(L^2\) kernel \(\iint\lvert K\rvert^2\,dx\,dt<\infty\) the standard hypothesis for Fredholm theory

Examples. \(K=xt\), \(K=x+t=x\cdot1+1\cdot t\), \(K=\sin(x+t)=\sin x\cos t+\cos x\sin t\), \(K=\cos(x-t)\) are all separable. \(K=e^{xt}\) is not (it is an infinite sum). \(K=\min(x,t)\) is symmetric but not separable. \(K=x-t\) is separable with rank 2 (as \(x\cdot1-1\cdot t\)) but not symmetric, since \(K(t,x)=-K(x,t)\) (skew-symmetric).

Leibniz rule

\(\frac{d}{dx}\int_{\alpha(x)}^{\beta(x)}F(x,t)\,dt=F(x,\beta)\beta'(x)-F(x,\alpha)\alpha'(x)+\int_{\alpha(x)}^{\beta(x)}\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}\,dt .\)

Special case used for Volterra equations:

\(\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^xF(x,t)\,dt=F(x,x)+\int_a^x\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}(x,t)\,dt .\)

Cauchy’s repeated-integration formula.

\(\int_a^x\!\!\int_a^{x_{n-1}}\!\!\cdots\!\int_a^{x_1}g(t)\,dt\,dx_1\cdots dx_{n-1}=\frac1{(n-1)!}\int_a^x(x-t)^{n-1}g(t)\,dt .\)

For \(n=2\): \(\int_a^x\int_a^sg=\int_a^x(x-t)g(t)\,dt\).

From a differential equation to an integral equation

Initial value problem to Volterra of the second kind.

Take \(y''+A(x)y'+B(x)y=g(x)\), \(y(0)=c_0\), \(y'(0)=c_1\). Integrate once from \(0\) to \(x\), integrate the \(Ay'\) term by parts, integrate again:

\(y(x)=f(x)+\int_0^xK(x,t)\,y(t)\,dt,\)

\(K(x,t)=-A(t)+(x-t)\big[A'(t)-B(t)\big],\quad f(x)=c_0+\big(c_1+A(0)c_0\big)x+\int_0^x(x-t)g(t)\,dt .\)

Special case \(A=0\) (by far the most common): \(y''+B(x)y=g(x)\) becomes

\(y(x)=c_0+c_1x+\int_0^x(x-t)g(t)\,dt-\int_0^x(x-t)B(t)y(t)\,dt .\)

So the kernel is \(K(x,t)=-(x-t)B(t)\). The initial conditions are built into \(f\).

First order: \(y'=F(x,y)\), \(y(a)=y_0\) gives \(y(x)=y_0+\int_a^xF(t,y(t))\,dt\) (Volterra, nonlinear unless \(F\) is linear in \(y\)). For the linear case \(y'+P(x)y=Q(x)\): \(y(x)=y_0+\int_a^x\big[Q(t)-P(t)y(t)\big]dt\).

Rule. Initial value problem leads to Volterra. Boundary value problem leads to Fredholm.

From an integral equation back to an ODE

Differentiate the equation using Leibniz’s rule until the integral disappears. The initial conditions come from setting \(x=a\) in the equation and in each derivative.

Example: \(\varphi(x)=1+\int_0^x(x-t)\varphi(t)\,dt\). Then \(\varphi'(x)=\int_0^x\varphi(t)\,dt\) (the term \(F(x,x)=0\)) and \(\varphi''=\varphi\). At \(x=0\): \(\varphi(0)=1\), \(\varphi'(0)=0\). So \(\varphi=\cosh x\).

First-kind Volterra. \(f(x)=\int_0^xK(x,t)\varphi(t)\,dt\). Differentiating: \(f'(x)=K(x,x)\varphi(x)+\int_0^xK_x(x,t)\varphi(t)\,dt\). This needs \(f(0)=0\) (necessary condition) and if \(K(x,x)\ne0\) it becomes a second-kind equation \(\varphi=\frac{f'}{K(x,x)}-\int\frac{K_x}{K(x,x)}\varphi\).

Boundary value problem to Fredholm equation: Green’s function

Consider a self-adjoint second-order operator \(L[y]=\big(p(x)y'\big)'+q(x)y\) on \([a,b]\) with homogeneous boundary conditions. The Green’s function \(G(x,t)\) gives the solution of \(L[y]=f\) as \(y(x)=\int_a^bG(x,t)f(t)\,dt\).

Defining properties (for fixed \(t\)).

  1. \(G\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) (including at \(x=t\)).
  2. \(L[G]=0\) for \(x\ne t\).
  3. \(G\) satisfies the boundary conditions at \(x=a\) and \(x=b\).
  4. \(G_x\) has a jump at \(x=t\): \(G_x(t^+,t)-G_x(t^-,t)=\dfrac1{p(t)}\).

Construction.

  1. Find \(u_1\) solving \(L[u]=0\) and the left boundary condition; \(u_2\) solving \(L[u]=0\) and the right boundary condition.
  2. \(G(x,t)=c\,u_1(x_<)\,u_2(x_>)\), where \(x_<=\min(x,t)\) and \(x_>=\max(x,t)\). This ensures continuity and both boundary conditions.
  3. Fix \(c\) from the jump: \(c\,\big[u_1u_2'-u_1'u_2\big]\,p=1\), i.e. \(c=\dfrac1{p\,W}\) where \(W=u_1u_2'-u_1'u_2\) is the Wronskian (\(pW\) is constant).

Properties: \(G\) is symmetric when \(L\) is self-adjoint (\(G(x,t)=G(t,x)\)). It exists iff the homogeneous problem \(L[y]=0\) with the boundary conditions has only the trivial solution.

Two standard Green’s functions for \(y''\) on \([0,1]\).

Boundary conditions \(G(x,t)\) for \(y''=f\)
\(y(0)=y(1)=0\) \(x(t-1)\) for \(x\le t\), and \(t(x-1)\) for \(x\ge t\)
\(y(0)=0\), \(y'(1)=0\) \(-\min(x,t)\)

Eigenvalue problem becomes a Fredholm equation. Take \(y''+\lambda y=0\), \(y(0)=y(1)=0\). Then \(y''=-\lambda y\), so \(y(x)=-\lambda\int_0^1G(x,t)y(t)\,dt\). Put \(\mathcal G=-G\ge0\):

\(y(x)=\lambda\int_0^1\mathcal G(x,t)\,y(t)\,dt,\qquad\mathcal G(x,t)=\begin{cases}t(1-x),&t\le x\\x(1-t),&x\le t.\end{cases}\)

This is a homogeneous Fredholm equation of the second kind with a symmetric kernel. Its eigenvalues are \(\lambda_n=n^2\pi^2\) and its eigenfunctions \(\sin n\pi x\). The inhomogeneous problem \(y''+\lambda y=f\) becomes \(y(x)=F(x)+\lambda\int_0^1\mathcal G(x,t)\,y(t)\,dt\) with \(F(x)=\int_0^1\mathcal G(x,t)f(t)\,dt\).

For the other boundary pair \(y(0)=0\), \(y'(1)=0\): \(y=\lambda\int_0^1\min(x,t)\,y(t)\,dt\), eigenvalues \(\lambda_n=\big(\tfrac{(2n-1)\pi}2\big)^2\), eigenfunctions \(\sin\frac{(2n-1)\pi x}2\).

Non-homogeneous boundary conditions. First subtract a function that satisfies them (for example a linear function) so that the new unknown has homogeneous conditions, then apply the Green’s function.

Meaning of eigenvalue of the BVP vs. kernel. The condition “\(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of the integral equation” means exactly that the BVP \(y''+\lambda y=0\) has a nontrivial solution. At such \(\lambda\) the resolvent kernel does not exist; the smallest positive one is \(\pi^2\) for the Dirichlet problem on \([0,1]\).

Abel’s integral equation (a classical singular case)

\(\displaystyle\int_0^x\frac{\varphi(t)}{(x-t)^\alpha}dt=f(x)\), \(0<\alpha<1\), has the solution

\(\varphi(x)=\frac{\sin\pi\alpha}{\pi}\,\frac{d}{dx}\int_0^x\frac{f(t)}{(x-t)^{1-\alpha}}\,dt .\)

For \(\alpha=\tfrac12\): \(\varphi=\frac1\pi\frac d{dx}\int_0^x\frac{f(t)}{\sqrt{x-t}}dt\). It arises in the tautochrone problem. Example: \(f(x)=x\), \(\alpha=\frac12\) gives \(\varphi(x)=\frac2\pi\sqrt x\).

Worked examples

Example 1: Classify

Classify each equation.

(a) \(\varphi(x)=x+\int_0^1(x+t)\varphi(t)\,dt\). Fredholm, second kind, inhomogeneous, separable symmetric kernel of rank 2.

(b) \(x^2=\int_0^x(x-t)\varphi(t)\,dt\). Volterra, first kind (convolution kernel).

(c) \(\varphi(x)=\lambda\int_0^{\pi}\sin(x+t)\varphi(t)\,dt\). Fredholm, second kind, homogeneous (eigenvalue problem).

(d) \(\varphi(x)=1+\int_0^x\varphi(t)^2dt\). Non-linear Volterra of the second kind.

Example 2: IVP to Volterra

Convert \(y''+y=0\), \(y(0)=0\), \(y'(0)=1\) to an integral equation.

Here \(A=0\), \(B=1\), \(g=0\), \(c_0=0\), \(c_1=1\). So \(y(x)=x-\int_0^x(x-t)y(t)\,dt\).

Check with \(y=\sin x\): \(\int_0^x(x-t)\sin t\,dt=x-\sin x\), and \(x-(x-\sin x)=\sin x\). ✓

Example 3: Integral equation to ODE

Convert \(\varphi(x)=x+\int_0^x(t-x)\varphi(t)\,dt\) to an initial value problem and solve it.

Differentiate: \(\varphi'=1+\big[(x-x)\varphi(x)\big]+\int_0^x(-1)\varphi\,dt=1-\int_0^x\varphi\,dt\). Again: \(\varphi''=-\varphi\). At \(x=0\): \(\varphi(0)=0\), \(\varphi'(0)=1\). So \(\varphi=\sin x\).

Example 4: Green’s function for a mixed problem

Find \(G\) for \(y''=f\), \(y(0)=0\), \(y'(1)=0\), and write \(y''+\lambda y=0\) with these conditions as an integral equation.

\(u_1=x\) (satisfies \(u_1(0)=0\)) and \(u_2=1\) (satisfies \(u_2'(1)=0\)). \(W=u_1u_2'-u_1'u_2=0-1=-1\), \(p=1\), so \(c=-1\). Then \(G(x,t)=-x_<\cdot1=-\min(x,t)\).

Check the jump: for \(x<t\), \(G=-x\) so \(G_x=-1\). For \(x>t\), \(G=-t\) so \(G_x=0\). Jump \(=0-(-1)=1=1/p\). ✓ The eigenvalue equation is \(y(x)=\lambda\int_0^1\min(x,t)\,y(t)\,dt\). Numerically, \(\int_0^1\min(x,t)\sin\frac{\pi t}2dt=\frac4{\pi^2}\sin\frac{\pi x}2\), so \(\lambda_1=\pi^2/4\).

Example 5: Dirichlet eigenvalue problem as a Fredholm equation

Show that \(y''+\lambda y=0\), \(y(0)=y(1)=0\) is equivalent to \(y=\lambda\int_0^1\mathcal G\,y\,dt\) and find the eigenvalues.

\(u_1=x\), \(u_2=x-1\), \(W=x\cdot1-1\cdot(x-1)=1\), \(c=1\). \(G=x_<(x_>-1)\), so \(G\le0\). From \(y''=-\lambda y\): \(y=-\lambda\int Gy=\lambda\int\mathcal Gy\) with \(\mathcal G=x_<(1-x_>)\). Substituting \(y=\sin n\pi x\) gives \(\int\mathcal G\sin n\pi t\,dt=\frac{\sin n\pi x}{n^2\pi^2}\) (confirmed numerically for \(n=1,2,3\)). So \(\lambda=n^2\pi^2\).

Example 6: Leibniz rule

Find \(\dfrac d{dx}\int_x^{x^2}\sin(xt)\,dt\).

Here \(\beta=x^2\), \(\alpha=x\), \(F=\sin(xt)\), \(F_x=t\cos(xt)\). So the derivative is \(2x\sin(x^3)-\sin(x^2)+\int_x^{x^2}t\cos(xt)\,dt\). The remaining integral equals \(\Big[\frac{t\sin xt}{x}+\frac{\cos xt}{x^2}\Big]_x^{x^2}\).

Common traps

  • Fredholm vs Volterra is about the upper limit: constant means Fredholm, \(x\) means Volterra. The kind is about whether \(\varphi\) also appears outside the integral.
  • “First kind” means no \(\varphi(x)\) outside, not “the first equation you see”.
  • IVP gives Volterra, BVP gives Fredholm. Mixing these up is a common one-mark loss.
  • Separable vs symmetric. \(K=x-t\) is separable but not symmetric (it is skew). \(K=\min(x,t)\) is symmetric but not separable. \(K=x+t\) is both.
  • Eigenvalue of the integral equation vs of the operator. With \(\varphi=\lambda\int K\varphi\), \(\lambda\) is the reciprocal of the operator’s eigenvalue. A kernel \(K=xt\) on \([0,1]\) has operator eigenvalue \(1/3\) and characteristic value \(\lambda=3\).
  • Leibniz boundary term. For \(\frac d{dx}\int_a^xF(x,t)dt\) the term \(F(x,x)\) must be kept; it vanishes only when \(F(x,x)=0\) (for example \((x-t)\) kernels).
  • Green’s function sign and jump. The jump of \(G_x\) is \(1/p(t)\) for \(L[G]=\delta\). With \(-y''=\lambda y\) the positive kernel is \(-G\). Check the sign before concluding that eigenvalues are positive.
  • Green’s function may not exist. If the homogeneous BVP has a nontrivial solution (for example \(y''+\pi^2y=0\) with Dirichlet conditions) there is no ordinary Green’s function.
  • Initial conditions in the integral equation. They are inside \(f\); the integral equation itself has no separate initial conditions.

Previous-year questions

2026 Q45 (worked key A, unofficial; medium confidence)

Question (restated). A two-point boundary value problem of the form \(y''=\lambda y\) (Dirichlet conditions) is converted with its Green’s function into a symmetric Fredholm equation \(y(x)=\lambda\int G(x,t)y(t)\,dt\). What is the smallest value of \(\lambda\) at which the resolvent kernel fails to exist? (The printed problem was only partly legible; the key reads it as the Dirichlet problem with characteristic values \(n^2\pi^2\).)

Answer. A, \(\lambda=\pi^2\).

Solution. The resolvent kernel fails to exist exactly at the characteristic values (eigenvalues) of the kernel. The Dirichlet Green’s function of \(-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\) on \([0,1]\) has characteristic values \(\lambda_n=n^2\pi^2\). The smallest is \(\pi^2\). The key notes that the sign of \(\lambda\) depends on the convention (\(-\pi^2\) for \(y''=\lambda y\)) and that only positive options were offered.

Quick revision

  • Fredholm: fixed limits \(a,b\). Volterra: upper limit \(x\).
  • First kind: \(\varphi\) only inside the integral. Second kind: \(\varphi\) also outside.
  • Homogeneous: \(f=0\). Eigenvalue \(\lambda\): nonzero solution of \(\varphi=\lambda\int K\varphi\).
  • Separable: \(K=\sum a_i(x)b_i(t)\); symmetric: \(K(x,t)=K(t,x)\).
  • IVP leads to Volterra 2nd kind; BVP leads to Fredholm 2nd kind via Green’s function.
  • \(y''+By=g\), \(y(0)=c_0,y'(0)=c_1\) becomes \(y=c_0+c_1x+\int(x-t)g-\int(x-t)By\).
  • Leibniz: \(\frac d{dx}\int_a^xF(x,t)dt=F(x,x)+\int_a^xF_x\,dt\).
  • Green’s function: continuous, \(L[G]=0\) off \(x=t\), BCs, jump \(1/p\) in \(G_x\).
  • Dirichlet \(y''+\lambda y=0\) on \([0,1]\): \(\mathcal G=x_<(1-x_>)\), \(\lambda_n=n^2\pi^2\).
  • Differentiate a Volterra equation to get an ODE and its initial conditions.
  • Abel’s equation \(\int_0^x\varphi(t)(x-t)^{-\alpha}dt=f\) is the classic singular case.
