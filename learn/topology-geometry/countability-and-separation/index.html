Why it matters for the exam

This topic gave a question in both the 2025 and 2026 papers: the separation axioms of metric spaces (2025-Q94), “totally bounded implies separable” (2025-Q96), and Urysohn metrization applied to a manifold (2026-Q42). Questions ask for the exact implication chain between \(T_0,\dots,T_4\), for a counterexample, or for the hypotheses of Urysohn’s lemma, Tietze’s theorem and Urysohn’s metrization theorem.

Core ideas

Countability axioms

  • First countable: every point has a countable local base (neighbourhoods \(U_1\supseteq U_2\supseteq\cdots\) such that every neighbourhood contains some \(U_n\)).
  • Second countable: the topology has a countable basis.
  • Separable: there is a countable dense subset.
  • Lindelöf: every open cover has a countable subcover.

Implications.

\(\text{second countable}\Rightarrow\text{first countable},\qquad\text{second countable}\Rightarrow\text{separable},\qquad\text{second countable}\Rightarrow\text{Lindelöf},\qquad\text{compact}\Rightarrow\text{Lindelöf}.\)

None of the reverse implications holds in general. For metric spaces:

  • every metric space is first countable (balls \(B(x,1/n)\));
  • separable \(\iff\) second countable \(\iff\) Lindelöf.

So in a metric space the three global countability conditions collapse into one.

Which operations preserve what.

Property Subspace Continuous image Countable product
first countable yes no (open continuous surjection: yes) yes
second countable yes no (open continuous surjection: yes) yes
separable no in general (yes for open subsets, and for all metric subspaces) yes yes
Lindelöf closed subspaces only yes no in general

Every uncountable product of nontrivial spaces fails to be first countable. A product of at most \(\mathfrak c\) separable spaces is separable (\(\mathbb R^{\mathbb R}\) is separable but not first countable).

Standard examples.

Space 1st countable 2nd countable Separable Lindelöf
\(\mathbb R^n\) (usual) yes yes yes yes
\(\mathbb R_\ell\) (lower limit) yes no yes yes
uncountable discrete yes no no no
countable discrete yes yes yes yes
uncountable cofinite no no yes yes (compact)
uncountable cocountable no no no yes
\(\ell^\infty\) (sup-norm) yes no no no
\(\ell^p\), \(1\le p<\infty\); \(C[a,b]\); \(L^p\) yes yes yes yes

Why \(\mathbb R_\ell\) is not second countable: for each real \(a\) the open set \([a,a+1)\) must contain a basic set \(B_a\) with \(a\in B_a\); then \(\inf B_a=a\), so \(a\mapsto B_a\) is injective and a basis is uncountable. \(\mathbb R_\ell\) is separable (\(\mathbb Q\) is dense), so separable does not imply second countable. This is the classic counterexample.

Why a totally bounded metric space is separable (2025-Q96): for each \(n\) choose a finite \(1/n\)-net \(F_n\). Then \(\bigcup_nF_n\) is countable and dense.

Sequences: in a first countable space, \(x\in\overline A\) iff some sequence in \(A\) converges to \(x\), and a function is continuous iff it preserves convergent sequences. Neither holds without first countability. Example: in an uncountable set with the cocountable topology only eventually constant sequences converge, yet the closure of an uncountable proper subset \(A\) is all of \(X\), and no sequence in \(A\) converges to a point outside \(A\).

The separation axioms

All spaces below: \(x,y\) distinct points; \(C,D\) disjoint closed sets.

Axiom Definition
\(T_0\) (Kolmogorov) for \(x\ne y\), some open set contains exactly one of them
\(T_1\) for \(x\ne y\), there is an open set containing \(x\) but not \(y\), and vice versa; equivalent: points are closed (so finite sets are closed)
\(T_2\) (Hausdorff) \(x\ne y\) have disjoint open neighbourhoods
Regular a closed set \(C\) and a point \(x\notin C\) have disjoint open neighbourhoods. \(T_3\) = regular and \(T_1\)
Completely regular for closed \(C\) and \(x\notin C\) there is a continuous \(f:X\to[0,1]\) with \(f(x)=0\), \(f\vert _C=1\). Tychonoff (\(T_{3\frac12}\)) = completely regular and \(T_1\)
Normal disjoint closed sets \(C,D\) have disjoint open neighbourhoods. \(T_4\) = normal and \(T_1\)
Completely normal every subspace is normal (\(T_5\) when also \(T_1\))

Useful reformulations.

  • Regular: for every open \(U\ni x\) there is an open \(V\) with \(x\in V\subseteq\overline V\subseteq U\).
  • Normal: for every closed \(C\subseteq U\) open there is an open \(V\) with \(C\subseteq V\subseteq\overline V\subseteq U\).
  • \(T_1\) implies \(T_0\); in the presence of \(T_1\), normal implies regular (points are closed).

The chain of implications.

\(T_4\Rightarrow T_{3\frac12}\Rightarrow T_3\Rightarrow T_2\Rightarrow T_1\Rightarrow T_0 .\)

None of the arrows reverses. Note: \(T_4\Rightarrow T_{3\frac12}\) uses Urysohn’s lemma; \(T_3\Rightarrow T_2\) because points are closed.

Counterexamples (know at least one for each).

Space Satisfies Fails
Sierpinski space \(\{0,1\}\), open sets \(\emptyset,\{1\},X\) \(T_0\) \(T_1\)
Infinite set, cofinite topology \(T_1\) \(T_2\)
\(\mathbb R\) with the \(K\)-topology (basis: open intervals and sets \((a,b)\setminus K\), \(K=\{1/n\}\)) \(T_2\) regular
Sorgenfrey plane \(\mathbb R_\ell\times\mathbb R_\ell\) completely regular, \(T_1\) normal
3-point space \(\{a,b,c\}\) with open sets \(\emptyset,\{a\},\{a,b\},X\) normal \(T_1\), regular
indiscrete space, 2 or more points regular, normal \(T_0\)

(There are also regular non-completely-regular spaces, but they are not examined.)

Two warnings from the table. First, normal and regular do not imply \(T_0\) without a \(T_1\) hypothesis (that is why \(T_3\) and \(T_4\) include \(T_1\)). Second, normal does not imply regular unless \(T_1\) holds.

Every metric space is \(T_4\) (indeed completely normal). Proof: points are closed and any two points have disjoint balls of radius \(d/2\), so \(T_2\). For disjoint closed \(A,B\) the sets \(U=\{x:d(x,A)<d(x,B)\}\) and \(V=\{x:d(x,B)<d(x,A)\}\) are open (distance functions are continuous), disjoint, and contain \(A\) and \(B\) respectively. The function \(f(x)=\frac{d(x,A)}{d(x,A)+d(x,B)}\) is continuous, \(0\) on \(A\) and \(1\) on \(B\) (so Urysohn’s lemma is trivial in metric spaces). Hence every metric space is \(T_1\), \(T_2\), \(T_3\) and \(T_4\); all four statements in 2025-Q94 are true.

Other facts.

  • Compact Hausdorff \(\Rightarrow\) normal. More generally regular Lindelöf \(\Rightarrow\) normal; hence second countable regular \(\Rightarrow\) normal.
  • Hereditary: \(T_0,T_1,T_2\), regular, completely regular. Normality is not hereditary (only for closed subspaces). Productive: \(T_0\) through completely regular. Normality is not productive: \(\mathbb R_\ell\times\mathbb R_\ell\) is not normal although \(\mathbb R_\ell\) is. (Also \(\mathbb R^J\) for uncountable \(J\) is not normal.)
  • Hausdorff: limits of sequences are unique; the diagonal is closed in \(X\times X\); two continuous maps into a Hausdorff space that agree on a dense set are equal; compact sets are closed; the graph of a continuous map into a Hausdorff space is closed.
  • Quotients: \(T_i\) properties are not preserved in general (see topic 4).

Urysohn’s lemma and Tietze’s theorem

Urysohn’s lemma. If \(X\) is normal and \(A,B\) are disjoint closed sets, there is a continuous \(f:X\to[0,1]\) with \(f|_A=0\) and \(f|_B=1\). (Converse: if such functions exist for all disjoint closed pairs, \(X\) is normal.)

Proof idea: build open sets \(U_r\) for dyadic rationals \(r\in[0,1]\) with \(\overline{U_r}\subseteq U_s\) for \(r<s\) by repeated use of the “\(C\subseteq V\subseteq\overline V\subseteq U\)” form of normality; define \(f(x)=\inf\{r:x\in U_r\}\).

Tietze extension theorem. If \(X\) is normal, \(A\subseteq X\) is closed and \(f:A\to[a,b]\) (or \(\mathbb R\)) is continuous, then \(f\) extends to a continuous function on all of \(X\). Converse: if every continuous \(f:A\to[0,1]\) on a closed \(A\) extends, \(X\) is normal. The extension can be chosen with the same bounds.

Why “closed” is needed: \(A=(0,1]\subset[0,1]\) and \(f(x)=\sin(1/x)\) is continuous and bounded on \(A\) but has no continuous extension to \([0,1]\).

Consequences. A normal \(T_1\) space has many continuous functions: it is completely regular. Disjoint closed sets in a normal space are separated by continuous functions.

Urysohn metrization theorem

Theorem. Every second countable regular (\(T_3\), i.e. regular and \(T_1\)) space is metrizable.

Proof idea: second countable and regular implies normal. Using Urysohn’s lemma pick a countable family of functions \(f_n:X\to[0,1]\) that separates points from closed sets; the map \(x\mapsto(f_n(x))_n\) embeds \(X\) into the Hilbert cube \([0,1]^{\mathbb N}\), which is metrizable. So \(X\) is homeomorphic to a subspace of a metric space.

Facts to remember.

  • It is a sufficient condition. A metrizable space need not be second countable: an uncountable discrete space is metrizable and not second countable. Metrizable and separable together are equivalent to second countable regular \(T_1\).
  • Every second countable regular \(T_1\) space embeds in the Hilbert cube \([0,1]^{\mathbb N}\).
  • A compact Hausdorff space is metrizable iff second countable.
  • Non-metrizable examples: cofinite on an infinite set (not Hausdorff); \(\mathbb R_\ell\) (separable but not second countable; a metrizable separable space is second countable); \(\mathbb R^{\mathbb R}\) (not first countable); the box product \(\mathbb R^\omega\) (not first countable); the long line (locally Euclidean, Hausdorff, not second countable); the ordered square \([0,1]\times[0,1]\) with lexicographic order topology (compact, not separable, not metrizable).
  • \(\mathbb R^\omega\) with the product topology is metrizable (\(d(x,y)=\sum 2^{-n}\min(\lvert x_n-y_n\rvert,1)\)).
  • Nagata-Smirnov: a space is metrizable iff it is regular and \(T_1\) with a \(\sigma\)-locally finite basis. Smirnov: iff paracompact Hausdorff and locally metrizable.

Manifolds and Urysohn (2026-Q42). A manifold is Hausdorff and second countable and locally Euclidean; locally compact Hausdorff implies regular, so Urysohn’s theorem makes it metrizable. If \(d\) is one compatible metric, so is \(c\,d\) for every \(c>0\) and \(\min(d,1)\), and so on. All these metrics are different but induce the same topology, so there are infinitely many compatible metrics. This is the answer to 2026-Q42.

Formulas and results

  • Chain: \(T_4\Rightarrow T_{3\frac12}\Rightarrow T_3\Rightarrow T_2\Rightarrow T_1\Rightarrow T_0\), none reversible.
  • \(T_1\iff\) points closed. \(T_3\) = regular + \(T_1\). \(T_4\) = normal + \(T_1\).
  • Every metric space: first countable, \(T_4\), completely regular.
  • Metric: separable \(\iff\) second countable \(\iff\) Lindelöf. Totally bounded \(\Rightarrow\) separable.
  • Second countable \(\Rightarrow\) first countable, separable, Lindelöf. Compact \(\Rightarrow\) Lindelöf.
  • Compact Hausdorff \(\Rightarrow\) normal. Regular + Lindelöf \(\Rightarrow\) normal.
  • Urysohn lemma: normal \(\Rightarrow\) \(\exists f:X\to[0,1]\), \(f|_A=0\), \(f|_B=1\).
  • Tietze: normal, \(A\) closed, \(f:A\to[a,b]\) continuous extends.
  • Urysohn metrization: second countable regular \(T_1\) \(\Rightarrow\) metrizable (converse false).
  • Hereditary: \(T_0\)-completely regular, countability axioms 1 and 2. Not hereditary: normality, separability, compactness.
  • Not productive: normality (\(\mathbb R_\ell^2\)).
  • \(\mathbb R_\ell\): separable, first countable, Lindelöf, not second countable, not metrizable.

Worked examples

Example 1. Classify a finite topology

\(X=\{a,b,c\}\), open sets \(\emptyset,\{a\},\{a,b\},X\).

  • \(T_0\): \(a\) and \(b\) are separated by \(\{a\}\), \(b\) and \(c\) by \(\{a,b\}\), \(a\) and \(c\) by \(\{a\}\). Yes.
  • \(T_1\): a point \(c\) and \(a\): every open set containing \(c\) is \(X\) so contains \(a\); no open set contains \(c\) but not \(a\). No. (Closed sets are \(\emptyset,\{c\},\{b,c\},X\); \(\{a\}\) is not closed.)
  • Hausdorff, regular: No (\(T_1\) fails, and \(a\), \(c\) have no disjoint neighbourhoods since every nbhd of \(c\) is \(X\)).
  • Normal: nonempty closed sets \(\{c\},\{b,c\},X\) all contain \(c\), so there are no disjoint nonempty closed sets, and normality holds vacuously. Yes.

So this space is \(T_0\), normal, not \(T_1\), not regular. It shows that normal does not imply regular without \(T_1\).

Example 2. \(\mathbb R_\ell\) is not second countable

Suppose \(\mathcal B\) is a basis of \(\mathbb R_\ell\). For each \(a\in\mathbb R\) the set \([a,a+1)\) is open and contains \(a\), so there is \(B_a\in\mathcal B\) with \(a\in B_a\subseteq[a,a+1)\). Every point of \(B_a\) is at least \(a\), so \(\min B_a=a\). Distinct \(a\) give distinct \(B_a\). Hence \(\mathcal B\) is uncountable. Therefore no countable basis exists.

Example 3. Every metric space is normal

Let \(A,B\) be disjoint nonempty closed sets in \((X,d)\). For \(x\notin A\), \(d(x,A)>0\) since \(A\) is closed. The function \(f(x)=d(x,A)/\bigl(d(x,A)+d(x,B)\bigr)\) has nonvanishing denominator (a point cannot be at distance \(0\) from both closed sets), is continuous, equals \(0\) on \(A\) and \(1\) on \(B\). Then \(f^{-1}[0,\tfrac12)\) and \(f^{-1}(\tfrac12,1]\) are disjoint open sets containing \(A\) and \(B\).

Example 4. Hypotheses of Urysohn metrization

Is an uncountable set \(X\) with the discrete topology metrizable? Yes (discrete metric). Is it second countable? No (a basis must contain every singleton). So Urysohn’s theorem gives a sufficient, not a necessary, condition.

Is the cofinite topology on \(\mathbb N\) metrizable? It is second countable (countably many cofinite sets) and \(T_1\), but not regular: for \(x\notin C\) finite closed, any two nonempty open sets meet. So the theorem does not apply, and in fact it is not Hausdorff, hence not metrizable.

Example 5. Number of compatible metrics

A manifold such as \(S^2\) is Hausdorff, second countable, regular, so metrizable. Given one metric \(d\), the metrics \(cd\) for \(c>0\) are pairwise different and all compatible. So the number of compatible metrics is infinite.

Example 6. Tietze needs a closed set

\(X=[0,1]\) is normal (compact Hausdorff). Take \(A=(0,1]\), which is not closed in \(X\), and \(f(x)=\sin(1/x)\), bounded and continuous on \(A\). A continuous extension \(g\) to \([0,1]\) would give \(g(0)=\lim_{x\to0^+}\sin(1/x)\), which does not exist. So the extension fails, and the hypothesis “\(A\) closed” cannot be dropped.

Common traps

  • The implication direction. Regular does not mean \(T_2\) unless \(T_1\) is included: indiscrete spaces are regular and normal. Hence the definitions of \(T_3,T_4\) add “and \(T_1\)”.
  • \(T_4\Rightarrow T_3\) needs \(T_1\). Normal alone does not give regular (3-point example).
  • Every metric space is \(T_4\). There is no “incorrect” option among \(T_1,\dots,T_4\) for metric spaces (2025-Q94).
  • Metrizable does not imply second countable; second countable regular implies metrizable. Examiners swap the direction.
  • Separable versus second countable. In general only second countable \(\Rightarrow\) separable. \(\mathbb R_\ell\) is separable and not second countable. In metric spaces they coincide.
  • Lindelöf is not compact. \(\mathbb R\) is Lindelöf, not compact. Second countable \(\Rightarrow\) Lindelöf.
  • Sequences. Closure points need not be limits of sequences in non-first-countable spaces.
  • Normality is not hereditary or productive. The Sorgenfrey plane is the standard counterexample; subspaces of normal spaces need not be normal; only closed subspaces are.
  • Hausdorff versus \(T_1\). Cofinite infinite space: \(T_1\), not Hausdorff. Finite \(T_1\) spaces are discrete.
  • Urysohn’s lemma needs normal; Tietze needs normal and closed \(A\). A completely regular space need not be normal.
  • Cocountable topology. It is \(T_1\), not Hausdorff, Lindelöf, not separable and not first countable.
  • Totally bounded implies separable, not compact.

Previous-year questions

2025-Q94 (official key D; the question is defective)

Which one of the following statements is incorrect? (A) Every metric space is a \(T_1\) space (B) … \(T_2\) space (C) … \(T_3\) space (D) … \(T_4\) space.

Official key: D. In fact all four statements are true: every metric space is \(T_1\), \(T_2\), \(T_3\) and \(T_4\) (the function \(d(x,A)/(d(x,A)+d(x,B))\) separates disjoint closed sets). The question has no correct “incorrect” option, and the official key still gives D, probably because \(T_4\) is the strongest. If asked in a test, remember that metric spaces satisfy all of them.

2025-Q96 (official key A)

Every totally bounded metric space is (A) separable (B) reducible (C) compact (D) locally compact.

Answer: A. A finite \(1/n\)-net for each \(n\) gives a countable dense set. (C) fails because compactness also needs completeness: \((0,1)\) is totally bounded but not compact. (D) fails for \(\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]\), which is totally bounded but not locally compact. “Reducible” is not a topological term.

2026-Q42 (worked key D; unofficial)

Let a manifold be Hausdorff, second countable and regular. Using the Urysohn metrization theorem, the number of compatible metrics possible is (A) \(0\) (B) \(1\) (C) finite (D) infinite.

Answer: D. The manifold is metrizable by Urysohn’s theorem. If \(d\) is a compatible metric, then \(c\,d\) with \(c>0\) is a different compatible metric, giving infinitely many. (The wording “number of compatible metrics” is vague, so the key is a judgement; it is the only consistent choice.)

Quick revision

  • Chain: \(T_4\Rightarrow T_{3\frac12}\Rightarrow T_3\Rightarrow T_2\Rightarrow T_1\Rightarrow T_0\), no arrow reverses.
  • \(T_1\iff\) points closed. \(T_3\) = regular + \(T_1\); \(T_4\) = normal + \(T_1\).
  • Every metric space is \(T_4\) (use \(d(x,A)\)).
  • Counterexamples: Sierpinski (\(T_0\), not \(T_1\)); cofinite (\(T_1\), not \(T_2\)); \(K\)-topology (\(T_2\), not regular); Sorgenfrey plane (regular, not normal).
  • 2nd countable \(\Rightarrow\) 1st countable, separable, Lindelöf. Metric: separable \(\iff\) 2nd countable \(\iff\) Lindelöf.
  • \(\mathbb R_\ell\): separable, 1st countable, not 2nd countable, not metrizable.
  • Totally bounded metric \(\Rightarrow\) separable.
  • Compact Hausdorff \(\Rightarrow\) normal; regular Lindelöf \(\Rightarrow\) normal.
  • Urysohn lemma: normal, disjoint closed \(A,B\) \(\Rightarrow\) continuous \(f:X\to[0,1]\), \(0\) on \(A\), \(1\) on \(B\).
  • Tietze: normal, \(A\) closed, bounded continuous \(f\) on \(A\) extends.
  • Urysohn metrization: second countable regular \(T_1\Rightarrow\) metrizable. Converse false (uncountable discrete).
  • Normality is neither hereditary nor productive.
