Why it matters for the exam

Geodesics were asked once in the last two papers (2026-Q34, geodesics between antipodal points of a sphere), but the syllabus names them explicitly and they sit on top of topics 07 and 08. The usual forms are: “geodesics of a plane / sphere / cylinder are …”, Clairaut’s relation, “\(\kappa_g=0\)”, and a Gauss-Bonnet statement or numerical use (angle sum of a geodesic triangle on a sphere, \(\iint K\,dA\) of a torus).

Core ideas

Geodesic curvature and geodesics

Let \(\gamma(s)\) be a unit-speed curve on a surface with unit normal \(\mathbf n\) and unit tangent \(T\). Its curvature vector \(\ddot\gamma=\kappa N_\gamma\) splits into a part along \(\mathbf n\) and a part in the tangent plane:

\[\ddot\gamma=\kappa_n\,\mathbf n+\kappa_g\,(\mathbf n\times T),\qquad \kappa_g=\ddot\gamma\cdot(\mathbf n\times T)=\mathbf n\cdot(T\times\ddot\gamma),\qquad \kappa^2=\kappa_n^2+\kappa_g^2 .\]

\(\kappa_n\) is the normal curvature (topic 07) and \(\kappa_g\) is the geodesic curvature: how much the curve bends within the surface. Reversing the orientation of the surface or of the curve changes the sign of \(\kappa_g\). It is intrinsic: it depends only on \(E,F,G\) (so it is preserved by isometries).

Definition. A curve on a surface is a geodesic if \(\kappa_g\equiv0\). Equivalent statements:

  • its acceleration \(\ddot\gamma\) is normal to the surface (so a geodesic is “straight” as seen from inside the surface);
  • its principal normal is \(\pm\mathbf n\) wherever \(\kappa\ne0\); then \(\kappa=\lvert\kappa_n\rvert\);
  • it satisfies the geodesic equations below (with constant speed);
  • it is a critical point of arc length among nearby curves with the same endpoints.

Facts:

  • A geodesic has constant speed; an arc-length parametrisation is the natural one. Straight lines on a surface (rulings of a cylinder or cone, lines of a plane) are geodesics because \(\kappa=0\).
  • Existence and uniqueness: through every point and in every tangent direction there is exactly one maximal geodesic (ODE theory, locally).
  • A shortest path between two points on a surface is a geodesic. The converse is false: a geodesic is only locally shortest. A great-circle arc longer than half the circle, or a helix going several times round a cylinder, is a geodesic that is not shortest.
  • A plane curve on a surface is a geodesic iff its plane is perpendicular to the surface along the curve (the plane contains the normal). A normal section by a plane of symmetry of the surface is a geodesic.
  • Isometries map geodesics to geodesics.

Geodesic equations

With \(\mathbf r_i\) the coordinate vectors, \(\mathbf r_{ij}=\Gamma^1_{ij}\mathbf r_u+\Gamma^2_{ij}\mathbf r_v+(\ldots)\mathbf n\) defines the Christoffel symbols. A curve \(u(t),v(t)\) with constant speed is a geodesic iff

\[u''+\Gamma^1_{11}u'^2+2\Gamma^1_{12}u'v'+\Gamma^1_{22}v'^2=0,\]
\[v''+\Gamma^2_{11}u'^2+2\Gamma^2_{12}u'v'+\Gamma^2_{22}v'^2=0 .\]

They depend only on \(E,F,G\). In orthogonal coordinates (\(F=0\)):

\[\Gamma^1_{11}=\frac{E_u}{2E},\ \Gamma^1_{12}=\frac{E_v}{2E},\ \Gamma^1_{22}=-\frac{G_u}{2E},\qquad \Gamma^2_{11}=-\frac{E_v}{2G},\ \Gamma^2_{12}=\frac{G_u}{2G},\ \Gamma^2_{22}=\frac{G_v}{2G}.\]

(General formulas exist, e.g. \(\Gamma^1_{11}=\dfrac{GE_u-2FF_u+FE_v}{2(EG-F^2)}\).)

The same equations are the Euler-Lagrange equations of the energy \(\tfrac12(Eu'^2+2Fu'v'+Gv'^2)\), or of the length \(\int\sqrt{Eu'^2+2Fu'v'+Gv'^2}\,dt\). If \(E,F,G\) do not depend on \(v\), then \(\partial L/\partial v'\) is constant along a geodesic: a first integral. For \(F=0\) this is \(Gv'=\) const (unit speed), which gives Clairaut below.

Example (sphere). \(E=a^2\), \(G=a^2\sin^2\theta\): \(\Gamma^2_{12}=\cot\theta\), \(\Gamma^1_{22}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta\). The equator \(\theta=\pi/2\), \(\varphi=t\) satisfies both equations (all relevant symbols vanish), so it is a geodesic.

Liouville’s formula and geodesic polar coordinates

Liouville’s formula. In orthogonal coordinates (\(F=0\)), let \(\varphi\) be the angle from the \(u\)-curve direction \(\mathbf r_u/\sqrt E\) to the unit tangent of a curve \(u(s),v(s)\). Then

\[\kappa_g=\frac{d\varphi}{ds}+\frac{G_u}{2G\sqrt E}\sin\varphi-\frac{E_v}{2E\sqrt G}\cos\varphi .\]

(Equivalently \(\kappa_g=\dfrac{d\varphi}{ds}+\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{EG}}\bigl(G_u\,v'-E_v\,u'\bigr)\).) Special cases: on a \(u\)-curve (\(v\) constant, \(\varphi=0\)) \(\kappa_g=-\dfrac{E_v}{2E\sqrt G}\); on a \(v\)-curve (\(u\) constant, \(\varphi=\pi/2\)) \(\kappa_g=\dfrac{G_u}{2G\sqrt E}\). A curve is a geodesic iff \(\kappa_g=0\), so Liouville’s formula gives the geodesic equation in the form \(\dfrac{d\varphi}{ds}=-\dfrac{G_u}{2G\sqrt E}\sin\varphi+\dfrac{E_v}{2E\sqrt G}\cos\varphi\).

Example. Unit sphere \(\mathrm I=du^2+\sin^2u\,dv^2\) (\(E=1\), \(G=\sin^2u\)). A parallel \(u=u_0\) has \(\kappa_g=\dfrac{2\sin u_0\cos u_0}{2\sin^2u_0}=\cot u_0\), which vanishes only on the equator. A meridian (\(v\) constant) has \(E_v=0\), so \(\kappa_g=0\): a geodesic. In the plane with \(\mathrm I=dr^2+r^2d\theta^2\) the circle \(r=c\) has \(\kappa_g=1/c\).

Geodesic polar coordinates. Around a point \(P\), use the distance \(r\) from \(P\) and the direction angle \(\theta\): \(\mathrm I=dr^2+G(r,\theta)\,d\theta^2\) with \(\sqrt G(0,\theta)=0\) and \((\sqrt G)_r(0,\theta)=1\). The Gaussian curvature is \(K=-\dfrac{(\sqrt G)_{rr}}{\sqrt G}\), and for the geodesic circle of radius \(r\) about \(P\)

\[L(r)=2\pi r-\frac{\pi K}{3}r^3+\cdots,\qquad A(r)=\pi r^2-\frac{\pi K}{12}r^4+\cdots,\]

so \(K=\lim_{r\to0}\dfrac{3(2\pi r-L(r))}{\pi r^3}=\lim_{r\to0}\dfrac{12(\pi r^2-A(r))}{\pi r^4}\). On a sphere of radius \(a\) the geodesic circle has \(L=2\pi a\sin(r/a)\), which is shorter than \(2\pi r\); on a surface with \(K<0\) it is longer. The distance \(r\) from \(P\) gives \(\kappa_g=\dfrac{(\sqrt G)_r}{\sqrt G}\) for the geodesic circle \(r=\) const.

Clairaut’s relation

Take a surface of revolution \(\mathbf r=(f(u)\cos v,f(u)\sin v,g(u))\), \(\mathrm I=E\,du^2+f^2dv^2\), \(E=f'^2+g'^2\). Let \(\rho=f(u)\) be the distance from the axis, and \(\psi\) the angle between a geodesic and the parallel through the point (the angle with the meridian is \(\pi/2-\psi\)).

Clairaut’s theorem. Along any geodesic (not a parallel),

\[\rho\cos\psi=\text{constant}\qquad(\text{equivalently } \rho\sin(\text{angle with meridian})=\text{constant}).\]

Converse. A regular curve that satisfies \(\rho\cos\psi=\) const and is not an arc of a parallel is a geodesic.

Proof (unit speed): \(Gv'=f^2v'\) is a first integral. The component of the unit tangent along the parallel direction is \(f\,v'=\cos\psi\). So \(f^2v'=f\cos\psi=c\).

Consequences:

  • Meridians are always geodesics (\(\cos\psi=0\) gives constant \(0\)).
  • A parallel \(u=u_0\) is a geodesic iff \(f'(u_0)=0\), i.e. iff the profile’s tangent is parallel to the axis (so \(\rho\) is stationary there). Sphere: only the equator. Cylinder: every parallel. Cone: no parallel. Torus: the outer and inner equators (\(u=0,\pi\)) but not the top and bottom circles.
  • A geodesic with constant \(c=\rho\cos\psi\) stays in the region \(\rho\ge\lvert c\rvert\); where \(\rho=\lvert c\rvert\) the geodesic is tangent to a parallel (\(\psi=0\)) and turns back or follows it.
  • Geodesic curvature of a parallel (arc-length profile): \(\lvert\kappa_g\rvert=\lvert f'\rvert/f\). This is \(0\) on the cylinder, \(\cot(u/a)/a\) on a sphere (colatitude \(u\)), \(1/u\) on a cone.

Geodesics of the standard surfaces

Plane. \(E=G=1\), \(F=0\), all \(\Gamma=0\): \(u''=v''=0\). Geodesics are straight lines. (In polar coordinates \(\mathrm I=dr^2+r^2d\theta^2\), the first integral \(r^2\theta'=h\) with unit speed gives \(r\cos(\theta-\theta_0)=h\), a line.)

Sphere of radius \(a\). Geodesics are the great circles (intersections with planes through the centre). Proof: for a unit-speed curve on the sphere \(\mathbf n=\gamma/a\) is radial, so a geodesic has \(\ddot\gamma\parallel\gamma\), hence \((\gamma\times\dot\gamma)'=\gamma\times\ddot\gamma=0\): the vector \(\gamma\times\dot\gamma\) is constant, so \(\gamma\) stays in a fixed plane through the origin. Properties:

  • Through two non-antipodal points there is exactly one great circle; it gives two geodesic arcs, the shorter (the unique shortest path, length \(<\pi a\)) and the longer.
  • Through two antipodal points there are infinitely many geodesics (every great semicircle, one for each plane containing the diameter), all of the same length \(\pi a\) and all shortest.
  • Every geodesic is closed, of length \(2\pi a\). Any two great circles meet in two antipodal points. A parallel other than the equator is not a geodesic: its geodesic curvature is \(\cot\theta_0/a\ne0\).

Cylinder of radius \(a\), \(\mathbf r=(a\cos\theta,a\sin\theta,z)\). \(E=a^2\), \(G=1\) are constants, so all \(\Gamma=0\): \(\theta''=z''=0\), i.e. \(\theta=\alpha t+\beta\), \(z=\gamma t+\delta\). Geodesics are the helices \(z=m\theta+c\), including the circles (\(m=0\)) and the rulings (\(\theta\) constant). (Unroll the cylinder to a plane: geodesics become straight lines.) Between two points on a cylinder there are infinitely many geodesics, one winding round \(k\) times for each integer \(k\). For two points on one ruling at height difference \(h\) the geodesic with \(k\) turns has length \(\sqrt{h^2+(2\pi ka)^2}\). An oblique plane section of the cylinder (an ellipse) is not a geodesic.

Cone. The cone unrolls onto a sector of angle \(2\pi\sin\alpha\) (topic 07), so geodesics are straight lines of the sector and the generators. A geodesic of a cone with semi-vertical angle \(\alpha\) crosses itself iff \(\sin\alpha<\tfrac12\) (a straight line of the unrolled plane subtends the angle \(\pi\) at the vertex, which exceeds the sector angle \(2\pi\sin\alpha\) exactly when \(\sin\alpha<\tfrac12\)). A parallel circle is never a geodesic.

Hyperbolic plane. On the upper half-plane with \(ds^2=(dx^2+dy^2)/y^2\) (\(K=-1\)) the geodesics are semicircles centred on the \(x\)-axis and vertical lines.

Gauss-Bonnet theorem (statement level)

Local form. Let \(R\) be a simple region (homeomorphic to a disc) on an oriented surface, bounded by a piecewise smooth, positively oriented simple closed curve with exterior angles \(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k\) at its corners. Then

\[\oint_{\partial R}\kappa_g\,ds+\iint_R K\,dA+\sum_i\theta_i=2\pi .\]

Geodesic triangle (sides geodesics, interior angles \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\); exterior angles \(\pi-\alpha\), etc.):

\[\alpha+\beta+\gamma=\pi+\iint_R K\,dA .\]

So the angle sum is \(>\pi\) if \(K>0\) (sphere), \(=\pi\) if \(K=0\) (plane, cylinder), \(<\pi\) if \(K<0\). On a sphere of radius \(a\) the spherical excess \(\alpha+\beta+\gamma-\pi\) equals Area\(/a^2\).

Global form. For a compact oriented surface \(S\) without boundary, genus \(g\) (Euler characteristic \(\chi=2-2g\)):

\[\iint_S K\,dA=2\pi\chi(S)=2\pi(2-2g) .\]

Sphere \(4\pi\), torus \(0\), genus \(2\): \(-4\pi\). It does not depend on the metric. Consequences: a compact surface of \(K>0\) in \(\mathbb R^3\) is topologically a sphere; there is no compact surface with \(K\le0\) everywhere in \(\mathbb R^3\) (every compact surface in \(\mathbb R^3\) has a point with \(K>0\)); and a closed geodesic cannot bound a disc on a surface with \(K\le0\) (Gauss-Bonnet would force \(\iint K\,dA=2\pi>0\)).

With smooth boundary: \(\oint_{\partial R}\kappa_g\,ds+\iint_RK\,dA=2\pi\chi(R)\); \(\chi=1\) for a disc, \(0\) for an annulus.

Completeness (Hopf-Rinow, statement). On a connected complete surface (the plane, sphere, cylinder, torus) any two points are joined by a shortest geodesic. The plane with a point removed is not complete: points on opposite sides of the hole have no shortest path.

Formulas and results

  • \(\kappa^2=\kappa_n^2+\kappa_g^2\); \(\kappa_g=\mathbf n\cdot(T\times\ddot\gamma)\); geodesic \(\iff\kappa_g=0\).
  • Geodesic equations: \(u''+\Gamma^1_{11}u'^2+2\Gamma^1_{12}u'v'+\Gamma^1_{22}v'^2=0\) and the same for \(v\) with \(\Gamma^2\).
  • Orthogonal coordinates: \(\Gamma^1_{11}=\dfrac{E_u}{2E}\), \(\Gamma^1_{12}=\dfrac{E_v}{2E}\), \(\Gamma^1_{22}=-\dfrac{G_u}{2E}\), \(\Gamma^2_{11}=-\dfrac{E_v}{2G}\), \(\Gamma^2_{12}=\dfrac{G_u}{2G}\), \(\Gamma^2_{22}=\dfrac{G_v}{2G}\).
  • Clairaut: \(\rho\cos\psi=\) const (\(\psi\) angle with the parallel; \(\rho\sin\psi'=\) const for the angle \(\psi'\) with the meridian). First integral for \(\mathrm I=E(u)du^2+G(u)dv^2\): \(G\,v'=\) const.
  • Parallel \(u=u_0\) is a geodesic iff \(f'(u_0)=0\); meridians are always geodesics; \(\lvert\kappa_g\rvert=\lvert f'\rvert/f\) on a parallel.
  • Plane: lines. Sphere: great circles. Cylinder: helices (circles, rulings included). Cone: lines of the unrolled sector.
  • Antipodal points of a sphere: infinitely many geodesics, all of length \(\pi a\).
  • Liouville (\(F=0\)): \(\kappa_g=\dfrac{d\varphi}{ds}+\dfrac{G_u\sin\varphi}{2G\sqrt E}-\dfrac{E_v\cos\varphi}{2E\sqrt G}\). Geodesic polar coordinates: \(\mathrm I=dr^2+G\,d\theta^2\), \(K=-(\sqrt G)_{rr}/\sqrt G\), \(L(r)=2\pi r-\pi Kr^3/3+\cdots\).
  • Cylinder, \(k\) turns over height \(h\): length \(\sqrt{h^2+(2\pi ka)^2}\).
  • Gauss-Bonnet: \(\oint\kappa_g\,ds+\iint K\,dA+\sum\theta_i=2\pi\); geodesic triangle: \(\alpha+\beta+\gamma=\pi+\iint K\,dA\); compact: \(\iint K\,dA=2\pi\chi=2\pi(2-2g)\).

Worked examples

Example 1: Clairaut on a sphere

On the unit sphere a geodesic crosses the equator at \(60^\circ\) to the equator. Find its greatest latitude. At the equator \(\rho=1\), \(\psi=60^\circ\), so \(\rho\cos\psi=\tfrac12\). At the highest point the geodesic is tangent to a parallel (\(\psi=0\)), so \(\rho=\tfrac12\). A parallel of radius \(\tfrac12\) is at latitude \(\arccos\tfrac12=60^\circ\). This agrees with the picture: a great circle inclined at \(60^\circ\) to the equator reaches latitude \(60^\circ\).

Example 2: geodesics on a cylinder

Cylinder with \(a=\dfrac{3}{2\pi}\) and two points on one ruling, a distance \(h=4\) apart. The geodesic that winds once round has length \(\sqrt{4^2+(2\pi a)^2}=\sqrt{16+9}=5\); the ruling has length \(4\); the geodesic with \(k\) turns has length \(\sqrt{16+9k^2}\). All are geodesics; only \(k=0\) is shortest.

Example 3: geodesic equations in polar form

The plane in polar coordinates is \(\mathrm I=dr^2+r^2d\theta^2\), \(E=1\), \(G=r^2\). Here \(\Gamma^1_{22}=-r\), \(\Gamma^2_{12}=1/r\), so \(r''-r\theta'^2=0\) and \(\theta''+\tfrac2rr'\theta'=0\). The second gives \((r^2\theta')'=0\), \(r^2\theta'=h\). Unit speed: \(r'^2+r^2\theta'^2=1\), hence \(\dfrac{dr}{d\theta}=\dfrac{r\sqrt{r^2-h^2}}{h}\), and \(\theta=\theta_0+\arccos(h/r)\), i.e. \(r\cos(\theta-\theta_0)=h\) – a straight line at distance \(h\) from the origin.

Example 4: geodesic curvature and Gauss-Bonnet on a cap

On the unit sphere take the circle of colatitude \(\theta_0=60^\circ\) bounding the polar cap. The circle has length \(2\pi\sin\theta_0=\pi\sqrt3\) and \(\kappa_g=\cot\theta_0=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\). So \(\oint\kappa_g\,ds=\pi\sqrt3\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt3}=\pi\). The cap has area \(2\pi(1-\cos60^\circ)=\pi\) and \(K=1\), so \(\iint K\,dA=\pi\). Sum \(=2\pi=2\pi\chi\)(disc). Gauss-Bonnet holds. For \(\theta_0=90^\circ\) (equator): \(\kappa_g=0\), area \(2\pi\), again \(2\pi\).

Example 5: spherical excess

A geodesic triangle on the unit sphere has angles \(80^\circ,70^\circ,60^\circ\). Sum \(=210^\circ\), excess \(=30^\circ=\pi/6\), so area \(=\pi/6\). On a sphere of radius \(2\) a triangle with angles \(100^\circ,50^\circ,40^\circ\) has excess \(10^\circ=\pi/18\) and area \(4\cdot\pi/18=2\pi/9\). The octant triangle (three right angles) has excess \(\pi/2\), area \(\pi a^2/2\), which is \(\tfrac18\) of the sphere.

Example 6: first integral on a surface of revolution

For \(\mathrm I=du^2+G(u)\,dv^2\), any unit-speed geodesic has \(G\,v'=c\). Then \(u'^2=1-c^2/G\), so the geodesic can only enter the region \(G\ge c^2\) and its equation is \(v=\displaystyle\int\frac{c\,du}{G\sqrt{1-c^2/G}}\). For the cylinder (\(G=a^2\), \(v\) the angle) this gives \(v'=c/a^2\) constant: a helix.

Common traps

  • Antipodal points on a sphere have infinitely many geodesics, not \(1\) or \(2\). The count “\(2\)” belongs to non-antipodal points (two arcs of one great circle), and there only one is shortest. Option set “1, 2, finite, infinite”: for antipodal points the answer is infinite.
  • Not every geodesic is shortest (long arc of a great circle; helices round a cylinder). Every shortest path is a geodesic.
  • Parallels are generally not geodesics; only where \(f'=0\) (equator of a sphere, outer and inner equator of a torus, any circle of a cylinder). Meridians always are.
  • Clairaut: \(\rho\cos\psi\) with \(\psi\) the angle to the parallel (or \(\rho\sin\psi\) with \(\psi\) the angle to the meridian). Mixing the two gives the wrong option. Clairaut applies only to surfaces of revolution.
  • \(\kappa_g=0\) vs \(\kappa=0\). A geodesic need not have zero curvature as a space curve; \(\kappa=\lvert\kappa_n\rvert\).
  • Cylinder geodesics are helices; circles and lines are special cases; ellipses are not.
  • Angle sum of a geodesic triangle is \(\pi+\iint K\,dA\), not \(\pi-\ldots\); on a sphere it exceeds \(\pi\).
  • Gauss-Bonnet total: \(\iint K\,dA=2\pi\chi\), so the torus gives \(0\) (not \(4\pi\)) and genus \(2\) gives \(-4\pi\). A common slip is \(4\pi\chi\) or forgetting the genus formula \(\chi=2-2g\).
  • Liouville’s formula needs orthogonal coordinates and the angle from the \(u\)-curve. The two extra terms carry opposite signs (\(+G_u\sin\varphi\), \(-E_v\cos\varphi\)). A parallel on the unit sphere has \(\kappa_g=\cot u\), not \(\cos u\).
  • Geodesic circle length. \(L(r)<2\pi r\) where \(K>0\) and \(L(r)>2\pi r\) where \(K<0\).
  • Shortest-path claims on the plane minus a point or a sphere minus a point need completeness.

Previous-year questions

2026-Q34. (Worked key, unofficial; the wording is partly OCR-uncertain.) Using global geometric arguments, determine the number of distinct geodesics between antipodal points of a sphere: (A) \(1\) (B) \(2\) (C) finite (D) infinite. Answer: D (infinite). Every plane through the two antipodal points cuts the sphere in a great circle, which gives two semicircular geodesics joining them. There are infinitely many such planes (one for each meridian), so infinitely many geodesics, all of length \(\pi a\). Option B is the count for non-antipodal points. (A pencil mark on the photographed paper shows B; the mathematics and the worked key give D.)

Quick revision

  • Geodesic means \(\kappa_g=0\): acceleration is normal to the surface; constant speed; locally shortest.
  • \(\kappa^2=\kappa_n^2+\kappa_g^2\); \(\kappa_g\) is intrinsic.
  • Plane: lines. Sphere: great circles. Cylinder: helices (incl. circles and rulings).
  • Antipodal points of a sphere: infinitely many geodesics, all of length \(\pi a\).
  • Surface of revolution: meridians are geodesics; parallels only where \(f'=0\).
  • Clairaut: \(\rho\cos\psi=\) const (\(\psi\) with the parallel); first integral \(G\,v'=\) const.
  • Cone with semi-angle \(\alpha\) unrolls to a sector of angle \(2\pi\sin\alpha\).
  • Liouville: \(\kappa_g=\varphi'+\dfrac{G_u\sin\varphi}{2G\sqrt E}-\dfrac{E_v\cos\varphi}{2E\sqrt G}\) (\(F=0\)). Geodesic circle: \(L(r)=2\pi r-\pi Kr^3/3+\cdots\).
  • Gauss-Bonnet: \(\oint\kappa_g+\iint K+\sum\theta_i=2\pi\).
  • Geodesic triangle: angle sum \(=\pi+\iint K\,dA\); sphere excess \(=\) Area\(/a^2\).
  • Compact orientable genus \(g\): \(\iint K\,dA=2\pi(2-2g)\) (sphere \(4\pi\), torus \(0\)).
