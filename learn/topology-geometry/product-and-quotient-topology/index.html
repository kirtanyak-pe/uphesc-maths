Why it matters for the exam

Tychonoff’s theorem was asked in 2019, and the syllabus names product, quotient and Tychonoff explicitly. Expect direct questions: product versus box topology, which properties a product inherits, what a quotient map is, and which space a given identification produces (circle, torus, Möbius band, projective plane). The recognition questions are quick marks if you know the standard pictures.

Core ideas

Product topology

Let \(\{X_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in I}\) be spaces and \(X=\prod X_\alpha\).

  • Product topology: basis = sets \(\prod U_\alpha\) with \(U_\alpha\) open in \(X_\alpha\) and \(U_\alpha=X_\alpha\) for all but finitely many \(\alpha\). Equivalently the subbasis \(\pi_\alpha^{-1}(U)\), \(U\) open in \(X_\alpha\). It is the coarsest topology making all projections \(\pi_\alpha\) continuous.
  • Box topology: basis = all sets \(\prod U_\alpha\) with \(U_\alpha\) open, with no restriction.

For finitely many factors the two topologies coincide, and for \(\prod X_i=X_1\times\dots\times X_n\) the basis is \(U_1\times\dots\times U_n\). The usual topology of \(\mathbb R^n\) is the product topology of \(n\) copies of \(\mathbb R\). The box topology is finer than the product topology for infinite products.

Universal property. A map \(f:Z\to\prod X_\alpha\) is continuous (product topology) iff every \(\pi_\alpha\circ f\) is continuous. This fails for the box topology: \(f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^\omega\), \(f(t)=(t,t,t,\dots)\) is continuous for the product topology but not for the box topology, because \(f^{-1}\bigl(\prod_n(-\tfrac1n,\tfrac1n)\bigr)=\{0\}\) is not open.

Convergence. \(x^{(k)}\to x\) in the product topology iff each coordinate converges. For \(\mathbb R^X\) this is pointwise convergence. So the product topology is the topology of pointwise convergence; uniform convergence is a different, finer topology.

Projections and basic facts

  • Each \(\pi_\alpha\) is continuous, surjective and open. It is generally not closed: \(\pi_1:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) sends the closed set \(\{xy=1\}\) to \(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\). If the fibre \(Y\) is compact, \(\pi_X:X\times Y\to X\) is closed.
  • \(X\times\{y\}\cong X\) (slices are subspaces homeomorphic to the factor).
  • \(X\) is Hausdorff iff the diagonal \(\Delta\subseteq X\times X\) is closed. The graph of a continuous \(f:X\to Y\) is closed in \(X\times Y\) if \(Y\) is Hausdorff.
  • Tube lemma: if \(Y\) is compact and \(N\) is an open set in \(X\times Y\) containing \(\{x_0\}\times Y\), then \(N\) contains \(W\times Y\) for some open \(W\ni x_0\).

What a product inherits.

Property Product topology Box topology (infinite)
\(T_0,T_1,T_2\), regular, completely regular yes (if all factors have it) yes
connected yes (arbitrary products) no (\(\mathbb R^\omega\): bounded sequences form a clopen set)
path-connected yes usually not
compact yes (Tychonoff) no (\(\{0,1\}^\omega\) is not compact; \([0,1]^\omega\) is not compact)
first countable iff all factors are and only countably many are nontrivial no (\(\mathbb R^\omega\))
second countable countable product of second countable no
separable up to \(\mathfrak c\) separable factors no in general
metrizable countable product of metrizable spaces no (\(\mathbb R^\omega\))
normal not in general not in general

Examples. \(\mathbb R^\omega\) with the product topology is metrizable by \(d(x,y)=\sum_n2^{-n}\min(\lvert x_n-y_n\rvert,1)\); with the box topology it is not first countable, not metrizable and not connected. \(\mathbb R^{\mathbb R}\) is not first countable. The Sorgenfrey plane is a product of normal spaces that is not normal.

Tychonoff’s theorem

Theorem (Tychonoff). An arbitrary product of compact spaces is compact in the product topology.

  • It is false for the box topology.
  • It is equivalent to the Axiom of Choice (for arbitrary index sets). For compact Hausdorff factors it is equivalent to the Boolean prime ideal theorem.
  • The finite case (\(X\times Y\) compact if both are) follows from the tube lemma.
  • Corollaries: \([0,1]^I\) is compact for every index set \(I\); the Hilbert cube \([0,1]^{\mathbb N}\) and the Cantor space \(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}\) are compact; \([a,b]^n\) is compact (the finite case), so closed bounded subsets of \(\mathbb R^n\) are compact (Heine-Borel). Every Tychonoff (\(T_{3\frac12}\)) space embeds in a product of copies of \([0,1]\), so it has a compactification.
  • A product of nonempty spaces is compact iff every factor is compact (given nonempty factors, the projections are continuous onto).

Quotient topology

Let \(q:X\to Y\) be a surjection. The quotient topology on \(Y\) is

\(V\subseteq Y\ \text{open}\iff q^{-1}(V)\ \text{open in }X.\)

It is the finest topology on \(Y\) making \(q\) continuous. A continuous surjection \(q\) is a quotient map iff \(Y\) carries this topology. For an equivalence relation \(\sim\), \(X/\!\sim\) with the quotient topology is the quotient space.

A subset of \(X\) is saturated if it is a union of equivalence classes. \(q\) is a quotient map iff images of saturated open (or closed) sets are open (or closed).

Universal property. \(g:Y\to Z\) is continuous iff \(g\circ q\) is continuous. A continuous \(f:X\to Z\) that is constant on the fibres of \(q\) factors uniquely as \(f=\bar f\circ q\) with \(\bar f\) continuous.

Sufficient conditions for a quotient map. A continuous surjection that is open or closed is a quotient map. In particular a continuous surjection from a compact space onto a Hausdorff space is a quotient map. The converse fails: quotient maps need not be open or closed.

Key result (used constantly). If \(X\) is compact and \(Y\) is Hausdorff and \(f:X\to Y\) is a continuous surjection, then \(f\) induces a homeomorphism \(X/\!\sim\ \cong Y\), where \(x\sim x'\) iff \(f(x)=f(x')\).

What quotients preserve. Connectedness, path-connectedness, compactness, separability are preserved by quotient maps (images under continuous maps). Hausdorffness, first countability, second countability are not preserved.

  • \(\mathbb R/\mathbb Q\) (cosets of \(\mathbb Q\) in \(\mathbb R\)) has the indiscrete topology: any nonempty saturated open set contains a dense coset, hence is everything.
  • \(\mathbb R/\mathbb Z\) with \(\mathbb Z\) collapsed to one point is not first countable at the collapsed point.
  • The line with two origins is the quotient of \(\mathbb R\sqcup\mathbb R\) identifying \(x\) in the first copy with \(x\) in the second for \(x\ne0\). It is locally Euclidean, second countable and not Hausdorff (the two origins have no disjoint neighbourhoods).
  • If \(q\) is open, \(X/\!\sim\) is Hausdorff iff the relation \(R=\{(x,x'):x\sim x'\}\) is closed in \(X\times X\).
  • A subspace of a quotient space need not be a quotient. The restriction of a quotient map to a saturated open (or closed) subset is a quotient map. The product of two quotient maps need not be a quotient map, but \(q\times\mathrm{id}_Z\) is one if \(Z\) is locally compact Hausdorff.

Boundary words for compact surfaces (read round the boundary of a polygon; \(a^{-1}\) means the edge is reversed): sphere \(aa^{-1}\); torus \(aba^{-1}b^{-1}\); projective plane \(aa\) (or \(abab\)); Klein bottle \(abab^{-1}\) (equivalently \(aabb\)).

Properties of these surfaces.

Surface Orientable Boundary Euler characteristic
sphere yes none \(2\)
torus yes none \(0\)
Möbius band no one circle \(0\)
Klein bottle no none \(0\)
\(\mathbb{RP}^2\) no none \(1\)
cylinder yes two circles \(0\)

Facts: the Möbius band is a one-sided surface with one boundary component; \(\mathbb{RP}^2\) = Möbius band \(\cup\) disc; the Klein bottle = two Möbius bands glued along their boundary; \(\mathbb{RP}^2\) and the Klein bottle cannot be embedded in \(\mathbb R^3\); \(\mathbb{RP}^1\cong S^1\); \(\mathbb{RP}^n\) is a compact connected Hausdorff \(n\)-manifold, orientable iff \(n\) is odd.

Orbit spaces. For a group \(G\) acting on \(X\), \(X/G\) has the quotient topology. \(T^n=\mathbb R^n/\mathbb Z^n\), \(\mathbb{RP}^n=S^n/\{\pm1\}\).

Formulas and results

  • Product topology basis: \(\prod U_\alpha\) with \(U_\alpha=X_\alpha\) for all but finitely many \(\alpha\); box: no restriction.
  • Product = coarsest topology making all \(\pi_\alpha\) continuous. \(f\) into a product is continuous iff all \(\pi_\alpha\circ f\) are.
  • Projections are continuous, open, surjective; not closed in general.
  • Product topology: coordinatewise (pointwise) convergence.
  • Tychonoff: arbitrary product of compact spaces is compact (product topology); equivalent to the Axiom of Choice.
  • Product inherits: \(T_0\)-\(T_{3\frac12}\), connected, path-connected, compact; countable product inherits first/second countable and metrizable; not normality.
  • Quotient topology: \(V\) open iff \(q^{-1}(V)\) open. Finest topology making \(q\) continuous.
  • Continuous open or closed surjection is a quotient map. Compact to Hausdorff continuous surjection is a quotient map and induces a homeomorphism from the quotient.
  • Quotients preserve connectedness, compactness, separability; not Hausdorff or countability.
  • \(S^1=[0,1]/\{0,1\}\); \(T^2=S^1\times S^1=\mathbb R^2/\mathbb Z^2\); \(\mathbb{RP}^2=S^2/\pm\); \(S^n=D^n/\partial D^n\).
  • Words: sphere \(aa^{-1}\), torus \(aba^{-1}b^{-1}\), \(\mathbb{RP}^2\) \(aa\), Klein bottle \(abab^{-1}\).
  • Euler characteristic: sphere \(2\), torus \(0\), Klein bottle \(0\), Möbius band \(0\), \(\mathbb{RP}^2\) \(1\).

Worked examples

Example 1. Product versus box topology

In \(\mathbb R^\omega\), let \(U=\prod_{n\ge1}(-\tfrac1n,\tfrac1n)\). Is \(U\) open in the product topology? In the box topology?

  • Box: \(U\) is a product of open sets, so it is a basic open set. Open.
  • Product: a basic open set around \(0\) is \(\prod V_n\) with \(V_n=\mathbb R\) for all but finitely many \(n\). Such a set contains points whose \(n\)-th coordinate is \(1\), for large \(n\), which is not in \((-\tfrac1n,\tfrac1n)\). So \(U\) contains no basic set around \(0\). Not open.

Hence the map \(t\mapsto(t,t,\dots)\) from \(\mathbb R\) is continuous in the product topology but not in the box topology.

Example 2. \([0,1]/\{0,1\}\cong S^1\)

Define \(f:[0,1]\to S^1\), \(f(t)=e^{2\pi it}\). It is continuous and surjective, and \(f(s)=f(t)\) iff \(s=t\) or \(\{s,t\}=\{0,1\}\). These are exactly the identifications. \([0,1]\) is compact and \(S^1\) is Hausdorff, so \(f\) induces a homeomorphism \([0,1]/\{0,1\}\to S^1\) (continuous bijection from compact to Hausdorff).

Example 3. A quotient that is not Hausdorff

\(X=\mathbb R\sqcup\mathbb R\) (two copies), with \(x\sim x'\) for \(x\ne0\) in different copies. The quotient has two origins \(0_1,0_2\). Open neighbourhoods of \(0_1\) and \(0_2\) both contain \((-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)\setminus\{0\}\) for small \(\varepsilon\), so they always intersect. The quotient is not Hausdorff although each copy of \(\mathbb R\) is. This is a locally Euclidean, second countable, non-Hausdorff space, hence not a manifold.

Example 4. Identify the quotient

The square \([0,1]^2\) with \((0,y)\sim(1,1-y)\). Walk along the horizontal direction and return flipped. This is the Möbius band. Its boundary is a single circle: the bottom edge runs from \((0,0)\) to \((1,0)\sim(0,1)\), then the top edge runs from \((0,1)\) to \((1,1)\sim(0,0)\), which closes the loop. Euler characteristic: \(\chi=0\) (it deformation retracts to its core circle). It is non-orientable.

Example 5. The figure eight

\([0,1]\) with \(0\sim\tfrac12\sim1\). The two intervals \([0,\tfrac12]\) and \([\tfrac12,1]\) each close up into a circle, and the two circles share the single identified point. This is the wedge \(S^1\vee S^1\) (figure eight). It is compact and connected but not a manifold: near the wedge point it looks like four arcs meeting at a point, so removing the point from a small connected neighbourhood leaves four pieces, whereas a point of a 1-manifold leaves two.

Example 6. Tychonoff in use

Show \(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}\) (the Cantor space) is compact. Each \(\{0,1\}\) is a finite discrete space, hence compact. By Tychonoff the product (product topology) is compact. It is compact and metrizable with \(d(x,y)=\sum2^{-n}\lvert x_n-y_n\rvert\), totally disconnected and has no isolated points; it is homeomorphic to the Cantor set.

Common traps

  • Box versus product. Tychonoff is false for the box topology. The “all but finitely many \(U_\alpha=X_\alpha\)” condition is what the product topology adds.
  • Projections. Open, not closed. Counterexample: the hyperbola \(xy=1\).
  • Normality and products. A product of normal spaces need not be normal (Sorgenfrey plane).
  • Quotient maps are not necessarily open or closed. Open or closed continuous surjections are quotient maps, not the other way round.
  • Hausdorff quotients. A quotient of a Hausdorff space need not be Hausdorff (\(\mathbb R/\mathbb Q\) indiscrete; the line with two origins).
  • Need all three conditions (compact domain, Hausdorff target, continuous surjection) to conclude \(X/\!\sim\ \cong Y\).
  • Möbius band versus Klein bottle versus \(\mathbb{RP}^2\). Möbius band has boundary; the other two are closed surfaces. All three are non-orientable.
  • Torus is a product \(S^1\times S^1\); the Klein bottle and \(\mathbb{RP}^2\) are not products.
  • \(S^n\) versus \(\mathbb{RP}^n\). \(\mathbb{RP}^n=S^n/\pm\) is a quotient; \(\mathbb{RP}^1\cong S^1\), but \(\mathbb{RP}^2\not\cong S^2\) (non-orientable, \(\chi=1\)).
  • Connected products. A product of connected spaces is connected in the product topology; in the box topology \(\mathbb R^\omega\) is disconnected.
  • Convergence in product. Coordinatewise, not uniform.

Previous-year questions

2019 (unnumbered; reconstructed from a solution video)

What does Tychonoff’s theorem assert? (a) a product of compact spaces is compact; the other options (not fully recovered) are different statements about products.

Answer: (a). The product of any family of compact spaces, with the product topology, is compact. (a) is the theorem. It needs the product topology; for the box topology the statement is false.

Quick revision

  • Product topology: basic sets restrict only finitely many coordinates; box topology restricts all. They agree for finite products.
  • Product topology = coarsest making projections continuous; convergence is coordinatewise.
  • \(f\) into a product is continuous iff each coordinate \(\pi_\alpha\circ f\) is.
  • Projections are open and continuous, not closed.
  • Tychonoff: any product of compact spaces is compact (product topology only); equivalent to the Axiom of Choice.
  • Product topology preserves connectedness, path-connectedness, Hausdorff; box \(\mathbb R^\omega\) is not connected.
  • Quotient topology: \(V\) open iff \(q^{-1}(V)\) open; finest topology making \(q\) continuous.
  • Compact to Hausdorff continuous surjection induces a homeomorphism from the quotient.
  • \(S^1=[0,1]/\{0,1\}\); torus \(=S^1\times S^1\); Möbius band \((0,y)\sim(1,1-y)\); \(\mathbb{RP}^2=S^2/\pm\).
  • Word \(aba^{-1}b^{-1}\) = torus; \(abab^{-1}\) = Klein bottle; \(aa\) = \(\mathbb{RP}^2\).
  • Quotients can destroy Hausdorff (\(\mathbb R/\mathbb Q\), line with two origins).
  • Möbius band, Klein bottle, \(\mathbb{RP}^2\) are non-orientable; Möbius band has boundary.
