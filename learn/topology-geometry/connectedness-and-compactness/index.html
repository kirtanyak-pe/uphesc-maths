Why it matters for the exam

This is the most asked part of topology. Typical questions: which properties a continuous map preserves, whether a given set is compact or connected, compact versus complete and totally bounded in metric spaces, and the topologist’s sine curve. Examiners build options that differ by one hypothesis (“Hausdorff”, “metric”, “continuous”), so the exact statements below matter.

Core ideas

Connected spaces

A space \(X\) is disconnected if \(X=U\cup V\) with \(U,V\) disjoint, nonempty and open. Otherwise \(X\) is connected. Equivalent forms:

  • the only clopen subsets are \(\emptyset\) and \(X\);
  • every continuous \(f:X\to\{0,1\}\) (discrete) is constant.

A subset \(A\subseteq X\) is connected if it is connected in the subspace topology. A separation of \(A\) means two disjoint nonempty sets, each open in \(A\), with union \(A\).

Connected subsets of \(\mathbb R\) are exactly the intervals (convex sets, including rays, \(\mathbb R\), points and \(\emptyset\)). So \(\mathbb Q\) is not connected, \([0,1]\) is, and \([0,1]\cup[2,3]\) is not.

Facts (all with proofs by the clopen test).

  1. A continuous image of a connected space is connected. (Intermediate value theorem: \(f([a,b])\) is an interval.)
  2. If \(\{A_\alpha\}\) are connected and all contain a common point (or \(A_\alpha\cap A_\beta\ne\emptyset\) in a chain), then \(\bigcup A_\alpha\) is connected.
  3. If \(A\) is connected and \(A\subseteq B\subseteq\overline A\), then \(B\) is connected. In particular \(\overline A\) is connected.
  4. A product of connected spaces is connected (product topology; arbitrary products too).
  5. If \(A\) is connected and \(A\) meets both \(C\) and \(X\setminus C\), then \(A\) meets \(\partial C\).

Connectedness is not hereditary (\(\mathbb Q\subset\mathbb R\)), but it is preserved by continuous maps and by quotients.

Components and total disconnectedness

The component of \(x\) is the union of all connected sets containing \(x\). Components partition \(X\) and are closed; they need not be open. In \(\mathbb Q\) every component is a single point but no point is open.

A space is totally disconnected if all components are points. Examples: \(\mathbb Q\), \(\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q\), the Cantor set, \(\mathbb R_\ell\), every discrete space. Totally disconnected does not mean discrete.

In a finite space the components are the minimal nonempty clopen sets (Example 1).

Examples of component counts:

Space Components
\(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\) 2
\(\mathbb R^n\setminus\{0\}\), \(n\ge2\) 1
\(GL_n(\mathbb R)\) (by the sign of \(\det\)) 2
\(GL_n(\mathbb C)\) 1
\(O(n)\) 2
\(SO(n)\) 1
\(\{(x,y):xy=1\}\) 2
\(\{(x,y):x^2-y^2=1\}\) 2

Path-connected spaces

\(X\) is path-connected if any two points are joined by a continuous path \(\gamma:[0,1]\to X\). Path-components are the equivalence classes of “joined by a path”.

Path-connected implies connected; the converse is false. The proof: \([0,1]\) is connected, so the image of a path is connected, and a union of such images through a base point is connected.

Topologist’s sine curve. \(S=\{(x,\sin(1/x)):0<x\le1\}\cup(\{0\}\times[-1,1])\).

  • \(S\) is connected: the graph \(G\) is a continuous image of \((0,1]\), hence connected, and \(S=\overline G\).
  • \(S\) is not path-connected: a path from \((0,0)\) to a point of \(G\) would have to approach \(x=0\) along the oscillating part, and the \(y\)-coordinate cannot converge continuously.
  • Consequence: any set \(B\) with \(G\subseteq B\subseteq S\) is connected, e.g. \(G\cup\{(0,0)\}\).

Another standard example: let \(K=\{1/n:n\ge1\}\) and let the comb be \(C=([0,1]\times\{0\})\cup(K\times[0,1])\cup(\{0\}\times[0,1])\). \(C\) is path-connected. The deleted comb \(D=C\setminus(\{0\}\times(0,1))\) is still connected (it lies between \(D\setminus\{(0,1)\}\) and its closure) but not path-connected, because \((0,1)\) cannot be joined to the other points. A connected, locally path-connected space is path-connected. In particular:

  • an open connected subset of \(\mathbb R^n\) is path-connected;
  • a connected manifold is path-connected.

A cut point \(p\) of a connected space is a point such that \(X\setminus\{p\}\) is disconnected. This is used to prove non-homeomorphism.

Local connectedness. \(X\) is locally connected if it has a basis of connected open sets. The topologist’s sine curve is not locally connected. Components of a locally connected space are open.

Compact spaces

A space is compact if every open cover has a finite subcover. Equivalent: every family of closed sets with the finite intersection property has nonempty intersection.

Main facts (with the exact hypotheses).

  1. A closed subset of a compact space is compact. (No Hausdorff needed.)
  2. A compact subset of a Hausdorff space is closed. (False in general: in the indiscrete or cofinite topologies compact sets need not be closed. In the cofinite topology on \(\mathbb N\), every subset is compact but only finite sets and \(\mathbb N\) are closed.)
  3. A continuous image of a compact space is compact.
  4. A continuous real function on a compact space is bounded and attains its max and min.
  5. A continuous bijection from a compact space onto a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism. More generally, a continuous map from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is closed.
  6. A finite union of compact sets is compact. A finite product of compact spaces is compact (tube lemma); arbitrary products: Tychonoff, topic 4.
  7. Compact Hausdorff spaces are normal (so regular and Hausdorff too).
  8. Two disjoint compact sets in a Hausdorff space have disjoint open neighbourhoods.
  9. Heine-Borel: a subset of \(\mathbb R^n\) is compact iff it is closed and bounded. This is only for \(\mathbb R^n\) with the usual metric.
  10. In a compact metric space, every continuous function is uniformly continuous. (Lebesgue number lemma.)

Compactness in metric spaces

For a metric space \((X,d)\) the following are equivalent.

  1. \(X\) is compact.
  2. \(X\) is sequentially compact (every sequence has a convergent subsequence).
  3. \(X\) is complete and totally bounded.
  4. Every infinite subset has a limit point.

Totally bounded means: for every \(\varepsilon>0\), \(X\) is covered by finitely many balls of radius \(\varepsilon\). Facts:

  • totally bounded implies bounded; the converse is false (an infinite set with the discrete metric \(d(x,y)=1\) for \(x\ne y\); the closed unit ball of \(\ell^2\));
  • totally bounded implies separable (and so second countable);
  • a metric space is totally bounded iff every sequence has a Cauchy subsequence;
  • a subset of a complete metric space is totally bounded iff its closure is compact;
  • uniformly continuous maps preserve total boundedness; continuous maps do not (\(\tan:(-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2)\to\mathbb R\)).
Space Complete Totally bounded Compact
\([0,1]\) yes yes yes
\((0,1)\) no yes no
\(\mathbb R\) yes no no
\(\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]\) no yes no
infinite discrete metric (all distances 1) yes no no
closed unit ball of \(\ell^2\) yes no no

Beyond metric spaces. Compact does not imply sequentially compact, and conversely. \([0,1]^{[0,1]}\) (product topology) is compact but not sequentially compact. \(\omega_1\) with the order topology is sequentially compact but not compact. For second countable spaces, compact and sequentially compact agree. Every compact space is Lindelöf (not conversely: \(\mathbb R\)).

Compactness in other topologies: in the discrete topology only finite sets are compact. In \(\mathbb R_\ell\), \([0,1]\) is not compact (cover by \([0,1-\tfrac1n)\) and \([1,2)\)). In the cofinite topology every subset is compact. In the indiscrete topology every subset is compact.

Weaker forms of compactness

  • \(X\) is limit point compact if every infinite subset has a limit point in \(X\).
  • \(X\) is countably compact if every countable open cover has a finite subcover.
  • \(X\) is sequentially compact if every sequence has a convergent subsequence.

Relations. Compact \(\Rightarrow\) countably compact \(\Rightarrow\) limit point compact, and sequentially compact \(\Rightarrow\) countably compact. In a \(T_1\) space, limit point compact \(\iff\) countably compact. A countably compact Lindelöf space is compact; in particular, for second countable spaces (and for metric spaces) countably compact, limit point compact and compact all coincide, and these are also equivalent to sequential compactness. Closed subsets and continuous images of countably compact spaces are countably compact.

Standard example. The ordinal space \([0,\omega_1)\) with the order topology is sequentially compact (hence countably compact and limit point compact) but not compact, because it is not Lindelöf: the open cover by \([0,\alpha)\), \(\alpha<\omega_1\), has no finite subcover. Non-examples of limit point compactness: \(\mathbb R\) (the set \(\mathbb Z\) has no limit point), \((0,1)\) (the set \(\{1/n\}\) has no limit point inside \((0,1)\)), and an infinite discrete space.

Trap. “Every infinite subset has a limit point \(\Rightarrow\) compact” is a theorem for metric spaces only. It fails for \([0,\omega_1)\).

The Cantor set

\(C=\bigcap_n C_n\), where \(C_0=[0,1]\) and \(C_{n+1}\) removes the open middle third of each interval of \(C_n\). \(C\) is compact, totally disconnected, perfect (no isolated points), uncountable, of Lebesgue measure zero, and homeomorphic to \(\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}\). It is the standard example of a compact space that is not connected and has empty interior.

Baire category theorem

A set \(A\subseteq X\) is nowhere dense if \(\overline A\) has empty interior; meagre (first category) if it is a countable union of nowhere dense sets. Points of \(\mathbb R\), the integers and the Cantor set are nowhere dense; \(\mathbb Q\) is meagre in \(\mathbb R\) (a countable union of points).

Baire category theorem. Let \(X\) be a complete metric space or a locally compact Hausdorff space. Then the intersection of countably many dense open sets is dense. Equivalently, a nonempty such \(X\) is not a countable union of nowhere dense sets (it is not meagre in itself), and if \(X=\bigcup F_n\) with each \(F_n\) closed, some \(F_n\) has nonempty interior.

Consequences that are asked.

  • \(\mathbb R\) cannot be written as a countable union of closed sets with empty interior. \(\mathbb R\) is not a countable union of Cantor-type sets.
  • A nonempty complete metric space with no isolated points is uncountable (singletons are nowhere dense). In particular the Cantor set and every interval are uncountable.
  • \(\mathbb Q\) is not a \(G_\delta\) in \(\mathbb R\) (otherwise \(\mathbb Q\) and \(\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q\) would be disjoint dense \(G_\delta\) sets, contradicting Baire). Hence \(\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q\) is not an \(F_\sigma\), and no function on \(\mathbb R\) is continuous exactly at the rationals.
  • \(\mathbb Q\) with the usual topology is not a Baire space: it is a countable union of its own singletons.
  • The set of continuity points of a pointwise limit of continuous functions \(\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) is a dense \(G_\delta\) (so is nonempty).

Trap. Completeness is not what matters but complete metrizability: \((0,1)\) is not complete in the usual metric but is homeomorphic to \(\mathbb R\), so it satisfies the conclusion of the Baire theorem. Baire spaces are a topological notion; completeness is not.

Local compactness and one-point compactification

\(X\) is locally compact (for Hausdorff spaces) if every point has a neighbourhood with compact closure, equivalently a compact neighbourhood.

  • Locally compact: \(\mathbb R^n\), every compact Hausdorff space, every discrete space, every manifold, open and closed subsets of a locally compact Hausdorff space.
  • Not locally compact: \(\mathbb Q\); \(\mathbb R_\ell\) (its compact subsets are countable, so none contains a nonempty open set); infinite-dimensional normed spaces (Riesz: the closed unit ball is not compact); \(\mathbb R^\omega\) with the product topology.

One-point (Alexandroff) compactification. For a locally compact Hausdorff, non-compact space \(X\), put \(X^*=X\cup\{\infty\}\) with open sets: the open sets of \(X\), and sets \(X^*\setminus K\) with \(K\) compact and closed in \(X\). Then:

  • \(X^*\) is compact and Hausdorff, and \(X\) is an open dense subspace;
  • \(X^*\) is Hausdorff iff \(X\) is locally compact Hausdorff;
  • \(\mathbb R^*\cong S^1\) and \((\mathbb R^n)^*\cong S^n\) (stereographic projection);
  • \((0,1)^*\cong S^1\), \(\mathbb N^*\cong\{0\}\cup\{1/n\}\) (a convergent sequence);
  • \((0,1)\sqcup(2,3)\) compactifies to a figure eight (two circles meeting at \(\infty\));
  • \(\mathbb Q\) is not locally compact, so \(\mathbb Q^*\) is compact but not Hausdorff;
  • the Stone-Cech compactification \(\beta X\) is the largest compactification (mention only).

What continuous maps preserve

Property Preserved by continuous maps?
connectedness yes
path-connectedness yes
compactness yes
sequential compactness yes
separability yes
Lindelöf yes
being open, closed no
boundedness, completeness, total boundedness no (metric properties)
Hausdorff, \(T_1\) no
first or second countability no (but yes for open continuous surjections)
local compactness no

Images of a compact set under a continuous map into a Hausdorff space are closed. A homeomorphism preserves all topological properties.

Formulas and results

  • Connected subsets of \(\mathbb R\) = intervals. Compact subsets of \(\mathbb R^n\) = closed and bounded.
  • Continuous image of connected is connected; of compact is compact.
  • Closed subset of compact is compact. Compact subset of Hausdorff is closed.
  • Continuous bijection compact to Hausdorff is a homeomorphism.
  • Metric: compact \(\iff\) sequentially compact \(\iff\) complete and totally bounded.
  • Totally bounded \(\iff\) every sequence has a Cauchy subsequence. Totally bounded \(\Rightarrow\) bounded, separable.
  • Path-connected \(\Rightarrow\) connected. Connected + locally path-connected \(\Rightarrow\) path-connected.
  • \(\overline A\) connected if \(A\) connected; union of connected sets sharing a point is connected.
  • Compact Hausdorff \(\Rightarrow\) normal.
  • One-point compactification: \(\mathbb R^n\to S^n\); \(X^*\) Hausdorff iff \(X\) locally compact Hausdorff.
  • \(GL_n(\mathbb R)\): 2 components. \(O(n)\): 2. \(SO(n)\) and \(GL_n(\mathbb C)\): 1.
  • Compact \(\Rightarrow\) countably compact \(\Rightarrow\) limit point compact; metric or second countable: all equivalent to compact.
  • Baire: a complete metric (or locally compact Hausdorff) space is not a countable union of nowhere dense sets; \(\mathbb Q\) is not a \(G_\delta\).

Worked examples

Example 1. Components of a finite space

Let \(X=\{1,2,3,4\}\) with the topology generated by the basis \(\{1\},\{2\},\{1,2\},\{3,4\}\) (so open sets are the unions of these, plus \(\emptyset\)). Find the connected components.

\(\{1,2\}\) and \(\{3,4\}\) are open, and their complements are each other, so both are clopen. Inside \(\{1,2\}\), the sets \(\{1\}\) and \(\{2\}\) are open and complementary, so \(\{1,2\}\) splits further into \(\{1\}\) and \(\{2\}\). Inside \(\{3,4\}\) the only open subsets are \(\emptyset\) and \(\{3,4\}\), so \(\{3,4\}\) is connected. Components: \(\{1\},\{2\},\{3,4\}\), so 3 components.

Example 2. The topologist’s sine curve is connected but not path-connected

Connectedness: \(G=\{(x,\sin(1/x)):0<x\le1\}\) is the continuous image of \((0,1]\), so it is connected. Every point \((0,y)\), \(-1\le y\le1\), is a limit of points of \(G\), so \(S=\overline G\). A set between a connected set and its closure is connected, so \(S\) is connected.

Path-connectedness: suppose \(\gamma:[0,1]\to S\) is continuous with \(\gamma(0)=(0,0)\) and \(\gamma(1)\in G\). Let \(t_0\) be the last time \(\gamma\) has first coordinate \(0\). After \(t_0\) the path moves in \(G\) with the first coordinate positive, and as \(t\downarrow t_0\) the first coordinate tends to \(0\) while \(\sin(1/x)\) oscillates between \(-1\) and \(1\) infinitely often. By the intermediate value theorem the second coordinate must take every value in \([-1,1]\) on arbitrarily short intervals near \(t_0\), contradicting continuity at \(t_0\). So no such path exists.

Example 3. Compact or not?

Decide which are compact in \(\mathbb R\): \(\{0\}\cup\{1/n:n\ge1\}\); \(\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]\); \([0,1)\); \(\mathbb Z\cap[-5,5]\).

  • \(\{0\}\cup\{1/n\}\): closed and bounded. Compact. (Any open set containing \(0\) contains all but finitely many \(1/n\).)
  • \(\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]\): bounded but not closed in \(\mathbb R\). Not compact.
  • \([0,1)\): bounded but not closed. Not compact (cover by \((-1,1-\tfrac1n)\)).
  • \(\mathbb Z\cap[-5,5]\): finite. Compact.

Example 4. \((0,1)\) is totally bounded but not compact

For \(\varepsilon>0\), the points \(\varepsilon,2\varepsilon,\dots\) up to \(1\) give finitely many \(\varepsilon\)-balls covering \((0,1)\). So \((0,1)\) is totally bounded. The sequence \(1/n\) is Cauchy but has no limit in \((0,1)\), so \((0,1)\) is not complete and hence not compact. This is the standard example for “every sequence has a Cauchy subsequence, but the space is not compact”.

Example 5. One-point compactification of two intervals

\(X=(0,1)\sqcup(2,3)\) is locally compact Hausdorff and non-compact. \(X^*\) adds one point \(\infty\) that is the common limit of all four ends. Each interval together with \(\infty\) is a circle (\((0,1)^*\cong S^1\)), and the two circles share only \(\infty\). So \(X^*\) is the figure eight.

Example 6. Why \([0,1]\) and \(\mathbb R_\ell\) do not mix

Is \([0,1]\) compact in \(\mathbb R_\ell\)? The open sets \([0,1-\tfrac1n)\) for \(n\ge2\) together with \([1,2)\) cover \([0,1]\). A finite subfamily covers only up to \(\max(1-\tfrac1n)<1\) plus \([1,2)\), so it misses points like \(1-\tfrac1{2N}\). No finite subcover: not compact in \(\mathbb R_\ell\), though compact in the usual topology.

Common traps

  • Heine-Borel outside \(\mathbb R^n\). “Closed and bounded implies compact” is false in general metric spaces. The closed unit ball of \(\ell^2\) is closed and bounded, not compact. In an infinite discrete metric space with \(d=1\) the whole space is closed and bounded.
  • Compact versus closed. Compact sets are closed only in Hausdorff spaces. Every subset of an infinite cofinite space is compact and not all are closed.
  • Totally bounded versus compact. Totally bounded does not need completeness. \((0,1)\), \(\mathbb Q\cap[0,1]\) are totally bounded, not compact.
  • Compact versus complete. Compact needs total boundedness as well. \(\mathbb R\) is complete, not compact.
  • Path-connected versus connected. Only path-connected implies connected. The topologist’s sine curve is the counterexample.
  • Continuous images. They preserve compactness and connectedness; they do not preserve openness, closedness, boundedness or completeness. (2019 question.)
  • Continuous bijection. Needs compact domain and Hausdorff codomain to be a homeomorphism.
  • Subsets. Connectedness and compactness are not hereditary. A closed subset of compact is compact; an arbitrary subset is not.
  • Components are closed, not necessarily open. In \(\mathbb Q\), no component is open.
  • Components versus path components. They coincide for locally path-connected spaces (hence manifolds) but not for the sine curve (1 component, 2 path components).
  • One-point compactification of a compact space. If \(X\) is already compact, \(\infty\) is an isolated point and \(X^*=X\sqcup\{\infty\}\).
  • \(\mathbb Q\) is not locally compact. Neither is \(\mathbb R_\ell\) nor any infinite-dimensional normed space.
  • Limit point compact is not compact in general. The equivalence of compact, sequentially compact and limit point compact is for metric (and second countable) spaces; \([0,\omega_1)\) is a counterexample.
  • Baire needs completeness (or local compactness). \(\mathbb Q\) is a countable union of points and is not a Baire space; and \(\mathbb Q\) is not a \(G_\delta\) in \(\mathbb R\).

Previous-year questions

2026-Q54 (worked key B; unofficial)

Let \(X\) be a metric space in which every sequence has a Cauchy subsequence. Which of the following must hold?

(A) compact only (B) totally bounded only (C) complete only (D) complete and totally bounded.

Answer: B. A metric space is totally bounded iff every sequence has a Cauchy subsequence. \(X=(0,1)\) satisfies the hypothesis but is neither complete nor compact, so (A), (C), (D) need not hold. (A photo of the paper has a handwritten underline on (D); mathematics supports only (B), and the worked key is B.)

2019 (unnumbered; reconstructed from a solution video)

Which of the properties “compactness”, “connectedness”, “openness” are not preserved by a continuous map \(f\)?

Answer: openness. A continuous image of a compact set is compact, of a connected set is connected, but the image of an open set under a continuous map need not be open (for example \(x\mapsto x^2\) sends \((-1,1)\) to \([0,1)\), or a constant map).

Quick revision

  • Connected subsets of \(\mathbb R\) are intervals; compact subsets of \(\mathbb R^n\) are closed and bounded (Heine-Borel, \(\mathbb R^n\) only).
  • Continuous image: connected stays connected, compact stays compact; openness is not preserved.
  • Metric space: compact \(\iff\) sequentially compact \(\iff\) complete and totally bounded.
  • Totally bounded \(\iff\) every sequence has a Cauchy subsequence; it implies bounded and separable.
  • Closed in compact is compact; compact in Hausdorff is closed.
  • Continuous bijection from compact to Hausdorff is a homeomorphism.
  • Compact Hausdorff implies normal.
  • Path-connected implies connected; topologist’s sine curve is connected, not path-connected.
  • Components are closed, not nec. open; \(\mathbb Q\) is totally disconnected, not discrete.
  • Components: \(GL_n(\mathbb R)\) 2, \(O(n)\) 2, \(SO(n)\) 1, \(GL_n(\mathbb C)\) 1.
  • One-point compactification of a locally compact Hausdorff space is compact Hausdorff; \(\mathbb R^n\to S^n\).
  • \([0,1]\) is not compact in \(\mathbb R_\ell\); \(\mathbb Q\) is not locally compact.
  • Baire: complete metric \(\Rightarrow\) countable intersection of dense open sets is dense; \(\mathbb Q\) is not a \(G_\delta\).
  • Limit point compact \(\Rightarrow\) compact only for metric/second countable spaces; \([0,\omega_1)\) is the counterexample.
