Why it matters for the exam

Gaussian curvature is the single most asked idea in differential geometry: three questions in the last two papers are “find \(K\)” (sphere of radius \(r\) twice in 2025, the paraboloid \(z=x^2+y^2\) in 2026). Expect also “what is \(K\) of a cylinder or plane”, “what does the Theorema Egregium say”, “which of these is a minimal surface” and one statement-type question on principal curvatures, umbilics or Euler’s theorem.

Core ideas

Principal curvatures and principal directions

At a point \(P\) the normal curvature \(\kappa_n(du:dv)=\mathrm{II}/\mathrm I\) varies with the direction. Its maximum \(\kappa_1\) and minimum \(\kappa_2\) are the principal curvatures; the directions where they occur are the principal directions. They are the roots of

\[(EG-F^2)\kappa^2-(EN-2FM+GL)\kappa+(LN-M^2)=0,\]

equivalently of \(\det(\mathrm{II}-\kappa\,\mathrm I)=0\), i.e.

\[\begin{vmatrix}L-\kappa E&M-\kappa F\\ M-\kappa F&N-\kappa G\end{vmatrix}=0 .\]

They are the eigenvalues of the Weingarten map (shape operator) \(S=-d\mathbf n\), whose matrix in the basis \(\mathbf r_u,\mathbf r_v\) is

\[\frac1{EG-F^2}\begin{pmatrix}GL-FM&GM-FN\\ EM-FL&EN-FM\end{pmatrix}\]

(acting on the column \((du,dv)\)). The Weingarten equations are

\[\mathbf n_u=\frac{(FM-GL)\mathbf r_u+(FL-EM)\mathbf r_v}{EG-F^2},\qquad \mathbf n_v=\frac{(FN-GM)\mathbf r_u+(FM-EN)\mathbf r_v}{EG-F^2}.\]

\(S\) is self-adjoint with respect to \(\mathrm I\), so its eigenvalues are real and the principal directions (for \(\kappa_1\ne\kappa_2\)) are orthogonal.

Reversing the normal \(\mathbf n\to-\mathbf n\) changes \(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\) to \(-\kappa_1,-\kappa_2\) (and swaps which is the maximum).

Gaussian and mean curvature

\[K=\kappa_1\kappa_2=\frac{LN-M^2}{EG-F^2},\qquad H=\frac{\kappa_1+\kappa_2}{2}=\frac{EN-2FM+GL}{2(EG-F^2)} .\]
  • \(K\) does not change under \(\mathbf n\to-\mathbf n\) (product of two sign changes). \(H\) changes sign.
  • Principal curvatures: \(\kappa=H\pm\sqrt{H^2-K}\). Always \(H^2\ge K\), with equality exactly at umbilics.
  • Monge patch \(z=f(x,y)\), with \(p,q,r,s,t\) as in topic 07:
\[K=\frac{rt-s^2}{(1+p^2+q^2)^2},\qquad H=\frac{(1+q^2)r-2pqs+(1+p^2)t}{2(1+p^2+q^2)^{3/2}} .\]
  • Surface of revolution with arc-length profile (\(f'^2+g'^2=1\), \(\mathrm I=du^2+f^2dv^2\)): the parallels and meridians are principal, \(\kappa_{\text{meridian}}=f'g''-f''g'\), \(\kappa_{\text{parallel}}=g'/f\), and
\[K=-\frac{f''}{f}.\]

For a general (not arc-length) profile \(K=\dfrac{(f'g''-f''g')\,g'}{f\,(f'^2+g'^2)^2}\).

  • The third fundamental form \(\mathrm{III}=d\mathbf n\cdot d\mathbf n\) satisfies \(\mathrm{III}-2H\,\mathrm{II}+K\,\mathrm I=0\) (Cayley-Hamilton for \(S\)).

Geometric meaning. \(\lvert K\rvert\) is the ratio of the area of the Gauss image \(\mathbf n(R)\subset S^2\) to the area of \(R\) as the region shrinks to \(P\). \(K>0\) means the surface lies locally on one side of its tangent plane; \(K<0\) means it crosses its tangent plane (saddle).

Euler’s theorem

If \(\phi\) is the angle (measured with \(\mathrm I\)) from the principal direction of \(\kappa_1\) to a direction at \(P\), then

\[\kappa_n(\phi)=\kappa_1\cos^2\phi+\kappa_2\sin^2\phi .\]

Consequences: \(\kappa_n(\phi)+\kappa_n(\phi+\pi/2)=\kappa_1+\kappa_2=2H\) (the average of the normal curvatures in two perpendicular directions is \(H\)); \(\kappa_2\le\kappa_n\le\kappa_1\). The directions with \(\kappa_n=0\) (asymptotic directions) satisfy \(L\,du^2+2M\,du\,dv+N\,dv^2=0\). They exist exactly when \(K\le0\), and measured from the first principal direction they satisfy \(\tan^2\phi=-\kappa_1/\kappa_2\) when \(\kappa_2\ne0\). At a parabolic point (\(K=0\), \(\kappa_2=0\ne\kappa_1\)) there is exactly one asymptotic direction, the principal direction with \(\kappa_n=0\); at a planar point every direction is asymptotic.

Classification of points

Type Condition Picture Examples
Elliptic \(K>0\) \(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\) same sign, \(\mathrm{II}\) definite sphere, ellipsoid, paraboloid \(z=x^2+y^2\), outer part of a torus
Hyperbolic \(K<0\) opposite signs, saddle \(z=xy\), hyperboloid of one sheet, inner part of a torus, helicoid, catenoid
Parabolic \(K=0\), \(\mathrm{II}\ne0\) one principal curvature \(0\) cylinder, cone, the two extreme circles of a torus
Planar \(L=M=N=0\) both \(0\) every point of a plane; the origin of the monkey saddle \(z=x^3-3xy^2\)

The Dupin indicatrix is the conic \(L\,x^2+2M\,xy+N\,y^2=\pm1\) in the tangent plane: an ellipse at elliptic points, a pair of conjugate hyperbolas at hyperbolic points, a pair of parallel lines at parabolic points.

Umbilics

A point is an umbilic if \(\kappa_1=\kappa_2\), i.e. \(\kappa_n\) is the same in all directions, i.e. \(\mathrm{II}\) is proportional to \(\mathrm I\): \(\dfrac{L}{E}=\dfrac{M}{F}=\dfrac{N}{G}\). Every direction is principal there. A planar point is an umbilic with \(\kappa=0\). Examples: all points of a sphere and a plane; the origin of \(z=x^2+y^2\); the four umbilics of an ellipsoid with three unequal axes (they lie on its principal ellipse in the plane of the longest and shortest axes). The cylinder, cone and torus have no umbilics.

Theorem. A connected surface all of whose points are umbilics is a part of a plane or of a sphere. (Also: if \(\kappa_1=\kappa_2=\kappa\) at all points, then \(\kappa\) is constant.)

Lines of curvature

A line of curvature is a curve whose tangent is always a principal direction. They form two orthogonal families (away from umbilics) and satisfy

\[(EM-FL)\,du^2+(EN-GL)\,du\,dv+(FN-GM)\,dv^2=0,\]

the determinant \(\begin{vmatrix}dv^2&-du\,dv&du^2\\E&F&G\\L&M&N\end{vmatrix}=0\).

  • The parametric curves are lines of curvature iff \(F=M=0\). So on a surface of revolution the meridians and parallels are lines of curvature.
  • Rodrigues’ formula: along a line of curvature \(d\mathbf n=-\kappa\,d\mathbf r\) with \(\kappa\) the corresponding principal curvature; conversely this characterises them.
  • On a sphere or a plane every direction is principal, so every curve is a line of curvature there.
  • Joachimsthal: if two surfaces meet along a curve at a constant angle, the curve is a line of curvature of one surface if and only if it is a line of curvature of the other.

Conjugate directions, orthogonal trajectories and the Monge form

Conjugate directions. Two tangent directions \((du:dv)\) and \((\delta u:\delta v)\) at \(P\) are conjugate if

\[L\,du\,\delta u+M\,(du\,\delta v+dv\,\delta u)+N\,dv\,\delta v=0,\]

i.e. \(\mathrm{II}(\mathbf t,\boldsymbol\delta)=0\), equivalently \(\mathrm I(S\mathbf t,\boldsymbol\delta)=0\) for the Weingarten map \(S\). They are conjugate diameters of the Dupin indicatrix.

  • Principal directions are conjugate to each other. Conversely, two directions that are both conjugate and orthogonal are the principal directions.
  • A direction is self-conjugate exactly when it is asymptotic.
  • The parametric curves form a conjugate net iff \(M=0\); they are lines of curvature iff \(F=M=0\).
  • At an elliptic point there are no self-conjugate directions; at a hyperbolic point there are two; at a parabolic point the unique asymptotic direction is conjugate to every direction.
  • Geometrically, the direction conjugate to \(\mathbf t\) is the limit of the line of intersection of the tangent planes at consecutive points along the curve in direction \(\mathbf t\).

Example. On \(z=xy\) at the origin, \(L=N=0\), \(M=1\), so the condition is \(du\,\delta v+dv\,\delta u=0\). Taking \((du:dv)=(1:0)\) forces \(\delta v=0\): the \(x\)-axis direction is self-conjugate (it is asymptotic), and the directions \((1:1)\) and \((1:-1)\) are conjugate to each other and are the principal directions.

Orthogonal trajectories. The curves with \(dv/du=m(u,v)\) are cut at right angles by the curves \(\delta v/\delta u=-\dfrac{E+Fm}{F+Gm}\), which comes from \(E\,du\,\delta u+F(du\,\delta v+dv\,\delta u)+G\,dv\,\delta v=0\). Example: on the unit sphere with \(\mathrm I=du^2+\sin^2u\,dv^2\) (\(u\) the colatitude), the orthogonal trajectories of the curves \(v=u+c\) (\(m=1\)) satisfy \(\delta v/\delta u=-1/\sin^2u\), so \(v=\cot u+c'\). On a surface of revolution the meridians (\(v=\) const) and parallels (\(u=\) const) are orthogonal trajectories of each other because \(F=0\).

Monge canonical form. Choose axes with the tangent plane at \(P\) as the \(xy\)-plane and the principal directions as the \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes. Then near \(P\)

\[z=\tfrac12(\kappa_1x^2+\kappa_2y^2)+\text{higher-order terms},\]

so \(p=q=s=0\), \(r=\kappa_1\), \(t=\kappa_2\) and \(K=rt-s^2=\kappa_1\kappa_2\). The Dupin indicatrix is \(\kappa_1x^2+\kappa_2y^2=\pm1\). Example: \(z=xy\) rotated by \(45^\circ\) becomes \(z=\tfrac12(x'^2-y'^2)\), so \(\kappa_1=1\), \(\kappa_2=-1\), \(K=-1\), \(H=0\) at the origin (agrees with Example 2). For \(z=x^2+2y^2\) at the origin, \(\kappa=2,4\) and \(K=8\).

Theorema Egregium

Gauss’s Theorema Egregium. The Gaussian curvature \(K\) is intrinsic: it can be computed from \(E,F,G\) and their derivatives alone. In orthogonal coordinates (\(F=0\)):

\[K=-\frac1{2\sqrt{EG}}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\!\left(\frac{G_u}{\sqrt{EG}}\right)+\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\!\left(\frac{E_v}{\sqrt{EG}}\right)\right].\]

Special cases: for \(\mathrm I=du^2+G\,dv^2\), \(K=-\dfrac{(\sqrt G)_{uu}}{\sqrt G}\). This formula gives \(K=1\) for \(G=\sin^2u\) (unit sphere), \(K=-1\) for \(G=\cosh^2u\) and \(G=e^{2u}\) (hyperbolic plane), and \(K=0\) for \(G=u^2\) or \(G=1\) (plane).

Consequences:

  1. Isometric surfaces have the same \(K\) at corresponding points. Hence a sphere cannot be mapped onto a plane without distorting distances, so no map projection is perfect.
  2. \(H\), \(\kappa_1,\kappa_2\) and \(\mathrm{II}\) are not intrinsic: the plane and the cylinder are locally isometric but have \(H=0\) and \(H=-1/(2a)\) respectively. The catenoid and helicoid are locally isometric (they belong to one associate family of minimal surfaces: bend one into the other) but not congruent. Being minimal does not by itself make two surfaces isometric.
  3. Minding’s theorem. Two surfaces of the same constant Gaussian curvature are locally isometric. So every surface with \(K=0\) is locally isometric to the plane.
  4. The converse of the Theorema Egregium is false: equal \(K\) at corresponding points does not give an isometry. Example: the helicoid \((u\cos v,u\sin v,v)\) and the surface of revolution \((u\cos v,u\sin v,\ln u)\) both have \(K=-1/(1+u^2)^2\), but the map \((u,v)\mapsto(u,v)\) between them is not an isometry (\(E\) is \(1\) on one and \(1+1/u^2\) on the other).
  5. The Gauss and Codazzi-Mainardi equations link \(E,F,G\) with \(L,M,N\) (the Gauss equation is the Egregium). Bonnet’s theorem: if \(E,F,G,L,M,N\) satisfy them, there is a surface with these fundamental forms, unique up to a rigid motion.

On the torus \(K>0\) on the outside (\(-\pi/2<u<\pi/2\)), \(K<0\) on the inside, and \(K=0\) on the top and bottom circles \(u=\pm\pi/2\). A developable surface (cylinder, cone, tangent developable of a curve) is a ruled surface with \(K=0\); a ruled surface is developable iff \(K=0\).

Minimal surfaces

A minimal surface has \(H=0\) at every point (\(\kappa_1=-\kappa_2\)), so \(K=-\kappa_1^2\le0\). For a graph \(z=f(x,y)\) the condition is Lagrange’s equation \((1+q^2)r-2pqs+(1+p^2)t=0\). Minimal surfaces are the critical points of area (soap films spanning a wire; Plateau’s problem asks for a minimal surface with a given boundary).

  • Examples: the plane, the catenoid (the only non-planar minimal surface of revolution), the helicoid (with the plane, the only ruled minimal surfaces – Catalan), Scherk’s surface \(z=\ln(\cos y/\cos x)\), Enneper’s surface.
  • There is no compact minimal surface in \(\mathbb R^3\) without boundary. The sphere, cylinder and torus are not minimal.
  • At every point of a minimal surface the asymptotic directions are perpendicular (since \(\kappa_1=-\kappa_2\) gives \(\tan^2\phi=1\)).

Formulas and results

  • \(K=\kappa_1\kappa_2=\dfrac{LN-M^2}{EG-F^2}\); \(H=\dfrac{\kappa_1+\kappa_2}2=\dfrac{EN-2FM+GL}{2(EG-F^2)}\); \(\kappa=H\pm\sqrt{H^2-K}\).
  • Characteristic equation: \((EG-F^2)\kappa^2-(EN-2FM+GL)\kappa+(LN-M^2)=0\).
  • Monge: \(K=\dfrac{rt-s^2}{(1+p^2+q^2)^2}\), \(H=\dfrac{(1+q^2)r-2pqs+(1+p^2)t}{2(1+p^2+q^2)^{3/2}}\).
  • Euler: \(\kappa_n=\kappa_1\cos^2\phi+\kappa_2\sin^2\phi\); \(\kappa_n(\phi)+\kappa_n(\phi+\tfrac\pi2)=2H\).
  • Lines of curvature: \((EM-FL)du^2+(EN-GL)du\,dv+(FN-GM)dv^2=0\); \(F=M=0\) gives parametric curves as lines of curvature.
  • Asymptotic directions: \(L\,du^2+2M\,du\,dv+N\,dv^2=0\); need \(K\le0\).
  • Umbilic: \(L/E=M/F=N/G\); all-umbilic connected surface is a plane or sphere.
  • \(\mathrm{III}-2H\,\mathrm{II}+K\,\mathrm I=0\).
  • Egregium: \(K=-\dfrac1{2\sqrt{EG}}\big[\partial_u\big(\tfrac{G_u}{\sqrt{EG}}\big)+\partial_v\big(\tfrac{E_v}{\sqrt{EG}}\big)\big]\); \(\mathrm I=du^2+G\,dv^2\Rightarrow K=-(\sqrt G)_{uu}/\sqrt G\).
  • Revolution, arc-length profile: \(K=-f''/f\).
  • \(K\): sphere \(1/a^2\); plane, cylinder, cone \(0\); helicoid \(-c^2/(u^2+c^2)^2\); catenoid \(-1/(a^2\cosh^4(u/a))\); torus \(\cos u/(a(b+a\cos u))\); paraboloid at the origin \(4\).
  • Minimal: \(H=0\), \(K\le0\); plane, catenoid, helicoid.
  • Conjugate directions: \(L\,du\,\delta u+M(du\,\delta v+dv\,\delta u)+N\,dv\,\delta v=0\); self-conjugate \(\iff\) asymptotic; principal directions are conjugate and orthogonal; conjugate net iff \(M=0\).
  • Orthogonal trajectories of \(dv/du=m\): \(\delta v/\delta u=-(E+Fm)/(F+Gm)\).
  • Monge form: \(z=\tfrac12(\kappa_1x^2+\kappa_2y^2)+\cdots\); Dupin indicatrix \(\kappa_1x^2+\kappa_2y^2=\pm1\).

Worked examples

Example 1: paraboloid at the origin and at \((1,0)\)

\(z=x^2+y^2\): \(p=2x\), \(q=2y\), \(r=t=2\), \(s=0\). At the origin \(p=q=0\), \(W=1\): \(K=\dfrac{4}{1}=4\), \(H=\dfrac{2+2}{2}=2\). \(\kappa=H\pm\sqrt{H^2-K}=2\pm0\), so both principal curvatures are \(2\) and the origin is an umbilic. At \((1,0)\): \(p=2\), \(q=0\), \(W^2=5\). \(K=\dfrac{4}{25}\), \(H=\dfrac{1\cdot2+5\cdot2}{2\cdot5^{3/2}}=\dfrac{6}{5\sqrt5}\). Then \(\kappa=\dfrac{6}{5\sqrt5}\pm\sqrt{\dfrac{36}{125}-\dfrac4{25}}=\dfrac{6}{5\sqrt5}\pm\dfrac{4}{5\sqrt5}\), so \(\kappa_1=\dfrac2{\sqrt5}\), \(\kappa_2=\dfrac2{5\sqrt5}\). The point is elliptic, not an umbilic.

Example 2: saddle \(z=xy\)

At the origin \(E=G=1\), \(F=0\), \(L=N=0\), \(M=1\). \(K=\dfrac{0-1}{1}=-1\). \(\kappa^2=1\) gives \(\kappa=\pm1\) (\(H=0\)). The principal directions are \(\pm45^\circ\) from the axes (\(\kappa_n=\sin2\alpha\), extreme at \(\alpha=45^\circ,135^\circ\)), and the asymptotic directions (where \(\kappa_n=0\)) are the \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes.

Example 3: helicoid

\(\mathrm I=du^2+(u^2+c^2)dv^2\), \(L=N=0\), \(M=-c/\sqrt{u^2+c^2}\). \(K=\dfrac{-M^2}{EG}=-\dfrac{c^2}{(u^2+c^2)^2}\) and \(H=\dfrac{EN-2FM+GL}{2(EG-F^2)}=0\) because \(F=0\) and \(L=N=0\). So \(\kappa=\pm\dfrac{c}{u^2+c^2}\); for \(c=1\), \(u=1\): \(\kappa=\pm\tfrac12\), \(K=-\tfrac14\). It is a minimal surface.

Example 4: Euler’s theorem

At a point \(\kappa_1=3\), \(\kappa_2=-1\). Normal curvature at \(\phi=30^\circ\) from the first principal direction: \(3\cos^230^\circ-\sin^230^\circ=\tfrac94-\tfrac14=2\). Asymptotic directions: \(3\cos^2\phi-\sin^2\phi=0\Rightarrow\tan^2\phi=3\Rightarrow\phi=\pm60^\circ\). Check: the two perpendicular directions \(30^\circ\) and \(120^\circ\) add to \(2H=2\): \(\kappa_n(120^\circ)=3\cdot\tfrac14-\tfrac34=0\). Yes, \(2+0=2=\kappa_1+\kappa_2\).

Example 5: Theorema Egregium on a metric

\(\mathrm I=du^2+\cosh^2u\,dv^2\). \(\sqrt G=\cosh u\), \((\sqrt G)_{uu}=\cosh u\), so \(K=-1\). For \(\mathrm I=du^2+\sin^2u\,dv^2\), \(K=-\dfrac{-\sin u}{\sin u}=1\). Since \(K\) is intrinsic, the first metric is never isometric to a piece of the second.

Example 6: torus

For the torus with \(a=1\), \(b=3\): \(K=\dfrac{\cos u}{3+\cos u}\). At the outer equator (\(u=0\)) \(K=\tfrac14\); at the inner equator (\(u=\pi\)) \(K=-\tfrac12\); at the top (\(u=\pi/2\)) \(K=0\). Principal curvatures at the outer equator: \(\kappa_1=1\) (the tube circle), \(\kappa_2=\tfrac14=\cos u/(b+a\cos u)\) (the equator circle). Product \(=\tfrac14\). At the inner equator they are \(1\) and \(-\tfrac12\) (product \(-\tfrac12\)).

Common traps

  • Sphere \(K=1/r^2\), not \(1/r\) (that is \(\kappa_1=\kappa_2=1/r\), the principal curvature or curve curvature) and not \(1/r^3\). Examiners give \(0\), \(1\), \(1/r\), \(1/r^2\).
  • \(K\) vs \(H\). \(K=\kappa_1\kappa_2\), \(H=\tfrac12(\kappa_1+\kappa_2)\). The sphere has \(H=\pm1/a\) and \(K=1/a^2\). Some books define \(H\) as the sum \(\kappa_1+\kappa_2\) (no \(\tfrac12\)); read the definition in the question.
  • \(H=0\) does not mean flat. It means minimal; the catenoid has \(H=0\), \(K<0\).
  • Cylinder, cone: \(K=0\) but \(H\ne0\). They are developable and locally isometric to the plane even though they bend in space.
  • Egregium misquoted. \(K\) is intrinsic; \(H\) and principal curvatures are not. “Mean curvature is preserved by isometries” is false.
  • The converse of Egregium is false (equal \(K\) pointwise does not imply isometric).
  • Sign of \(K\) for a sphere does not depend on the normal; sign of \(H\) does.
  • At \((0,0)\) vs elsewhere. The paraboloid’s \(K\) is \(4\) only at the origin, not constant. Do not forget \((1+p^2+q^2)^2\) in the denominator.
  • Umbilic is not “\(K=0\)”. It is \(\kappa_1=\kappa_2\); \(K=0\) plus \(H=0\) gives a planar point, which is a special umbilic.
  • Minimal \(\ne\) minimizing area. Minimal surfaces are critical for area but not always minimizing.
  • Direction of asymptotic lines exist only where \(K\le0\); elliptic points have none.
  • Conjugate is not orthogonal. Conjugate directions satisfy \(\mathrm{II}(\mathbf t,\boldsymbol\delta)=0\), orthogonal ones satisfy \(\mathrm I(\mathbf t,\boldsymbol\delta)=0\). Both together characterise the principal directions. An asymptotic direction is conjugate to itself.

Previous-year questions

2025-Q97. The Gaussian curvature at a point of a sphere of radius \(r\) is: (A) zero (B) \(1\) (C) \(1/r\) (D) \(1/r^2\). Answer: D. \(\kappa_1=\kappa_2=1/r\) (in absolute value), so \(K=\kappa_1\kappa_2=1/r^2\). For the parametrisation of topic 07, \(K=\dfrac{(-a)(-a\sin^2\theta)}{a^2\cdot a^2\sin^2\theta}=\dfrac1{a^2}\).

2025-Q99. The Gaussian curvature at every point of a sphere of radius \(a\) is: (A) \(a^3\) (B) \(1/a^3\) (C) \(1/a^2\) (D) \(4\pi a^3\). Answer: C. Same fact; \(K=1/a^2\). The options \(a^3\) and \(4\pi a^3\) are distractors (volume-type), and \(1/a^3\) is a dimension error.

2026-Q58. (Worked key, unofficial.) For the surface \(z=x^2+y^2\), evaluate the first and second fundamental forms and find the Gaussian curvature at the origin: (A) \(0\) (B) \(1\) (C) \(4\) (D) depends on direction. Answer: C (\(4\)). At \((0,0)\): \(p=q=0\), so \(E=G=1\), \(F=0\); \(L=f_{xx}=2\), \(N=f_{yy}=2\), \(M=0\). \(K=\dfrac{LN-M^2}{EG-F^2}=4\). Option D is wrong because \(K\) is a number, independent of direction; option B (\(1\)) is what you get if you wrongly use \(z=\tfrac12(x^2+y^2)\).

Quick revision

  • \(K=\kappa_1\kappa_2=\dfrac{LN-M^2}{EG-F^2}\); \(H=\dfrac{EN-2FM+GL}{2(EG-F^2)}\).
  • Principal curvatures: roots of \((EG-F^2)\kappa^2-(EN-2FM+GL)\kappa+(LN-M^2)=0\).
  • Euler: \(\kappa_n=\kappa_1\cos^2\phi+\kappa_2\sin^2\phi\).
  • Monge: \(K=\dfrac{rt-s^2}{(1+p^2+q^2)^2}\).
  • Sphere \(K=1/a^2\); plane, cylinder, cone \(K=0\); helicoid and catenoid are minimal with \(K<0\).
  • Conjugate: \(\mathrm{II}(\mathbf t,\boldsymbol\delta)=0\); self-conjugate means asymptotic; conjugate and orthogonal means principal.
  • Monge form \(z=\tfrac12(\kappa_1x^2+\kappa_2y^2)\) at a point with the tangent plane as \(xy\)-plane.
  • \(K>0\) elliptic, \(K<0\) hyperbolic, \(K=0\) parabolic (planar if \(L=M=N=0\)).
  • Umbilic: \(\kappa_1=\kappa_2\), i.e. \(L/E=M/F=N/G\); all umbilic \(\Rightarrow\) plane or sphere.
  • Lines of curvature: \(F=M=0\) gives parametric curves as lines of curvature (surface of revolution).
  • Theorema Egregium: \(K\) depends only on \(E,F,G\); isometries preserve \(K\) but not \(H\).
  • \(\mathrm I=du^2+G\,dv^2\Rightarrow K=-(\sqrt G)_{uu}/\sqrt G\).
  • Minimal surface: \(H=0\), hence \(K\le0\); no compact one in \(\mathbb R^3\).
