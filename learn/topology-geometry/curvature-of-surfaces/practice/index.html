Curvature of surfaces

14 questions. Pick an answer to see if it is right and why.

0 of 14 answered · 0 correct

  1. Question 1Easy
    The Gaussian curvature of a surface in terms of the two fundamental forms is

    Why

    \(K=\kappa_1\kappa_2=\det(\text{Weingarten map})=\dfrac{LN-M^2}{EG-F^2}\). The second formula is the mean curvature \(H\) (with the factor \(\tfrac12\) and the cross term).
  2. Question 2Easy
    The principal curvatures at a point of a surface are

    Why

    By definition the principal curvatures are the extreme values of \(\kappa_n\) over all tangent directions; they are the eigenvalues of the Weingarten map. The parametric directions need not be principal unless \(F=M=0\).
  3. Question 3Easy
    The Gaussian curvature of a sphere of radius \(5\) is

    Why

    Both principal curvatures equal \(\pm\dfrac15\) (every point is an umbilic), so \(K=\kappa_1\kappa_2=\dfrac1{25}\). The value \(\tfrac15\) is \(\lvert H\rvert\).
  4. Question 4Easy
    Every point of a circular cylinder is

    Why

    One principal curvature is \(1/a\) (the cross-section circle) and the other is \(0\) (the rulings), so \(K=0\) but \(H=\pm\dfrac1{2a}\ne0\). A planar point needs both to vanish.
  5. Question 5Medium
    The Gaussian curvature of the paraboloid \(z=x^2+y^2\) at the origin is

    Why

    At the origin \(E=G=1\), \(F=0\), \(L=N=2\), \(M=0\), so \(K=\dfrac{LN-M^2}{EG-F^2}=4\). The value \(2\) is each principal curvature (and \(H\)), not \(K\).
  6. Question 6Medium
    The mean curvature (in absolute value) of \(z=x^2+3y^2\) at the origin is

    Why

    At the origin \(E=G=1\), \(F=M=0\), \(L=2\), \(N=6\). So \(\kappa_1=2\), \(\kappa_2=6\) and \(H=\dfrac{\kappa_1+\kappa_2}{2}=4\). The value \(12\) is \(K\).
  7. Question 7Medium
    At a point the principal curvatures are \(3\) and \(1\). By Euler’s theorem, the normal curvature in a direction making \(60^\circ\) with the principal direction of \(\kappa=3\) is

    Why

    \(\kappa_n=\kappa_1\cos^2\theta+\kappa_2\sin^2\theta=3\cdot\tfrac14+1\cdot\tfrac34=\dfrac32\). The value \(\tfrac52\) comes from swapping \(\cos^2\) and \(\sin^2\); \(2\) is just the average \(H\).
  8. Question 8Medium
    A torus has tube radius \(1\) and centre-circle radius \(3\). Its Gaussian curvature at a point of the inner equator (nearest the axis) is

    Why

    For a torus \(K=\dfrac{\cos u}{a(b+a\cos u)}\). At the inner equator \(u=\pi\): \(K=\dfrac{-1}{1\cdot(3-1)}=-\dfrac12\) (hyperbolic: the two principal directions curve opposite ways). At the outer equator \(K=\dfrac14\).
  9. Question 9Medium
    For the helicoid \(\mathbf r=(u\cos v,\,u\sin v,\,2v)\), the Gaussian curvature at \(u=2\) is

    Why

    For \(\mathbf r=(u\cos v,u\sin v,cv)\), \(K=-\dfrac{c^2}{(c^2+u^2)^2}\). With \(c=2\), \(u=2\): \(K=-\dfrac{4}{64}=-\dfrac1{16}\). A helicoid is a ruled minimal surface, so \(K\le0\) everywhere.
  10. Question 10Medium
    Consider: (i) a minimal surface has \(K\le0\) at every point; (ii) the plane is a minimal surface; (iii) the sphere is a minimal surface; (iv) \(K\) is unchanged by an isometry of surfaces. Which statements are correct?

    Why

    (i) \(H=0\) gives \(\kappa_2=-\kappa_1\), so \(K=-\kappa_1^2\le0\). (ii) is true since \(H=0\). (iii) is false: \(H=\pm1/a\ne0\). (iv) is the Theorema Egregium.
  11. Question 11Medium
    Match the surface point with the sign of \(K\). Points: P: any point of a sphere; Q: the origin of \(z=x^2-y^2\); R: any point of a cylinder. Values: 1: \(K>0\); 2: \(K=0\); 3: \(K<0\).

    Why

    Sphere: \(K=1/a^2>0\). Saddle \(z=x^2-y^2\) at the origin has \(\kappa=\pm2\), so \(K=-4<0\). Cylinder: \(K=0\).
  12. Question 12Hard
    At a point the mean curvature is \(H=5\) and the Gaussian curvature is \(K=21\). The larger principal curvature is

    Why

    The principal curvatures are the roots of \(\kappa^2-2H\kappa+K=0\), i.e. \(\kappa=H\pm\sqrt{H^2-K}=5\pm2\). The larger is \(7\) (check: \(7\cdot3=21\), \(\tfrac{7+3}{2}=5\)). The value \(9\) forgets the square root.
  13. Question 13Hard
    The cone \(x^2+y^2=z^2\) has a point at distance \(2\) from the vertex measured along a generator. Taking the mean curvature as \(H=\tfrac12(\kappa_1+\kappa_2)\), the magnitude of \(H\) there is

    Why

    One principal curvature is \(0\) (along the generator). The other, along the circle of latitude, is \(\dfrac{\cot\alpha}{v}=\dfrac12\) for \(\alpha=45^\circ\), \(v=2\). So \(\lvert H\rvert=\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac12=\dfrac14\) (and \(K=0\)). The value \(\tfrac12\) is the non-zero principal curvature, not the mean.
  14. Question 14Hard
    Assertion (A): No piece of a sphere can be mapped isometrically onto a piece of the plane. Reason (R): The Gaussian curvature is preserved by local isometries, and a sphere has \(K>0\) while the plane has \(K=0\). Choose the correct option.

    Why

    This is the Theorema Egregium: \(K\) depends only on \(E,F,G\) and their derivatives, so an isometry preserves it. Since \(1/a^2\ne0\), no isometry exists. This is why every flat map of the Earth distorts distances.
