Why it matters for the exam

Topology gives 3 to 4 questions in each recent paper, and the first-topic questions are the easiest marks in the unit: “is this family a topology?”, “which statement about the discrete or cofinite topology is true?”, “which function is continuous?”. Almost every later topic (compactness, separation, quotients, manifolds) uses the definitions here, so a slip here costs marks everywhere in the unit.

Core ideas

Topology, open and closed sets

A topology on a set \(X\) is a family \(\tau\) of subsets (the open sets) such that

  1. \(\emptyset \in \tau\) and \(X \in \tau\);
  2. any union of members of \(\tau\) is in \(\tau\);
  3. the intersection of finitely many members of \(\tau\) is in \(\tau\).

A set is closed if its complement is open. Closed sets satisfy the dual rules: \(\emptyset\) and \(X\) are closed, arbitrary intersections of closed sets are closed, finite unions of closed sets are closed. A set can be open, closed, both (clopen) or neither. \(X\) and \(\emptyset\) are always clopen.

To test a family on a small set, check three things: \(\emptyset, X\) present; closed under pairwise union; closed under pairwise intersection. The usual failure is a missing intersection or a missing union.

Standard topologies (know all of these)

Topology Open sets Key facts
Discrete on \(X\) every subset finest topology; every function out of \(X\) is continuous; every subset is clopen
Indiscrete (trivial) only \(\emptyset, X\) coarsest topology; every function into \(X\) is continuous; every sequence converges to every point
Cofinite on \(X\) \(\emptyset\) and sets with finite complement for infinite \(X\): \(T_1\), not Hausdorff, compact, connected; any two nonempty open sets meet
Cocountable on uncountable \(X\) \(\emptyset\) and sets with countable complement \(T_1\), not Hausdorff, connected, Lindelöf, not separable; convergent sequences are eventually constant
Usual on \(\mathbb R\) unions of open intervals \((a,b)\) metric topology from \(\lvert x-y\rvert\)
Lower limit (Sorgenfrey) \(\mathbb R_\ell\) unions of \([a,b)\) strictly finer than usual; each \([a,b)\) is clopen
Sierpinski space \(\{0,1\}\) \(\emptyset,\{1\},\{0,1\}\) smallest \(T_0\) space that is not \(T_1\)
Particular point \(p\) \(\emptyset\) and sets containing \(p\) compact if finite; not \(T_1\)
Metric topology unions of open balls every metric space is Hausdorff

For a finite set, cofinite, cocountable and discrete are the same topology.

Comparing topologies

If \(\tau_1 \subseteq \tau_2\), then \(\tau_2\) is finer (larger) and \(\tau_1\) is coarser (smaller). The discrete topology is the finest, the indiscrete the coarsest. The intersection of any family of topologies on \(X\) is a topology. The union of two topologies is usually not a topology (on \(\{a,b,c\}\), \(\{\emptyset,X,\{a\}\}\cup\{\emptyset,X,\{b\}\}\) lacks \(\{a,b\}\)).

A finer topology has more open sets, more closed sets, fewer convergent sequences, and is harder to be compact or connected. The identity map from a finer to a coarser topology is continuous.

The values for \(n\le 4\) were confirmed by brute force; the \(n=5\) values are the standard published counts. On \(\{a,b\}\) the four topologies are: indiscrete, discrete, and two Sierpinski topologies (\(\{\emptyset,\{a\},X\}\) and \(\{\emptyset,\{b\},X\}\)). The two Sierpinski topologies are homeomorphic, which is why there are 3 up to homeomorphism.

Basis and subbasis

A basis for a topology is a collection \(\mathcal B\) of open sets such that every open set is a union of members of \(\mathcal B\). A family \(\mathcal B\) of subsets of \(X\) is a basis for some topology iff

  1. \(\mathcal B\) covers \(X\), and
  2. if \(x\in B_1\cap B_2\) with \(B_1,B_2\in\mathcal B\), there is \(B_3\in\mathcal B\) with \(x\in B_3\subseteq B_1\cap B_2\).

The topology generated by \(\mathcal B\) is the set of all unions of members of \(\mathcal B\).

A subbasis is a family whose finite intersections form a basis. The generated topology is the smallest topology containing the family. Example: the open rays \((a,\infty)\), \((-\infty,b)\) form a subbasis of the usual topology on \(\mathbb R\).

Comparison lemma. Let \(\mathcal B,\mathcal B'\) generate \(\tau,\tau'\). Then \(\tau'\) is finer than \(\tau\) iff for every \(x\) and every \(B\in\mathcal B\) with \(x\in B\) there is \(B'\in\mathcal B'\) with \(x\in B'\subseteq B\).

Using it: \([x,x+\varepsilon)\subseteq(a,b)\) is possible for \(x\in(a,b)\), so \(\mathbb R_\ell\) is finer than the usual topology. It is strictly finer because \([0,1)\) is open in \(\mathbb R_\ell\), but no open interval around \(0\) fits inside \([0,1)\).

A family that is not a basis: all open intervals of length exactly 1. It covers \(\mathbb R\), but \((0,1)\cap(\tfrac12,\tfrac32)=(\tfrac12,1)\) contains no interval of length 1 around any of its points.

Standard bases: open balls (or open boxes) in \(\mathbb R^n\); intervals with rational endpoints for \(\mathbb R\) (a countable basis); \(\{[a,b)\}\) for \(\mathbb R_\ell\).

Neighbourhoods, closure, interior, boundary, limit points

Let \(A\subseteq X\).

  • \(\overline A\) (closure) = smallest closed set containing \(A\) = intersection of closed sets containing \(A\).
  • \(A^\circ\) (interior) = largest open set inside \(A\) = union of open sets inside \(A\).
  • \(x\) is a limit point (accumulation point) of \(A\) if every neighbourhood of \(x\) meets \(A\setminus\{x\}\). The set of limit points is the derived set \(A'\).
  • \(\overline A = A\cup A'\).
  • \(\partial A = \overline A\setminus A^\circ=\overline A\cap\overline{X\setminus A}\).
  • \(A\) is dense if \(\overline A=X\). \(A\) is nowhere dense if \((\overline A)^\circ=\emptyset\).
  • \(x\in\overline A\) iff every basic neighbourhood of \(x\) meets \(A\).
Rule Status
\(\overline{A\cup B}=\overline A\cup\overline B\) always true
\(\overline{A\cap B}\subseteq\overline A\cap\overline B\) inclusion only: \(A=\mathbb Q\), \(B=\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q\) gives \(\emptyset\) on the left, \(\mathbb R\) on the right
\((A\cap B)^\circ=A^\circ\cap B^\circ\) always true
\(A^\circ\cup B^\circ\subseteq(A\cup B)^\circ\) inclusion only: \(A=[0,1]\), \(B=[1,2]\)
\(X\setminus\overline A=(X\setminus A)^\circ\) always true
\(A\) closed iff \(A'\subseteq A\) iff \(A=\overline A\) always true

An isolated point of \(A\) is a point of \(A\) that is not a limit point of \(A\).

In the cofinite topology on an infinite set, every infinite subset is dense, and every point of \(X\) is a limit point of every infinite subset.

Kuratowski’s closure-complement problem. Starting from one set and repeatedly applying closure and complement gives at most 14 distinct sets, and 14 is attained.

Subspace topology

For \(Y\subseteq X\), the subspace topology is \(\tau_Y=\{U\cap Y: U\in\tau\}\). Closed sets of \(Y\) are \(C\cap Y\) with \(C\) closed in \(X\). If \(\mathcal B\) is a basis for \(X\), then \(\{B\cap Y\}\) is a basis for \(Y\).

  • A set open in \(Y\) need not be open in \(X\). If \(Y\) is open in \(X\), then open in \(Y\) implies open in \(X\); if \(Y\) is closed in \(X\), then closed in \(Y\) implies closed in \(X\).
  • \((\tfrac12,1]\) is open in \([0,1]\) (equal to \((\tfrac12,2)\cap[0,1]\)) but not open in \(\mathbb R\).
  • \(\mathbb Z\subset\mathbb R\) and \(\{1/n\}\subset\mathbb R\) are discrete. \(\{0\}\cup\{1/n\}\) is not discrete: \(0\) is a limit point.
  • \(\mathbb Q\subset\mathbb R\) has no isolated points, yet is totally disconnected.
  • A subspace of a subspace is the subspace: \((Z\subseteq Y\subseteq X)\) gives the same topology on \(Z\) either way.

Hereditary properties (inherited by all subspaces): Hausdorff, \(T_0\), \(T_1\), regular, first countable, second countable, metrizable. Not hereditary: compactness, connectedness, normality, separability (in general), completeness.

Continuity, open and closed maps

For \(f:X\to Y\) the following are equivalent (each is a definition):

  1. \(f^{-1}(V)\) is open for every open \(V\subseteq Y\).
  2. \(f^{-1}(C)\) is closed for every closed \(C\subseteq Y\).
  3. \(f(\overline A)\subseteq\overline{f(A)}\) for every \(A\subseteq X\).
  4. \(f^{-1}(B)\) is open for every basic (or subbasic) open \(B\) of \(Y\).
  5. For every \(x\) and every neighbourhood \(V\) of \(f(x)\) there is a neighbourhood \(U\) of \(x\) with \(f(U)\subseteq V\).

Facts: compositions, restrictions and inclusions of continuous maps are continuous. Pasting lemma: if \(X=A\cup B\) with \(A,B\) both closed (or both open) and \(f|_A\), \(f|_B\) continuous and agreeing on \(A\cap B\), then \(f\) is continuous. (Closed sets: finitely many only.)

In a first countable space (every metric space), \(f\) is continuous iff it preserves convergent sequences. For a general topological space this fails.

Examples.

  • Every map out of a discrete space is continuous. Every map into an indiscrete space is continuous.
  • \(\mathrm{id}:(\mathbb R,\text{discrete})\to(\mathbb R,\text{usual})\) is continuous; the reverse direction is not.
  • A continuous map from \((\mathbb R,\text{cofinite})\) to \(\mathbb R\) (usual) must be constant: if \(f(x)\ne f(y)\), take disjoint open sets around the two values; their preimages would be disjoint nonempty open sets in the cofinite topology, which cannot exist. The same argument shows that a continuous map from a space in which any two nonempty open sets meet to a Hausdorff space is constant.
  • A function \(f:\mathbb R_\ell\to\mathbb R\) (usual topology on \(\mathbb R\)) is continuous iff \(f\) is right-continuous at every point. The indicator \(\chi_{[0,1)}\) is continuous on \(\mathbb R_\ell\) but not on \(\mathbb R\).

A map \(f\) is open if it sends open sets to open sets, closed if it sends closed sets to closed sets. They are independent of continuity and of each other.

Map Continuous Open Closed
Projection \(\pi_1:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) yes yes no (the image of the closed hyperbola \(xy=1\) is \(\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}\))
Inclusion of \([0,1]\) in \(\mathbb R\) yes no yes
\(x\mapsto x^2\), \(\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) yes no (\((-1,1)\mapsto[0,1)\)) yes
Constant map \(\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) yes no yes
\(\mathrm{id}:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R_\ell\) (usual to lower limit) no yes yes (usual-closed sets are \(\mathbb R_\ell\)-closed)

Homeomorphism

\(f:X\to Y\) is a homeomorphism if it is a bijection, continuous, with continuous inverse. Equivalent: a continuous open bijection, or a continuous closed bijection. A property preserved by homeomorphisms is a topological property (connectedness, compactness, Hausdorff, countability axioms, number of components, existence of a cut point). Boundedness and completeness are metric, not topological: \((0,1)\cong\mathbb R\).

Continuous bijections that are not homeomorphisms.

  • \(f:[0,2\pi)\to S^1\), \(t\mapsto(\cos t,\sin t)\).
  • \(\mathrm{id}:(\mathbb R,\text{discrete})\to(\mathbb R,\text{usual})\).

If \(X\) is compact and \(Y\) is Hausdorff, every continuous bijection \(X\to Y\) is a homeomorphism (topic 2).

Proving two spaces are not homeomorphic. Find a topological property one has and the other lacks. The usual tool is removal of a point.

  • \([0,1]\not\cong(0,1)\): one is compact.
  • \((0,1)\not\cong[0,1)\): removing an interior point of \((0,1)\) always disconnects it; removing \(0\) from \([0,1)\) leaves it connected, and removing any other point disconnects. So \([0,1)\) has a non-cut point and \((0,1)\) has none.
  • \(\mathbb R\not\cong\mathbb R^2\): removing a point disconnects \(\mathbb R\) but not \(\mathbb R^2\).
  • \(S^1\not\cong[0,1]\): removing a point leaves \(S^1\) connected, but removing an interior point disconnects \([0,1]\).
  • \(\mathbb R^n\not\cong\mathbb R^m\) for \(n\ne m\) (invariance of domain; point removal is not enough).

Homeomorphisms to know. \((a,b)\cong(0,1)\cong\mathbb R\) (use \(\tan\)). \(S^n\setminus\{\text{pt}\}\cong\mathbb R^n\) (stereographic projection). Open disc \(\cong\mathbb R^2\). \([a,b]\cong[0,1]\). Any open convex bounded subset of \(\mathbb R^n\) is homeomorphic to \(\mathbb R^n\).

Convergence

A sequence \(x_n\to x\) if every neighbourhood of \(x\) contains \(x_n\) for all large \(n\). In a Hausdorff space limits are unique. In an indiscrete space every sequence converges to every point. In the cofinite topology on an infinite set, any sequence of distinct points converges to every point of \(X\). In a discrete space only eventually constant sequences converge.

Formulas and results

  • Topology axioms: \(\emptyset,X\); arbitrary unions; finite intersections.
  • Discrete: \(2^{\lvert X\rvert}\) open sets. Indiscrete: 2 open sets.
  • Topologies on an \(n\)-set (labelled): \(1,4,29,355,6942\) for \(n=1,\dots,5\). Up to homeomorphism: \(1,3,9,33,139\).
  • Basis test: covers \(X\), and a point in \(B_1\cap B_2\) has a basic set inside \(B_1\cap B_2\).
  • \(\overline A=A\cup A'\); \(A^\circ=X\setminus\overline{X\setminus A}\); \(\partial A=\overline A\setminus A^\circ\).
  • \(\overline{A\cup B}=\overline A\cup\overline B\); \((A\cap B)^\circ=A^\circ\cap B^\circ\); the other two are inclusions only.
  • Subspace: \(\tau_Y=\{U\cap Y\}\); closed sets are \(C\cap Y\).
  • \(f\) continuous \(\iff\) preimage of open (closed, basic) is open (closed, open) \(\iff f(\overline A)\subseteq\overline{f(A)}\).
  • Pasting lemma for finite closed (or any open) covers.
  • Homeomorphism = continuous bijection with continuous inverse. Compact to Hausdorff: continuous bijection is a homeomorphism.
  • \(\mathbb R_\ell\): basis \([a,b)\), strictly finer than usual, \(f:\mathbb R_\ell\to\mathbb R\) is continuous iff right-continuous.
  • Finer topology: more open sets, fewer convergent sequences.

Worked examples

Example 1. Which family is a topology? (2025-Q95 style)

On \(X=\{a,b,c\}\) decide which of these are topologies.

  1. \(\{\emptyset,X,\{a\},\{b,c\}\}\)
  2. \(\{\emptyset,X,\{a,b\},\{b,c\}\}\)
  3. \(\{\emptyset,X,\{a,b\},\{b,c\},\{b\}\}\)
  4. \(\{\emptyset,X,\{a\},\{a,b\}\}\)

Check each for pairwise unions and intersections.

  • (1) \(\{a\}\cup\{b,c\}=X\), \(\{a\}\cap\{b,c\}=\emptyset\). Topology.
  • (2) \(\{a,b\}\cap\{b,c\}=\{b\}\) is missing. Not a topology.
  • (3) the missing \(\{b\}\) is now present; \(\{b\}\cup\{a,b\}=\{a,b\}\), \(\{b\}\cup\{b,c\}=\{b,c\}\). Topology.
  • (4) a chain \(\emptyset\subset\{a\}\subset\{a,b\}\subset X\); any chain of sets containing \(\emptyset,X\) is a topology.

Example 2. Closure, interior, boundary and derived set in \(\mathbb R\)

Let \(A=(0,1]\cup\{2\}\cup(\mathbb Q\cap(3,4))\).

  • Closure: \([0,1]\cup\{2\}\cup[3,4]\) (rationals are dense in \((3,4)\)).
  • Interior: \((0,1)\). The point \(1\) is not interior; \(2\) is isolated; the rationals have empty interior.
  • Boundary: closure minus interior \(=\{0,1,2\}\cup[3,4]\).
  • Derived set: \([0,1]\cup[3,4]\). The point \(2\) is isolated, so it is not a limit point.

Example 3. Closure in the lower limit topology

Find \(\overline{(a,b)}\) in \(\mathbb R_\ell\). A point \(x\) is in the closure iff every \([x,x+\varepsilon)\) meets \((a,b)\).

  • \(x=a\): \([a,a+\varepsilon)\) meets \((a,b)\). Yes.
  • \(x=b\): \([b,b+\varepsilon)\) misses \((a,b)\). No.
  • \(x<a\) or \(x>b\): small \(\varepsilon\) misses. No.

So \(\overline{(a,b)}=[a,b)\), which is also open: \([a,b)\) is clopen in \(\mathbb R_\ell\), and \(\partial(a,b)=\{a\}\).

Example 4. \(\mathbb R_\ell\) is strictly finer than the usual topology

Use the comparison lemma. For \(x\in(a,b)\) choose \(\varepsilon=b-x\); then \([x,x+\varepsilon)\subseteq(a,b)\), so every usual-open set is \(\mathbb R_\ell\)-open. Conversely \([0,1)\) is \(\mathbb R_\ell\)-open but a usual-open set containing \(0\) contains negative numbers, so \([0,1)\) is not usual-open. Hence the inclusion is strict.

Example 5. Showing two spaces are not homeomorphic

Show \((0,1)\not\cong[0,1)\). Suppose \(h:[0,1)\to(0,1)\) is a homeomorphism. Remove \(0\): \(h\) restricts to a homeomorphism \((0,1)\to(0,1)\setminus\{h(0)\}\). The left side is connected, the right side is disconnected (it splits into \((0,h(0))\) and \((h(0),1)\)). Contradiction.

Example 6. A continuous map out of a cofinite space

Show that every continuous \(f:(\mathbb R,\text{cofinite})\to(\mathbb R,\text{usual})\) is constant. If \(f(x)\ne f(y)\), choose disjoint open intervals \(V\ni f(x)\), \(W\ni f(y)\). Then \(f^{-1}(V)\) and \(f^{-1}(W)\) are disjoint, nonempty (they contain \(x\) and \(y\)) and open in the cofinite topology. But two nonempty cofinite sets always intersect on an infinite set. Contradiction.

Common traps

  • Infinite intersections. Open sets are closed only under finite intersections: \(\bigcap_n(-\tfrac1n,\tfrac1n)=\{0\}\) is not open in \(\mathbb R\).
  • “Not open” does not mean closed. \([0,1)\) in \(\mathbb R\) is neither; in \(\mathbb R_\ell\) it is both.
  • Inclusion for closure of intersection. \(\overline{A\cap B}=\overline A\cap\overline B\) is false; the equality holds for unions only. Dually for interiors.
  • Union of topologies. The union of two topologies is not a topology in general; the intersection always is.
  • Open in a subspace. \((\tfrac12,1]\) is open in \([0,1]\) but not in \(\mathbb R\). Examiners often ask about “open in \(Y\)” versus “open in \(X\)”.
  • Continuous bijection. A continuous bijection is not automatically a homeomorphism (the circle map from \([0,2\pi)\); the identity from discrete to usual).
  • Direction of a finer topology. The identity from a finer to a coarser topology is continuous, not the other way round. Cofinite is coarser than usual on \(\mathbb R\).
  • Cofinite is not Hausdorff (infinite set), yet it is \(T_1\) and compact.
  • Discrete topology on an infinite set is Hausdorff, metrizable, not compact, not connected. It is \(T_1\) (every point is closed).
  • Limit point versus closure point. A point of \(A\) may fail to be a limit point of \(A\) (isolated point). In a discrete space \(A'=\emptyset\).
  • Open map versus closed map. The projection \(\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) is open, not closed.
  • Basis condition. A cover by sets is not enough: the intersection condition must also hold.

Previous-year questions

2025-Q95 (official key B)

Let \(X=\{a,b,c\}\). Which of the following is not a topology on \(X\)?

(A) \(\{\emptyset,X,\{a\},\{b,c\}\}\) (B) \(\{\emptyset,X,\{a,b\},\{b,c\}\}\) (C) \(\{\emptyset,X,\{a,b\},\{b,c\},\{b\}\}\) (D) \(\{\emptyset,X,\{a\},\{a,b\}\}\)

Answer: B. \(\{a,b\}\cap\{b,c\}=\{b\}\) is not in the family, so it is not closed under finite intersection. In (C) the set \(\{b\}\) has been added, which repairs this. See Example 1.

2019 (unnumbered; reconstructed from a solution video, option letters may differ)

Let \(X\) be an infinite set with the discrete topology. Which of these is true? (a) \(X\) is connected (b) \(X\) is compact (c) every function from \(X\) to any topological space is continuous (d) \(X\) is not \(T_1\).

Answer: (c). Every subset of \(X\) is open, so for any \(f:X\to Y\) the preimage of every set is open. (a) is false (a point is a nonempty proper clopen set); (b) is false (the cover by singletons has no finite subcover as \(X\) is infinite); (d) is false (points are closed, so \(X\) is \(T_1\), indeed metrizable).

  • Topology: \(\emptyset\), \(X\), arbitrary unions, finite intersections.
  • Counts of topologies on \(1,2,3,4\) points: \(1,4,29,355\); up to homeomorphism \(1,3,9,33\).
  • Discrete: finest, every map out is continuous. Indiscrete: coarsest, every map in is continuous.
  • Cofinite on infinite set: \(T_1\), not Hausdorff, compact, connected.
  • \(\mathbb R_\ell\) (basis \([a,b)\)) is strictly finer than the usual topology; \([a,b)\) is clopen there.
  • \(\overline A=A\cup A'\); \(\partial A=\overline A\setminus A^\circ\); closure commutes with finite unions, interior with finite intersections.
  • Subspace topology: open sets \(U\cap Y\); “open in \(Y\)” is not “open in \(X\)”.
  • \(f\) continuous iff preimages of open (equivalently closed) sets are open (closed).
  • Homeomorphism = continuous bijection with continuous inverse; \([0,2\pi)\to S^1\) is a continuous bijection but not a homeomorphism.
  • Non-homeomorphic by removing a point: \(\mathbb R\not\cong\mathbb R^2\), \(S^1\not\cong[0,1]\), \((0,1)\not\cong[0,1)\).
  • Basis test: cover plus “inside the intersection” condition.
