Why it matters for the exam

Space curves are the most reliably asked part of differential geometry: five of the nine differential-geometry questions in the 2025 and 2026 papers are about curves (helix torsion, “constant curvature and torsion”, curvature of \((t,t^2,t^3)\), a curve with \(\tau=0\)). Almost every question is one formula applied to a standard curve, or one theorem about helices. Learn the formulas for \(\kappa\) and \(\tau\) in a general parameter and the circular-helix values cold.

Core ideas

Regular curves and arc length

A parametrised curve is \(\gamma:I\to\mathbb R^3\), smooth. It is regular if \(\gamma'(t)\ne 0\) for all \(t\). The arc length from \(t_0\) is

\[s(t)=\int_{t_0}^{t}\lVert\gamma'(u)\rVert\,du,\qquad \frac{ds}{dt}=\lVert\gamma'(t)\rVert .\]

A regular curve can always be reparametrised by \(s\); then \(\lVert\dot\gamma\rVert=1\) (a dot means \(d/ds\)). This is the unit-speed or natural parametrisation. Arc length does not depend on the parametrisation.

Examples: the circular helix \((a\cos t,a\sin t,bt)\) has speed \(\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\), so one turn has length \(2\pi\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\). For the curve \((t,t^2,t^3)\) the speed is \(\sqrt{1+4t^2+9t^4}\).

The Frenet triad

For a unit-speed curve:

Vector Definition Name
\(T\) \(\dot\gamma\) unit tangent
\(N\) \(\dot T/\kappa\) (needs \(\kappa\ne0\)) principal normal
\(B\) \(T\times N\) binormal

\(T,N,B\) is a right-handed orthonormal frame at each point. The curvature is \(\kappa=\lVert\dot T\rVert=\lVert\ddot\gamma\rVert\ge0\). It measures how fast the tangent turns. The torsion \(\tau\) is defined by \(\dot B=-\tau N\), equivalently \(\tau=\dot N\cdot B\). It measures how fast the curve leaves its osculating plane. Curvature is never negative; torsion has a sign (positive for a right-handed helix).

Sign convention. Most Indian textbooks (and the 2025 paper) use \(\dot B=-\tau N\), which gives \(\tau=b/(a^2+b^2)>0\) for the helix \((a\cos t,a\sin t,bt)\), \(b>0\). Some books (do Carmo) use \(\dot B=\tau N\) and get the opposite sign. If an option shows a negative torsion for a right-handed helix, the book used the other convention; in this exam use the first.

Serret-Frenet formulas

\[\dot T=\kappa N,\qquad \dot N=-\kappa T+\tau B,\qquad \dot B=-\tau N .\]

In matrix form the coefficient matrix is skew-symmetric, which is why the frame stays orthonormal. The Darboux vector \(\omega=\tau T+\kappa B\) gives all three at once: \(\dot T=\omega\times T\), \(\dot N=\omega\times N\), \(\dot B=\omega\times B\). Proof sketch: \(T\cdot N=0\) and \(\lVert N\rVert=1\) give \(\dot N\perp N\), so \(\dot N=aT+cB\); differentiating \(N\cdot T=0\) gives \(a=-\kappa\); define \(\tau=c\).

Formulas in an arbitrary parameter

If \(\gamma(t)\) is regular but not unit speed, write primes for \(d/dt\):

\[\kappa=\frac{\lVert\gamma'\times\gamma''\rVert}{\lVert\gamma'\rVert^{3}},\qquad \tau=\frac{(\gamma'\times\gamma'')\cdot\gamma'''}{\lVert\gamma'\times\gamma''\rVert^{2}}=\frac{[\gamma',\gamma'',\gamma''']}{\lVert\gamma'\times\gamma''\rVert^{2}}.\]

The frame is \(T=\gamma'/\lVert\gamma'\rVert\), \(B=\gamma'\times\gamma''/\lVert\gamma'\times\gamma''\rVert\), \(N=B\times T\). Also \(\kappa=0\) at a point exactly when \(\gamma'\parallel\gamma''\) there, and then \(N,\tau\) are undefined.

For a plane curve \(y=f(x)\) the same formula gives

\[\kappa=\frac{\lvert f''\rvert}{(1+f'^2)^{3/2}},\]

for a parametric plane curve \(\kappa=\dfrac{\lvert x'y''-y'x''\rvert}{(x'^2+y'^2)^{3/2}}\), and in polar form \(r=r(\theta)\)

\[\kappa=\frac{\lvert r^2+2r'^2-rr''\rvert}{(r^2+r'^2)^{3/2}}.\]

The radius of curvature is \(\rho=1/\kappa\). For an ellipse \(x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2=1\) the radius of curvature is \(b^2/a\) at \((\pm a,0)\) and \(a^2/b\) at \((0,\pm b)\).

Planes attached to a curve

At a point \(P=\gamma(t_0)\) with position vector \(\mathbf r\) of a general point:

Plane Spanned by Normal vector Equation
Osculating \(T,N\) \(B\) \([\mathbf r-\gamma,\gamma',\gamma'']=0\)
Normal \(N,B\) \(T\) \((\mathbf r-\gamma)\cdot\gamma'=0\)
Rectifying \(T,B\) \(N\) \((\mathbf r-\gamma)\cdot N=0\)

The osculating plane is the limit of the plane through three nearby points of the curve. The curve crosses its osculating plane at a point where \(\tau\ne0\).

Curves with special curvature

  • \(\kappa\equiv0\) if and only if the curve is a straight line (or part of one).
  • If \(\kappa\ne0\): \(\tau\equiv0\) if and only if the curve is planar. Proof of “\(\Rightarrow\)”: \(\tau=0\) gives \(\dot B=0\), so \(B\) is constant; then \(\frac{d}{ds}(\gamma\cdot B)=T\cdot B=0\), so \(\gamma\cdot B\) is constant, which is a plane. The converse is clear because \(B\) is the normal of that plane.
  • A plane curve has \(\tau=0\) and its curvature can be given a sign. A plane curve with constant \(\kappa>0\) is a circle of radius \(1/\kappa\).
  • Fundamental theorem. Given smooth \(\kappa(s)>0\) and \(\tau(s)\) there is a unit-speed curve with these curvature and torsion, unique up to a rigid motion of \(\mathbb R^3\). So \(\kappa,\tau\) determine the curve’s shape completely.
  • Curvature and torsion do not change under rigid motions; a reflection changes the sign of \(\tau\).

Helices

A general (cylindrical) helix is a curve whose tangent makes a constant angle with a fixed direction.

Lancret’s theorem. A curve with \(\kappa\ne 0\) is a general helix if and only if \(\tau/\kappa\) is constant. If the angle with the axis is \(\theta\), then \(\tau/\kappa=\cot\theta\). Plane curves (\(\tau=0\)) are the degenerate case \(\theta=90^\circ\).

Idea of proof: if \(T\cdot u=\cos\theta\) for a fixed unit vector \(u\), differentiate: \(\kappa N\cdot u=0\), so \(u\in\operatorname{span}(T,B)\), \(u=\cos\theta\,T+\sin\theta\,B\). Differentiating \(N\cdot u=0\) gives \(-\kappa\cos\theta+\tau\sin\theta=0\), i.e. \(\tau/\kappa=\cot\theta\). The converse takes \(u=\cos\theta\,T+\sin\theta\,B\) and shows it is constant.

The circular helix \(\gamma(t)=(a\cos t,a\sin t,bt)\), \(a>0\), \(b\ne0\):

\[\kappa=\frac{a}{a^2+b^2},\qquad \tau=\frac{b}{a^2+b^2},\qquad \frac{\tau}{\kappa}=\frac ba .\]

Its axis is the \(z\)-axis, the tangent makes the angle \(\theta\) with it where \(\tan\theta=a/b\), and it lies on the cylinder of radius \(a\).

In terms of the helix angle. Let \(\alpha\) be the inclination of the tangent to the plane of the base circle, so \(\tan\alpha=b/a\) and \(\alpha=90^\circ-\theta\). Then

\[\kappa=\frac{\cos^2\alpha}{a},\qquad \tau=\frac{\sin\alpha\cos\alpha}{a},\qquad \rho=\frac1\kappa=a\sec^2\alpha,\qquad \frac\tau\kappa=\tan\alpha .\]

So the radius of curvature of a circular helix of radius \(a\) and helix angle \(\alpha\) is \(a\sec^2\alpha\), which is larger than the radius \(a\) of the cylinder; the principal normal points along the inward radius of the cylinder, so \(N=(-\cos t,-\sin t,0)\) and the centre of curvature \(\gamma+\rho N\) lies on a coaxial cylinder of radius \(a\tan^2\alpha\) (the evolute is again a helix). In arc length, \(\gamma(s)=\bigl(a\cos(s/c),a\sin(s/c),bs/c\bigr)\) with \(c=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\).

Other characterisations of a general helix. (i) The tangent indicatrix \(T(s)\) is a circle on the unit sphere; (ii) the principal normal is always perpendicular to the fixed axis \(u\); (iii) the binormal also makes a constant angle with \(u\); (iv) the curve is a geodesic on a cylinder (it cuts the generators at a constant angle). Example: if a curve has \(\tau/\kappa=\sqrt3\), then \(\cot\theta=\sqrt3\), so the tangent makes \(30^\circ\) with the axis.

Theorem. A curve with \(\kappa\) and \(\tau\) both constant is a circular helix, provided \(\tau\ne0\) and \(\kappa>0\). (If \(\tau=0\) it is a circle; if \(\kappa=0\) it is a line.) Reason: constant \(\kappa,\tau\) means \(\tau/\kappa\) constant, so a general helix, and the fundamental theorem says the curve with these constants is the helix with \(a=\kappa/(\kappa^2+\tau^2)\), \(b=\tau/(\kappa^2+\tau^2)\).

The converse is false: a general helix need not have constant \(\kappa,\tau\). For example \(\gamma(t)=(3t-t^3,\,3t^2,\,3t+t^3)\) has \(\kappa=\tau=\dfrac{1}{3(1+t^2)^2}\), so \(\tau/\kappa=1\) (tangent at \(45^\circ\) to the \(z\)-axis), but neither is constant. The hierarchy is: circular helix \(\subset\) general helix, and the question stem “constant curvature and torsion” means circular helix, not just “a helix”.

Osculating circle and osculating sphere

The osculating circle at \(P\) lies in the osculating plane, has radius \(\rho=1/\kappa\) and centre (the centre of curvature) \(C=\gamma+\rho N\). It has second-order contact with the curve.

The osculating sphere has centre and radius

\[C=\gamma+\rho N+\frac{\dot\rho}{\tau}B,\qquad R^2=\rho^2+\Big(\frac{\dot\rho}{\tau}\Big)^2,\]

where \(\dot\rho=d\rho/ds\) (needs \(\tau\ne0\)). Differentiating \(C\) gives \(\dot C=\big(\rho\tau+\tfrac{d}{ds}(\dot\rho/\tau)\big)B\), so the osculating sphere has a fixed centre, i.e. the curve lies on a sphere, exactly when \(\rho\tau+\dfrac{d}{ds}\Big(\dfrac{\dot\rho}{\tau}\Big)=0\). Then \(R^2=\rho^2+(\dot\rho/\tau)^2\) is the constant squared radius. Caution: \(R^2\) being constant is not enough (a circular helix has \(\dot\rho=0\) and constant \(R^2=\rho^2\), but it is not spherical).

Involutes and evolutes

The involute of a unit-speed curve \(\gamma\) is obtained by unwinding a taut string:

\[\bar\gamma(s)=\gamma(s)+(c-s)\,T(s),\qquad c\ \text{constant}.\]

Then \(\dot{\bar\gamma}=(c-s)\kappa N\), so the tangent of the involute is \(N\): the involute cuts all tangent lines of \(\gamma\) at right angles. Different \(c\) give a family of parallel involutes. The curvature of the involute is

\[\bar\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa^2+\tau^2}}{\kappa\,\lvert c-s\rvert},\]

which for a plane curve reduces to \(1/\lvert c-s\rvert\) (the centre of curvature of the involute is the point of \(\gamma\)). Example: the involute of the circle of radius \(a\) is \(a(\cos t+t\sin t,\ \sin t-t\cos t)\) with curvature \(1/(at)\).

An evolute of \(\gamma\) is a curve whose tangent lines are the normal lines of \(\gamma\) (so \(\gamma\) is an involute of its evolutes). For a plane curve the evolute is the locus of centres of curvature \(\gamma+\rho N\). In space the evolutes are

\[\Gamma(s)=\gamma+\rho N+\rho\cot\!\Big(\int\tau\,ds+c\Big)B,\]

one for each constant \(c\); for a plane curve \(\tau=0\) and the cotangent factor is constant, and the plane evolute is \(c=\pi/2\). Examples: the evolute of the parabola \(y^2=4ax\) is the semicubical parabola \(27ay^2=4(x-2a)^3\); the evolute of a circle is its centre; the evolute of a cycloid is an equal cycloid.

Angles of contingence and torsion; spherical indicatrices

For a unit-speed curve the frame turns by small angles over an arc \(ds\):

  • angle of contingence (between consecutive tangents): \(d\varphi=\kappa\,ds\);
  • angle of torsion (between consecutive binormals): \(d\psi=\lvert\tau\rvert\,ds\);
  • angle between consecutive principal normals: \(d\chi=\sqrt{\kappa^2+\tau^2}\,ds\), because \(\dot N=-\kappa T+\tau B\) has length \(\sqrt{\kappa^2+\tau^2}\). This is also the magnitude of the Darboux vector \(\omega=\tau T+\kappa B\).

So \(\kappa=d\varphi/ds\), \(\lvert\tau\rvert=d\psi/ds\), and the three angles satisfy \(d\chi^2=d\varphi^2+d\psi^2\).

Spherical indicatrices. The curves \(T(s)\), \(N(s)\), \(B(s)\) on the unit sphere are the tangent, principal normal and binormal indicatrices.

Indicatrix Speed (arc length element) Tangent vector of the indicatrix Curvature (as a space curve)
tangent \(T(s)\) \(\kappa\,ds\) \(N\) \(\dfrac{\sqrt{\kappa^2+\tau^2}}{\kappa}\)
principal normal \(N(s)\) \(\sqrt{\kappa^2+\tau^2}\,ds\) \(\dfrac{-\kappa T+\tau B}{\sqrt{\kappa^2+\tau^2}}\) not needed for exams
binormal \(B(s)\) \(\lvert\tau\rvert\,ds\) \(-\operatorname{sgn}(\tau)N\) \(\dfrac{\sqrt{\kappa^2+\tau^2}}{\lvert\tau\rvert}\)

For a general helix, \(\tau/\kappa=\cot\theta\) is constant, so the tangent indicatrix has constant curvature \(\sqrt{1+\cot^2\theta}=\csc\theta\), i.e. it is a circle of radius \(\sin\theta\) on the unit sphere. Conversely, a curve whose tangent indicatrix is a circle (a small circle of the sphere) is a general helix. For a plane curve (\(\tau=0\)) the tangent indicatrix runs on a great circle.

Example. For the circular helix with \(\kappa=a/c^2\), \(\tau=b/c^2\) (\(c^2=a^2+b^2\)), the angle between consecutive principal normals is \(\sqrt{\kappa^2+\tau^2}\,ds=ds/c\), and the length of one turn of the tangent indicatrix (a circle of radius \(a/c\)) is \(2\pi a/c\); this agrees with \(\int\kappa\,ds=\kappa\cdot2\pi c=2\pi a/c\).

Other results that appear in exams

  • Bertrand curves. Two curves with the same principal normals at corresponding points. A curve has a Bertrand mate if and only if \(a\kappa+b\tau=1\) for constants \(a,b\) with \(a\ne0\) – a linear relation between \(\kappa\) and \(\tau\). Circular helices are Bertrand; in fact they have infinitely many mates.
  • Curves on a sphere of radius \(R\) have \(\kappa\ge1/R\).
  • The tangent indicatrix is the curve \(T(s)\) on the unit sphere; \(\lVert\dot T\rVert=\kappa\) is its speed. The binormal indicatrix has speed \(\lvert\tau\rvert\) and the principal normal indicatrix speed \(\sqrt{\kappa^2+\tau^2}\) (see the previous subsection).

Formulas and results

Frenet

  • \(\dot T=\kappa N\), \(\dot N=-\kappa T+\tau B\), \(\dot B=-\tau N\); \(B=T\times N\), \(N=B\times T\), \(T=N\times B\).
  • \(\kappa=\lVert\ddot\gamma\rVert\) for unit speed; \(\tau=\dot N\cdot B=\dfrac{[\dot\gamma,\ddot\gamma,\dddot\gamma]}{\kappa^2}\).
  • Any parameter: \(\kappa=\dfrac{\lVert\gamma'\times\gamma''\rVert}{\lVert\gamma'\rVert^3}\), \(\tau=\dfrac{[\gamma',\gamma'',\gamma''']}{\lVert\gamma'\times\gamma''\rVert^2}\).
  • Plane curves: \(\kappa=\dfrac{\lvert f''\rvert}{(1+f'^2)^{3/2}}\); \(\kappa=\dfrac{\lvert x'y''-y'x''\rvert}{(x'^2+y'^2)^{3/2}}\); polar \(\dfrac{\lvert r^2+2r'^2-rr''\rvert}{(r^2+r'^2)^{3/2}}\).

Standard curves

Curve \(\kappa\) \(\tau\)
Line \(0\) undefined
Circle of radius \(r\) \(1/r\) \(0\)
Helix \((a\cos t,a\sin t,bt)\) \(a/(a^2+b^2)\) \(b/(a^2+b^2)\)
Twisted cubic \((t,t^2,t^3)\) \(\dfrac{2\sqrt{1+9t^2+9t^4}}{(1+4t^2+9t^4)^{3/2}}\) \(\dfrac{3}{1+9t^2+9t^4}\)
\((3t-t^3,3t^2,3t+t^3)\) \(\dfrac{1}{3(1+t^2)^2}\) \(\dfrac{1}{3(1+t^2)^2}\)

At \(t=0\) the twisted cubic has \(\kappa=2\), \(\tau=3\).

Theorems

  • \(\tau\equiv0\) (\(\kappa\ne0\)) \(\iff\) planar. \(\kappa\equiv0\iff\) line.
  • Lancret: general helix \(\iff\tau/\kappa\) constant (\(=\cot\theta\)).
  • \(\kappa,\tau\) both constant and \(\tau\ne0\) \(\iff\) circular helix; \(a=\kappa/(\kappa^2+\tau^2)\), \(b=\tau/(\kappa^2+\tau^2)\).
  • Fundamental theorem: \(\kappa(s)>0,\tau(s)\) determine the curve up to rigid motion.
  • Osculating circle: radius \(1/\kappa\), centre \(\gamma+\rho N\). Osculating sphere: centre \(\gamma+\rho N+(\dot\rho/\tau)B\).
  • Involute: \(\gamma+(c-s)T\); evolute (plane): \(\gamma+\rho N\).
  • Circular helix with helix angle \(\alpha\) (tangent to base plane), radius \(a\): \(\kappa=\cos^2\alpha/a\), \(\tau=\sin\alpha\cos\alpha/a\), \(\rho=a\sec^2\alpha\), \(\tau/\kappa=\tan\alpha\).
  • Angle of contingence \(d\varphi=\kappa\,ds\); angle of torsion \(d\psi=\lvert\tau\rvert\,ds\); angle between consecutive principal normals \(\sqrt{\kappa^2+\tau^2}\,ds\). Indicatrix speeds: \(T\): \(\kappa\); \(N\): \(\sqrt{\kappa^2+\tau^2}\); \(B\): \(\lvert\tau\rvert\).
  • General helix \(\iff\) tangent indicatrix is a small circle; \(N\perp\) axis; \(B\) at a constant angle to the axis.

Worked examples

Example 1: curvature and torsion of a helix

Find \(\kappa,\tau\) for \(\gamma(t)=(3\cos t,3\sin t,4t)\).

\(\gamma'=(-3\sin t,3\cos t,4)\), \(\gamma''=(-3\cos t,-3\sin t,0)\), \(\gamma'''=(3\sin t,-3\cos t,0)\). \(\gamma'\times\gamma''=(12\sin t,-12\cos t,9)\), so \(\lVert\gamma'\times\gamma''\rVert=\sqrt{144+81}=15\) and \(\lVert\gamma'\rVert=5\). \(\kappa=15/125=3/25\). \((\gamma'\times\gamma'')\cdot\gamma'''=36\sin^2t+36\cos^2t=36\), so \(\tau=36/225=4/25\). This agrees with \(a/(a^2+b^2)=3/25\) and \(b/(a^2+b^2)=4/25\).

Example 2: twisted cubic

For \(\gamma=(t,t^2,t^3)\): \(\gamma'=(1,2t,3t^2)\), \(\gamma''=(0,2,6t)\), \(\gamma'''=(0,0,6)\). \(\gamma'\times\gamma''=(6t^2,-6t,2)\), norm squared \(=36t^4+36t^2+4=4(9t^4+9t^2+1)\). \([\gamma',\gamma'',\gamma''']=12\), so \(\tau=\dfrac{12}{4(9t^4+9t^2+1)}=\dfrac{3}{1+9t^2+9t^4}\). At \(t=1\): \(\tau=3/19\), \(\kappa=\dfrac{2\sqrt{19}}{14^{3/2}}\). At \(t=0\): \(\gamma'=(1,0,0)\), \(\gamma''=(0,2,0)\), so \(\kappa=2/1=2\) and \(\tau=3\).

Example 3: osculating plane

Osculating plane of the twisted cubic at \(t=1\), point \((1,1,1)\). The binormal direction is \(\gamma'\times\gamma''=(6,-6,2)\parallel(3,-3,1)\). The plane is \(3(x-1)-3(y-1)+(z-1)=0\), i.e. \(3x-3y+z=1\).

Example 4: a general helix and its angle

Show \(\gamma(t)=(3t-t^3,3t^2,3t+t^3)\) is a general helix and find the angle with its axis. \(\gamma'=(3-3t^2,6t,3+3t^2)\), \(\lVert\gamma'\rVert^2=9\,(2+2t^4+4t^2)=18(1+t^2)^2\), so \(\lVert\gamma'\rVert=3\sqrt2(1+t^2)\). Then \(T\cdot(0,0,1)=\dfrac{3(1+t^2)}{3\sqrt2(1+t^2)}=\dfrac1{\sqrt2}\) is constant, so the axis is the \(z\)-axis and \(\theta=45^\circ\). Consistently \(\tau/\kappa=\cot45^\circ=1\).

Example 5: curvature of a plane curve

Radius of curvature of the ellipse \(x^2/9+y^2/4=1\) at \((3,0)\). With \(x=3\cos t\), \(y=2\sin t\) at \(t=0\): \(x'=0\), \(y'=2\), \(x''=-3\), \(y''=0\). \(\kappa=\lvert 0\cdot0-2\cdot(-3)\rvert/(0+4)^{3/2}=6/8=3/4\). So \(\rho=b^2/a=4/3\).

Example 6: constants to helix

A curve has constant \(\kappa=3\) and \(\tau=4\). Then it is a circular helix with \(a=3/25\), \(b=4/25\) (radius of its cylinder \(3/25\)), pitch \(2\pi b=8\pi/25\), and \(\tau/\kappa=4/3=b/a\).

Common traps

  • “Constant \(\kappa\) and \(\tau\)” gives a circular helix, not a circle. A circle needs \(\tau=0\). Examiners use circle, line, helix, ellipse as options.
  • “Helix” vs “circular helix”. A general (cylindrical) helix only needs \(\tau/\kappa\) constant. In 2025-Q100 the correct option is “circular helix”, while “a helix” and “a cylindrical helix” are look-alikes.
  • Torsion formula mix-ups. The helix has \(\kappa=a/(a^2+b^2)\) and \(\tau=b/(a^2+b^2)\): both have denominator \(a^2+b^2\), not \((a\pm b)^2\) and not \(a^2-b^2\). Note \(\kappa\) has \(a\) upstairs, \(\tau\) has \(b\).
  • Wrong cross-product order. \(\tau\) uses \((\gamma'\times\gamma'')\cdot\gamma'''\), with the triple product in order; the denominator is \(\lVert\gamma'\times\gamma''\rVert^2\), not the cube and not \(\lVert\gamma'\rVert^3\).
  • Torsion of a plane curve is \(0\), and torsion of a line is not defined (not “\(0\) or infinity”).
  • Curvature is non-negative. If the answer is \(-2\) you used a signed plane curvature.
  • Unit speed. \(\kappa=\lVert\ddot\gamma\rVert\) only for the arc-length parameter. In any other parameter \(\gamma''\) also has a tangential part.
  • \(\tau=0\) implies planar only when \(\kappa\ne0\). Without \(\kappa\ne0\) the frame does not exist.
  • A curve with \(\kappa=1\), \(\tau=0\) is a unit circle; it needs a plane (\(\mathbb R^2\)) to sit in, though the circle itself is a \(1\)-dimensional manifold.
  • Which angle? For the helix angle \(\alpha\) measured from the base plane, \(\tau/\kappa=\tan\alpha\) and \(\rho=a\sec^2\alpha\); measured from the axis (\(\theta\)), \(\tau/\kappa=\cot\theta\). Read which angle the question gives. Also \(\rho=a\sec^2\alpha\ne a\): the radius of curvature of a helix is not the radius of its cylinder.
  • Consecutive-normal angle is not \(\kappa\,ds\). The angle between consecutive tangents is \(\kappa\,ds\), between consecutive binormals \(\lvert\tau\rvert\,ds\), and between consecutive principal normals \(\sqrt{\kappa^2+\tau^2}\,ds\).
  • Frenet formulas: \(\dot N=-\kappa T+\tau B\) (sign on \(\kappa T\) is negative); \(\dot B=-\tau N\) (minus). Options flip these signs.

Previous-year questions

2025-Q98. The torsion of the circular helix \(\mathbf r=(a\cos u,a\sin u,bu)\) is: (A) \(\dfrac{ab}{(a+b)^2}\) (B) \(\dfrac{b}{a^2+b^2}\) (C) \(\dfrac{a}{a^2-b^2}\) (D) \(\dfrac{ab}{(a-b)^2}\). Answer: B. \(\mathbf r'=(-a\sin u,a\cos u,b)\), \(\mathbf r''=(-a\cos u,-a\sin u,0)\), \(\mathbf r'''=(a\sin u,-a\cos u,0)\). \(\mathbf r'\times\mathbf r''=(ab\sin u,-ab\cos u,a^2)\), norm\(^2=a^2(a^2+b^2)\). Triple product \(=a^2b\). \(\tau=a^2b/(a^2(a^2+b^2))=b/(a^2+b^2)\). (Option letters follow the official key; the printed options are partly garbled in the scan, but the key is \(b/(a^2+b^2)\).)

2025-Q100. If the curvature and torsion of a curve are both constant, the curve is: (A) a cylindrical helix (B) a helix (C) a circular helix (D) none of these. Answer: C. Constant \(\kappa,\tau\) gives \(\tau/\kappa\) constant (a general helix) and the fundamental theorem fixes it as a circular helix (for \(\tau\ne0\), \(\kappa>0\)). A and B are true but weaker; C is the exact name.

2026-Q49. (Worked key, unofficial.) A curve has \(\kappa=1\), \(\tau=0\). Using the geometric interpretation and manifold embedding, the dimension of the minimal ambient manifold containing the curve is: (A) \(1\) (B) \(3\) (C) \(2\) (D) infinite. Answer: C (\(2\)). \(\tau\equiv0\) makes it a plane curve and constant \(\kappa=1\) makes it a unit circle, which sits in a plane, dimension \(2\). Not fully certain: the wording is vague, and if “manifold” meant the circle itself the dimension would be \(1\) (A). The worked key takes the ambient plane.

2026-Q62. (Worked key, unofficial.) For \(\gamma(t)=(t,t^2,t^3)\) the curvature at \(t=0\) is: (A) \(0\) (B) \(1\) (C) \(2\) (D) \(3\). Answer: C. At \(t=0\), \(\gamma'=(1,0,0)\), \(\gamma''=(0,2,0)\), \(\gamma'\times\gamma''=(0,0,2)\), \(\kappa=2/1^3=2\). Option D (\(3\)) is the torsion at \(t=0\).

2026-Q99. (Worked key, unofficial.) A curve with constant non-zero curvature and torsion has the geometric form: (A) circle (B) line (C) helix (D) ellipse (the paper shows options as circle, helix and others; the key is the helix). Answer: C (helix). A circle has \(\tau=0\), a line has \(\kappa=0\), so only the circular helix remains.

Quick revision

  • Unit speed: \(\kappa=\lVert\ddot\gamma\rVert\); general: \(\kappa=\lVert\gamma'\times\gamma''\rVert/\lVert\gamma'\rVert^3\).
  • \(\tau=[\gamma',\gamma'',\gamma''']/\lVert\gamma'\times\gamma''\rVert^2\).
  • \(\dot T=\kappa N\), \(\dot N=-\kappa T+\tau B\), \(\dot B=-\tau N\).
  • Helix \((a\cos t,a\sin t,bt)\): \(\kappa=\frac{a}{a^2+b^2}\), \(\tau=\frac{b}{a^2+b^2}\).
  • Twisted cubic at \(t=0\): \(\kappa=2\), \(\tau=3\).
  • \(\tau=0\) with \(\kappa\ne0\) means a plane curve; \(\kappa=0\) means a line.
  • \(\tau/\kappa\) constant \(=\cot\theta\) means a general helix (Lancret).
  • Constant \(\kappa,\tau\ne0\) means a circular helix; constant \(\kappa\), \(\tau=0\) means a circle.
  • Osculating plane normal is \(B\); the osculating circle has radius \(1/\kappa\) and centre \(\gamma+\rho N\).
  • Involute: \(\gamma+(c-s)T\); evolute of a plane curve is its locus of centres of curvature.
  • \(\kappa\) and \(\tau\) determine a curve up to rigid motion.
  • Helix with angle \(\alpha\) to the base plane: \(\rho=a\sec^2\alpha\), \(\tau/\kappa=\tan\alpha\).
  • Angle of contingence \(\kappa\,ds\); angle of torsion \(\lvert\tau\rvert\,ds\); consecutive principal normals \(\sqrt{\kappa^2+\tau^2}\,ds\).
  • General helix: tangent indicatrix is a circle, \(N\perp\) axis.
