Why it matters for the exam

Unit 10 carries 5 to 7 questions a paper (2025: 5, of which 3 are number theory proper and 2 are Indian-mathematics history; 2026: 7, all number theory). This topic is the base for all of them. Direct questions are rare (2025 Q88 on twin primes is the only one), but the ideas appear inside other questions: prime factorisations, gcd and lcm, trailing zeros of \(n!\), perfect and Mersenne numbers, and statements such as “there are infinitely many primes”. Expect one-line statement questions and short numeric ones.

Core ideas

Divisibility

For integers \(a, b\) with \(a \ne 0\), we write \(a \mid b\) if \(b = ak\) for some integer \(k\).

  • Every nonzero \(a\) divides \(0\). \(1\) and \(-1\) divide everything. \(0 \nmid b\) unless \(b = 0\).
  • Transitive: \(a \mid b\) and \(b \mid c\) give \(a \mid c\).
  • Linear: \(a \mid b\) and \(a \mid c\) give \(a \mid (bx + cy)\) for all integers \(x, y\).
  • If \(a \mid b\) and \(b \ne 0\) then \(\lvert a\rvert \le \lvert b\rvert\).
  • If \(a \mid b\) and \(b \mid a\) then \(a = \pm b\).

Division algorithm

For integers \(a\) and \(b > 0\) there are unique integers \(q, r\) with \(a = bq + r, \qquad 0 \le r < b.\) Uniqueness is what makes remainders well defined. For negative \(a\) the remainder is still taken in \([0, b)\): \(-17 = 5\cdot(-4) + 3\).

gcd and lcm

\(\gcd(a,b)\) is the largest positive integer dividing both. \(\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)\) is the smallest positive common multiple. \(a, b\) are coprime (relatively prime) if \(\gcd(a,b) = 1\).

Key facts:

  • \(\gcd(a,b)\cdot\operatorname{lcm}(a,b) = \lvert ab\rvert\) (for two numbers only; false for three).
  • \(\gcd(a,b) = \gcd(b, r)\) where \(a = bq + r\). This is the engine of the Euclidean algorithm.
  • \(\gcd(ka, kb) = k\gcd(a,b)\) for \(k>0\). If \(d = \gcd(a,b)\) then \(\gcd(a/d, b/d) = 1\).
  • Bezout’s identity. \(\gcd(a,b)\) is the smallest positive integer of the form \(ax + by\). Hence \(a, b\) are coprime iff \(ax + by = 1\) has an integer solution. The set of all values \(ax+by\) is exactly the set of multiples of \(\gcd(a,b)\).
  • Euclid’s lemma. If \(a \mid bc\) and \(\gcd(a,b) = 1\), then \(a \mid c\). In particular, if \(p\) is prime and \(p \mid ab\), then \(p \mid a\) or \(p \mid b\).
  • If \(a \mid c\), \(b \mid c\) and \(\gcd(a,b) = 1\) then \(ab \mid c\).
  • \(\gcd(2^a - 1, 2^b - 1) = 2^{\gcd(a,b)} - 1\). Consecutive Fibonacci numbers are coprime. \(\gcd(n, n+1) = 1\) and \(\gcd(2n+1, n) = 1\).

Through prime factorisations \(a = \prod p_i^{\alpha_i}\), \(b = \prod p_i^{\beta_i}\): \(\gcd(a,b) = \prod p_i^{\min(\alpha_i,\beta_i)}, \qquad \operatorname{lcm}(a,b) = \prod p_i^{\max(\alpha_i,\beta_i)}.\)

Euclidean algorithm

Repeat division with remainder until the remainder is \(0\). The last nonzero remainder is the gcd.

For \(1071\) and \(462\): \(1071 = 2\cdot 462 + 147,\quad 462 = 3\cdot 147 + 21,\quad 147 = 7\cdot 21 + 0.\) So \(\gcd = 21\). Back-substituting gives Bezout coefficients: \(21 = 462 - 3\cdot147 = 462 - 3(1071 - 2\cdot 462) = 7\cdot 462 - 3\cdot 1071\).

The algorithm is slowest on consecutive Fibonacci numbers (Lamé): the number of steps is at most about \(5\) times the number of decimal digits of the smaller number.

Linear Diophantine equation \(ax + by = c\)

  • Solvable in integers iff \(\gcd(a,b) \mid c\).
  • If \((x_0, y_0)\) is one solution and \(d = \gcd(a,b)\), all solutions are \(x = x_0 + \frac{b}{d}\,t, \qquad y = y_0 - \frac{a}{d}\,t, \qquad t \in \mathbb Z.\)
  • To find \((x_0,y_0)\): run the Euclidean algorithm for \(a,b\), get \(ax' + by' = d\), then multiply by \(c/d\).
  • Positive or non-negative solutions: substitute the general solution into \(x > 0\), \(y > 0\) and count the integers \(t\).
  • Frobenius (postage stamp) result: if \(\gcd(a,b) = 1\) the largest \(n\) not expressible as \(ax + by\) with \(x, y \ge 0\) is \(ab - a - b\).
  • For \(ax + by + cz = d\), solvable iff \(\gcd(a,b,c) \mid d\).

Primes and the fundamental theorem of arithmetic

A prime is an integer \(p > 1\) whose only positive divisors are \(1\) and \(p\). \(1\) is not prime. \(2\) is the only even prime.

Fundamental theorem of arithmetic (FTA). Every integer \(n > 1\) can be written as a product of primes, and this factorisation is unique apart from the order of the factors. Equivalently, \(n = p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_k^{a_k}\) with \(p_1 < \dots < p_k\) is unique (canonical form).

Proof idea. Existence: induct on \(n\) (if \(n\) is composite, split it). Uniqueness: if \(p_1\cdots p_r = q_1\cdots q_s\), then \(p_1 \mid q_1\cdots q_s\), so by Euclid’s lemma \(p_1 \mid q_j\) for some \(j\), hence \(p_1 = q_j\). Cancel and repeat. So uniqueness rests on Euclid’s lemma, which rests on Bezout.

Consequences:

  • gcd and lcm from exponents (above), and the divisor formulas of the next topic.
  • A positive integer is a perfect square iff every exponent in its factorisation is even.
  • \(\sqrt 2\) is irrational: \(a^2 = 2b^2\) gives odd exponent of \(2\) on the right and even on the left.
  • Unique factorisation can fail in other rings. In \(\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]\): \(6 = 2\cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})\).

A composite \(n\) has a prime factor \(\le \sqrt n\). This is why the Sieve of Eratosthenes only needs primes up to \(\sqrt n\), and why to test whether \(n\) is prime you only divide by primes up to \(\sqrt n\).

Infinitely many primes

Euclid. If \(p_1,\dots,p_k\) were all the primes, then \(N = p_1\cdots p_k + 1\) has a prime factor, which cannot be any \(p_i\) (it would divide \(1\)). Contradiction. Note: \(N\) itself need not be prime (\(2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13 + 1 = 30031 = 59\cdot 509\)).

Other facts to know at statement level:

  • Infinitely many primes of the form \(4k+3\) and of the form \(6k+5\) (elementary proofs). The same for \(4k+1\) is also true.
  • Dirichlet’s theorem. If \(\gcd(a,m) = 1\), there are infinitely many primes \(\equiv a \pmod m\).
  • \(\sum 1/p\) over all primes diverges (Euler).
  • Arbitrarily large gaps between primes: \(n!+2, n!+3, \dots, n!+n\) are \(n-1\) consecutive composites.
  • Bertrand’s postulate (Chebyshev): for every \(n \ge 1\) there is a prime \(p\) with \(n < p \le 2n\).
  • Twin primes are pairs \((p, p+2)\). That there are infinitely many is unproved. Goldbach (every even number \(> 2\) is a sum of two primes) is unproved.
  • No nonconstant polynomial takes only prime values. Euler’s \(n^2 + n + 41\) is prime for \(n = 0,\dots,39\) but \(40^2+40+41 = 41^2\).

Prime counting function

\(\pi(x)\) is the number of primes \(\le x\). Values: \(\pi(10) = 4\), \(\pi(100) = 25\), \(\pi(1000) = 168\).

Prime number theorem (PNT). \(\displaystyle \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\pi(x)}{x/\ln x} = 1\), written \(\pi(x) \sim x/\ln x\). Proved independently by Hadamard and de la Vallee Poussin (1896). Chebyshev earlier proved bounds of the form \(c_1 x/\ln x \le \pi(x) \le c_2 x/\ln x\). The \(n\)-th prime satisfies \(p_n \sim n\ln n\).

Perfect numbers

\(n\) is perfect if \(\sigma(n) = 2n\), that is, \(n\) equals the sum of its proper divisors. The first four: \(6, 28, 496, 8128\) (the fifth is \(33550336\)). \(n\) is abundant if \(\sigma(n) > 2n\) and deficient if \(\sigma(n) < 2n\).

Euclid-Euler theorem. An even number is perfect iff it has the form \(n = 2^{p-1}(2^p - 1) \quad\text{with } 2^p - 1 \text{ prime}.\) Euclid proved that such \(n\) are perfect. Euler proved that every even perfect number is of this form. Whether an odd perfect number exists is open.

Proof of the easy direction: with \(M = 2^p - 1\) prime, \(\sigma(2^{p-1}M) = \sigma(2^{p-1})\sigma(M) = (2^p - 1)(M+1) = (2^p-1)2^p = 2n\).

Every even perfect number is a triangular number and ends in \(6\) or \(28\).

Mersenne numbers

\(M_n = 2^n - 1\).

  • If \(M_n\) is prime then \(n\) is prime (because \(2^{ab}-1\) is divisible by \(2^a - 1\)). The converse is false: \(M_{11} = 2047 = 23\cdot 89\).
  • \(M_p\) is prime for \(p = 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 19, 31, 61, 89, 107, 127\) (and others, larger). Known Mersenne primes number only about fifty; it is unknown whether there are infinitely many.
  • Lucas-Lehmer test for odd prime \(p\): put \(s_0 = 4\), \(s_{k+1} = s_k^2 - 2 \pmod{M_p}\). Then \(M_p\) is prime iff \(s_{p-2} \equiv 0 \pmod{M_p}\).
  • Any prime divisor \(q\) of \(M_p\) (\(p\) an odd prime) has the form \(q = 2kp + 1\) and \(q \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8\).

Mersenne primes and even perfect numbers are in one-to-one correspondence.

Fermat numbers

\(F_n = 2^{2^n} + 1\).

  • \(F_0, \dots, F_4 = 3, 5, 17, 257, 65537\) are prime. Fermat conjectured all are prime. Euler showed \(F_5 = 2^{32}+1 = 641\cdot 6700417\). No other Fermat primes are known.
  • If \(2^m + 1\) is prime then \(m\) is a power of \(2\). (Compare: \(2^n - 1\) prime forces \(n\) prime.)
  • Recurrence: \(F_n = F_0F_1\cdots F_{n-1} + 2\).
  • Distinct Fermat numbers are coprime (\(\gcd(F_m, F_n) = 1\), \(m \ne n\)). This gives another proof that there are infinitely many primes.
  • Gauss-Wantzel. A regular \(n\)-gon is constructible by ruler and compass iff \(n = 2^k p_1\cdots p_r\) with the \(p_i\) distinct Fermat primes. So the 17-gon is constructible; the 7-gon is not.

Legendre’s formula and trailing zeros of \(n!\)

The exponent of a prime \(p\) in \(n!\) is \(v_p(n!) = \sum_{k\ge 1}\left\lfloor \frac{n}{p^k}\right\rfloor = \frac{n - s_p(n)}{p-1},\) where \(s_p(n)\) is the digit sum of \(n\) in base \(p\). The sum is finite: terms vanish once \(p^k > n\).

Trailing zeros of \(n!\) in base \(10\): the number of \(10\)’s is \(\min(v_2, v_5) = v_5(n!)\), since there are always more factors of \(2\). So \(Z(n) = \left\lfloor \frac n5\right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac n{25}\right\rfloor + \left\lfloor \frac n{125}\right\rfloor + \cdots\)

For a base \(b\), factor \(b\) into primes and take the minimum of \(\lfloor v_{p}(n!)/e_p\rfloor\) over \(p^{e_p}\,\|\, b\).

\(Z(n)\) jumps by \(1\) at most multiples of \(5\), by \(2\) at multiples of \(25\) and by \(3\) at multiples of \(125\). So some values are skipped: \(Z(24) = 4\) and \(Z(25) = 6\), so no \(n\) has \(n!\) ending in exactly \(5\) zeros. Any value that is attained is attained by exactly \(5\) consecutive \(n\).

Related: the product of \(k\) consecutive integers is divisible by \(k!\), because \(\binom{n}{k}\) is an integer.

Worked examples

Example 1: gcd and Bezout

Find \(\gcd(252, 198)\) and integers \(x, y\) with \(252x + 198y = \gcd\).

\(252 = 1\cdot198 + 54\), \(198 = 3\cdot 54 + 36\), \(54 = 1\cdot 36 + 18\), \(36 = 2\cdot 18\). So \(\gcd = 18\).

Back-substitute: \(18 = 54 - 36 = 54 - (198 - 3\cdot 54) = 4\cdot 54 - 198 = 4(252 - 198) - 198 = 4\cdot 252 - 5\cdot 198\).

So \((x, y) = (4, -5)\), and \(\operatorname{lcm} = 252\cdot198/18 = 2772\).

Example 2: non-negative solutions of a Diophantine equation

Solve \(172x + 20y = 1000\) in non-negative integers.

\(\gcd(172, 20) = 4\) and \(4 \mid 1000\), so divide by \(4\): \(43x + 5y = 250\). A particular solution is \((0, 50)\). The general solution is \(x = 5t\), \(y = 50 - 43t\). For \(x \ge 0\) and \(y \ge 0\): \(t \ge 0\) and \(t \le 50/43\), so \(t = 0, 1\).

Solutions: \((0, 50)\) and \((5, 7)\). There are exactly \(2\).

Example 3: positive solutions

How many positive integer solutions has \(7x + 11y = 200\)?

\(7\cdot 5 + 11\cdot 15 = 35 + 165 = 200\). General: \(x = 5 + 11t\), \(y = 15 - 7t\). Need \(x>0\): \(t \ge 0\) (since \(t=-1\) gives \(x=-6\)). Need \(y>0\): \(t \le 2\). So \(t = 0, 1, 2\) giving \((5,15), (16,8), (27,1)\): 3 solutions.

Example 4: trailing zeros

Find the number of zeros at the end of \(150!\) and of \(1000!\), and the exponent of \(2\) in \(100!\).

\(Z(150) = 30 + 6 + 1 = 37\). \(Z(1000) = 200 + 40 + 8 + 1 = 249\). \(v_2(100!) = 50 + 25 + 12 + 6 + 3 + 1 = 97\). Check with \((100 - s_2(100))/1\): \(100 = 1100100_2\) has digit sum \(3\), so \(97\).

Example 5: perfect number check

Is \(8128\) perfect? \(8128 = 2^6(2^7 - 1)\) and \(2^7 - 1 = 127\) is prime, so it is perfect by Euclid’s theorem. Direct check: \(\sigma(8128) = (2^7 - 1)(127 + 1) = 127\cdot128 = 16256 = 2\cdot 8128\).

Is \(2^{11}-1\) prime? No: \(2047 = 23\cdot 89\), even though \(11\) is prime. So \(2^{10}(2^{11}-1)\) is not perfect.

Example 6: a divisibility argument

Show that \(n^2 + n\) is always even, and that \(\gcd(n, n+1) = 1\).

\(n^2+n = n(n+1)\) is a product of two consecutive integers, one of which is even. If \(d \mid n\) and \(d \mid n+1\) then \(d \mid (n+1) - n = 1\), so \(d = 1\).

Common traps

  • \(1\) is not prime. FTA is for \(n > 1\). Questions that count primes or “prime numbers up to \(N\)” must exclude \(1\).
  • \(p \mid ab \Rightarrow p \mid a\) or \(p \mid b\) needs \(p\) prime. For composite \(6 \mid 2\cdot3\) but \(6\nmid 2\) and \(6 \nmid 3\). Options that drop “prime” are false.
  • \(\gcd\cdot\operatorname{lcm} = \) product holds for two numbers only. It fails for three: \(\gcd(2,2,2)\operatorname{lcm}(2,2,2) = 4 \ne 8\).
  • \(M_p\) is not always prime for prime \(p\) (\(M_{11} = 2047 = 23\cdot 89\)). Only the converse direction (“\(M_n\) prime implies \(n\) prime”) is true.
  • \(F_n\) is not always prime (\(F_5 = 641\cdot6700417\)). Fermat’s conjecture is false.
  • Euclid’s number \(p_1\cdots p_k + 1\) need not be prime; it only has a new prime factor.
  • Odd perfect numbers: do not say they do not exist; it is an open problem. Only the even ones are classified.
  • Twin prime and Goldbach statements are unproved. Options saying “proved” are wrong.
  • Diophantine equation without a solution: \(6x + 9y = 100\) has none since \(3 \nmid 100\). Always check \(\gcd \mid c\) first.
  • Trailing zeros count \(5\)s only, but remember higher powers \(25, 125\). \(Z(100) = 24\), not \(20\).
  • Pairwise versus overall coprime: \(\gcd(6, 10, 15) = 1\), yet every pair has gcd \(>1\).

Previous-year questions

2025 Q88. The number of twin prime pairs between \(51\) and \(100\) is: (A) 1 (B) 2 (C) 3 (D) 5. Answer: B (official key).

Solution: the primes in \(51\) to \(100\) are \(53, 59, 61, 67, 71, 73, 79, 83, 89, 97\). Pairs differing by \(2\): \((59, 61)\) and \((71, 73)\). Check the others: \(53\) and \(55\) (no), \(67\) and \(69\) (no), \(79\) and \(81\) (no), \(83\) and \(85\) (no), \(89\) and \(91 = 7\cdot13\) (no), \(97\) and \(99\) (no). So there are \(2\) pairs.

Quick revision

  • \(a = bq + r\) with \(0 \le r < b\), \(q, r\) unique.
  • \(\gcd(a,b)\operatorname{lcm}(a,b) = ab\) for two numbers; gcd uses minimum exponents, lcm maximum.
  • Bezout: \(\gcd(a,b) = ax+by\); \(ax + by = c\) solvable iff \(\gcd \mid c\); solutions step by \(b/d\) and \(a/d\).
  • Frobenius number of coprime \(a, b\) is \(ab - a - b\).
  • FTA: unique prime factorisation for \(n > 1\); \(1\) is not prime; \(2\) is the only even prime.
  • Composite \(n\) has a prime factor \(\le \sqrt n\).
  • Euclid: infinitely many primes; \(\pi(x) \sim x/\ln x\); \(\pi(100) = 25\).
  • Even perfect numbers are exactly \(2^{p-1}(2^p-1)\) with \(2^p-1\) prime; odd ones are an open problem.
  • \(M_n\) prime \(\Rightarrow\) \(n\) prime, not conversely (\(2047 = 23\cdot 89\)).
  • \(F_0\) to \(F_4\) are prime, \(F_5 = 641\cdot 6700417\); Fermat numbers are pairwise coprime.
  • \(v_p(n!) = \sum\lfloor n/p^k\rfloor\); zeros of \(100!\) are \(24\), of \(1000!\) are \(249\).
  • No \(n\) gives exactly \(5\) trailing zeros of \(n!\) (\(Z(24) = 4\), \(Z(25) = 6\)).
