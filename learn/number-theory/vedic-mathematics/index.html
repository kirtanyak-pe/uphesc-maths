Why it matters for the exam

The syllabus line is “basic concepts of vedic mathematics” and it showed up in 2026 Q48, where a product of two numbers near 1000 had to be reduced modulo 13. Expect either a short calculation done with one of the sutras (multiplying near a base, squaring numbers ending in 5, recurring decimals, divisibility) or a name-and-meaning question on the sutras. The calculation methods are quick to learn and every one of them can be checked by ordinary arithmetic.

Core ideas

The book and its status

Vedic Mathematics, or Sixteen Simple Mathematical Formulae from the Vedas was written by Swami Bharati Krishna Tirtha (1884 to 1960), Shankaracharya of the Govardhana Math at Puri. It was published in 1965, after his death, by Motilal Banarsidass (Varanasi), edited by V. S. Agrawala. The list of sutras was compiled by the editor from stray references in the text, and the list appears at the start of the book.

The sutras are not found in the Vedas. The editor says so in his foreword, and historians (Shukla, Hogendijk, Dani, Plofker) agree. For the exam: the methods are valid arithmetic and algebra; the name “Vedic” is a title and not a historical claim.

Nikhilam: multiplying near a base

Choose the power of 10 nearest to both numbers as the base. For each number write its deviation from the base (negative if below, positive if above).

Both below the base. Cross-subtract (take the deviation of one from the other number) for the left part, multiply the deviations for the right part. The right part has as many digits as the base has zeros.

\(97\times94\), base \(100\), deviations \(-3\) and \(-6\):

97  -3
94  -6
-----------
91 | 18     ->  9118

\(97-6=91\) (or \(94-3\)), and \((-3)(-6)=18\). Check: \(97\times94=9118\).

Both above the base. \(103\times107\): deviations \(+3,+7\). Left: \(103+7=110\); right: \(21\). Answer \(11021\).

One above, one below. The right part is negative, so subtract it. \(1003\times997\), base \(1000\), deviations \(+3\) and \(-3\): \(1003-3=1000,\qquad(+3)(-3)=-9\ \Rightarrow\ 1000\,000-9=999\,991.\) Likewise \(89\times107\) with base \(100\) (deviations \(-11,+7\)): left \(89+7=96\), right \(-77\), so \(9600-77=9523\).

Why it works. With base \(B\) and deviations \(a,b\): \((B+a)(B+b)=B(B+a+b)+ab\). The left part is \(B+a+b\) and the right part is \(ab\).

Sub-base. If the numbers are close to 50, use base \(100\) with the working base \(50=100/2\). For \(46\times53\): deviations from 50 are \(-4,+3\); left \(46+3=49\), multiplied by 50 gives \(2450\); right \(-12\); so \(2438\).

Urdhva-tiryagbhyam: vertically and crosswise

The all-purpose multiplication. Line up the digits and add the products of digits that sit vertically and crosswise, working from the units upward, carrying as you go.

For \((ab)\times(cd)\) (digits \(a,b\) and \(c,d\)): \(\text{units: }bd,\qquad\text{tens: }ad+bc,\qquad\text{hundreds: }ac.\) \(23\times14\): units \(3\cdot4=12\); tens \(2\cdot4+3\cdot1=11\); hundreds \(2\cdot1=2\). Write \(12\to2\) carry \(1\); \(11+1=12\to2\) carry \(1\); \(2+1=3\). Result \(322\).

For three digits there are five columns. \(132\times213\) (digits \(1,3,2\) and \(2,1,3\)): \(2\cdot3=6;\quad 3\cdot3+2\cdot1=11;\quad 1\cdot3+3\cdot1+2\cdot2=10;\quad 1\cdot1+3\cdot2=7;\quad 1\cdot2=2.\) Reading from the left with carries: \(28116\).

Algebra. \((ax+b)(cx+d)=acx^2+(ad+bc)x+bd\): the same pattern, without carries.

Squaring. For a two-digit number \((ab)^2\) the columns are \(b^2\), \(2ab\), \(a^2\) (the “duplex”). \(23^2\): \(9\); \(2\cdot2\cdot3=12\); \(4\). So \(4\,|\,12\,|\,9\) with carry: \(529\).

Ekadhikena Purvena

Squares ending in 5. For \((n5)^2\): the left part is \(n(n+1)\) and the right part is always \(25\). \(75^2=7\cdot8\,|\,25=5625,\qquad 115^2=11\cdot12\,|\,25=13225.\) Reason: \((10n+5)^2=100n(n+1)+25\).

Recurring decimals of \(1/(10k-1)\). Take the “one more than the previous” of the tens digit. For \(1/19\) it is \(1+1=2\). Start with the last digit \(1\) and build the decimal from right to left: each new digit is the units digit of \(2\times\)(previous digit)\(+\)carry, and the carry is the tens digit.

Digits right to left: \(1,2,4,8,6,3,7,4,9,8,7,5,1,3,6,2,5,0\). Reading backwards: \(\frac1{19}=0.\overline{052631578947368421}.\) The block has 18 digits, because 10 is a primitive root modulo 19. For \(1/29\) use multiplier \(3\) and get a block of 28 digits, \(0.\overline{0344827586206896551724137931}\).

Paravartya Yojayet: division by transposing

Divisors with a small second digit (negative flag). To divide by \(12\) the flag digit is \(2\), transposed to \(-2\). Split the dividend: the last digit is the remainder zone; the others give the quotient. Each quotient digit is the next dividend digit plus \((-2)\times\) the previous quotient digit.

\(1234\div12\): \(q_1=1\); \(q_2=2-2\cdot1=0\); \(q_3=3-2\cdot0=3\); remainder digit \(4-2\cdot3=-2\). So quotient \(103\) and remainder \(-2\). Fix the negative remainder: quotient \(102\), remainder \(12-2=10\). Check: \(12\cdot102+10=1234\).

Divisors just below a base (Nikhilam division). For \(9\) the complement is \(1\); add each result to the next digit. \(1234\div9\): \(1\); \(2+1=3\); \(3+3=6\); remainder digit \(4+6=10\). Quotient \(136\), remainder \(10\), which is \(9+1\), so quotient \(137\), remainder \(1\).

Linear equations. Transposing a term and adjusting its sign: \(ax+b=cx+d\Rightarrow x=\dfrac{d-b}{a-c}\).

Yavadunam: squares near a base

Let \(d\) be the deviation of \(n\) from the base \(B\). Then \(n^2=(n+d)\,|\,d^2\), where the right part has as many digits as \(B\) has zeros. \(98^2:\ d=-2,\ 98-2=96,\ d^2=04\ \Rightarrow\ 9604.\) \(103^2:\ 103+3=106,\ 09\ \Rightarrow\ 10609.\qquad 94^2:\ 94-6=88,\ 36\ \Rightarrow\ 8836.\) \(1006^2:\ 1012\,|\,036=1012036.\) Reason: \((B+d)^2=B(B+2d)+d^2=B(n+d)+d^2\).

Other algebraic sutras

  • Sankalana-vyavakalanabhyam (add and subtract). \(45x-23y=113\), \(23x-45y=91\). Adding: \(68x-68y=204\Rightarrow x-y=3\). Subtracting: \(22x+22y=22\Rightarrow x+y=1\). So \(x=2\), \(y=-1\).
  • Anurupye Sunyamanyat (if one is in ratio, the other is zero). \(6x+7y=8\), \(19x+14y=16\). The \(y\) coefficients are in ratio \(1:2\) and so are the constants \(8:16\), hence \(x=0\) and \(y=8/7\).
  • Sunyam Samyasamuccaye. \(\dfrac1{x+2}+\dfrac1{x+3}=\dfrac1{x+4}+\dfrac1{x+1}\). The sum of the denominators on each side is the same, \(2x+5\), so \(2x+5=0\) and \(x=-\tfrac52\) (it is the only solution because the difference of the two denominator products is the constant \(-2\)).
  • Gunitasamuccayah (check by sum of coefficients). \((x+7)(x+9)=x^2+16x+63\): put \(x=1\): \(8\cdot10=80=1+16+63\).
  • Ekanyunena Purvena (by one less). Multiplication by \(9,99,999,\dots\): \(34\times99=(34-1)\,|\,(99-33)=3366\); \(8\times999=7\,|\,992=7992\).
  • Antyayor Dasake’pi. If the first parts are equal and the last digits add to 10: \(47\times43=4\cdot5\,|\,7\cdot3=2021\); \(81\times89=8\cdot9\,|\,09=7209\).

Digit sums and casting out nines

The digit sum (beejank) of \(n\) is its remainder modulo 9, with 9 written as 0. Because \(10\equiv1\pmod9\), \(n\equiv\text{sum of digits}\pmod9.\) So digit sums can check an addition or multiplication: \(\mathrm{ds}(ab)\equiv\mathrm{ds}(a)\,\mathrm{ds}(b)\). For \(23\times14=322\): \(\mathrm{ds}(23)=5\), \(\mathrm{ds}(14)=5\), \(5\cdot5=25\to7\), and \(\mathrm{ds}(322)=7\). A passed check is necessary, not sufficient: it cannot detect an error that is a multiple of 9 or a transposition of digits. A wrong answer \(232\) also has digit sum 7.

Related: casting out elevens uses the alternating digit sum, because \(10\equiv-1\pmod{11}\).

Osculation (Vestanam). For a divisor \(m\) ending in 9 the positive osculator is the Ekadhikena number \((m+1)/10\): \(19\to2\), \(29\to3\), \(39\to4\). For \(m=7\): \(7\times7=49\), so \(+5\); for \(m=13\): \(13\times3=39\), so \(+4\). Example for 7: \(3367\to336+5\cdot7=371\to37+5\cdot1=42\), a multiple of 7. For 13: \(4056\to405+4\cdot6=429\to42+4\cdot9=78=6\cdot13\).

The 1001 test. \(5471893\to5\,|\,471\,|\,893\): \(893-471+5=427=7\cdot61\), so \(7\mid5471893\) and also \(5471893\equiv427\pmod{13}\) and mod \(11\) (the alternating sum keeps the remainder for all three).

Square roots and cube roots of perfect powers

Square root. The last digit of the square decides the last digit of the root up to two choices (\(1,9\to1\) or \(9\); \(4\to2\) or \(8\); \(5\to5\); \(6\to4\) or \(6\); \(9\to3\) or \(7\); \(0\to0\)). The digits before the last two decide the first digit. Example \(5476\): the part \(54\) lies between \(7^2=49\) and \(8^2=64\), so the first digit is 7; the last digit 6 gives 4 or 6; compare \(54\) with \(7\times8=56\): it is smaller, so take the smaller digit, 4. Root \(74\). For \(7396\): \(73\) is between \(64\) and \(81\) so first digit 8; \(73>8\times9=72\), so take the larger digit, 6. Root \(86\).

Cube root. The last digit of the cube fixes the last digit of the root: \(1\to1\), \(8\to2\), \(7\to3\), \(4\to4\), \(5\to5\), \(6\to6\), \(3\to7\), \(2\to8\), \(9\to9\). Cut off the last three digits; the remaining number’s cube root (rounded down) is the first digit. \(17576\): last digit 6 gives 6; the left part \(17\) lies between \(2^3=8\) and \(3^3=27\) so 2. Root \(26\).

Worked examples

Example 1: Nikhilam with a negative right part

Compute \(1012\times998\). Base \(1000\), deviations \(+12\) and \(-2\). Left: \(1012-2=1010\). Right: \(12\times(-2)=-24\). So \(1010\,000-24=1\,009\,976.\)

Example 2: A large Urdhva product

Compute \(412\times305\). Digit columns, units upward: \(2\cdot5=10\); \(1\cdot5+2\cdot0=5\); \(4\cdot5+1\cdot0+2\cdot3=26\); \(4\cdot0+1\cdot3=3\); \(4\cdot3=12\). Write the columns left to right \(12,3,26,5,10\) and carry from the right: \(10\to0\) carry \(1\); \(5+1=6\); \(26\to6\) carry \(2\); \(3+2=5\); \(12\). Result \(125660\).

Example 3: Squares

Find \(105^2\) and \(48^2\).

  • \(105^2\) as a number ending in 5: \(10\cdot11\,|\,25=11025\).
  • \(48^2\) with sub-base \(50\), deviation \(-2\): \((48-2)=46\), times \(50\) gives \(2300\); add \(d^2=4\). So \(2304\).

Example 4: Division by 12

Divide \(2345\) by \(12\) by the flag method. The flag is \(-2\). \(q_1=2\); \(q_2=3-2\cdot2=-1\); \(q_3=4-2\cdot(-1)=6\); remainder digit \(5-2\cdot6=-7\). Quotient \(2\,|\,{-1}\,|\,6=200-10+6=196\) with remainder \(-7\). Correct to quotient \(195\), remainder \(12-7=5\). Check: \(12\cdot195+5=2345\).

Example 5: Divisibility of 2717 by 13

Use the osculator \(+4\): \(2717\to271+4\cdot7=299\to29+4\cdot9=65=5\cdot13\). So \(13\mid2717\) (indeed \(2717=13\cdot209\)).

Example 6: Check a product by casting out nines

Verify \(47\times38=1786\). \(\mathrm{ds}(47)=2\), \(\mathrm{ds}(38)=2\), product \(4\); \(\mathrm{ds}(1786)=22\to4\). The check passes. A wrong answer \(1768\) also passes (a transposition), so a passed check never proves correctness.

Common traps

  • Sutra versus sub-sutra. Yavadunam is a sutra; Yavadunam Tavadunam is a sub-sutra. Vilokanam, Lopanasthapanabhyam and Anurupyena are sub-sutras; Puranapuranabhyam and Sunyam Samyasamuccaye are sutras.
  • Ekadhikena versus Ekanyunena. “One more than the previous” (squares ending in 5, decimals of \(1/19\)) against “one less than the previous” (multiplying by 9, 99, 999).
  • Sign of the right part in Nikhilam. If one number is above the base and the other below, the right part is negative: subtract it, do not add it.
  • Width of the right part. It has as many digits as the base has zeros: \(97\times94\) has two digits, and \(103\times101\) needs \(03\) (so \(104\,|\,03=10403\)). Overflow is carried into the left part.
  • Casting out nines gives a necessary condition only.
  • Origin. The sutras are not in the Vedas; the book appeared in 1965, after the author’s death in 1960.
  • A mod question is a congruence question. Do the Vedic product first if it is cheap, then reduce, or reduce the factors first. Both must agree. In 2026 Q48 neither route gives an option; see below.

Previous-year questions

2026, Q48 (worked key B; question defective)

Using the Vedic method and congruences, if \(x=1003\times997\), then \(x\pmod{13}\) equals: (A) 1 (B) 3 (C) 12 (D) 9.

Solution. Nikhilam with base 1000: \(1003\times997=1000^2-9=999991\). Since \(999999=999\cdot1001\) and \(1001=7\cdot11\cdot13\), we have \(999999\equiv0\pmod{13}\), so \(999991\equiv-8\equiv5\pmod{13}\). Direct check: \(1003\equiv2\) and \(997\equiv9\), so \(x\equiv18\equiv5\).

The value 5 is not among the options. The worked key records B (3) as a guess. The note on this question says it is a misprint: the same product gives \(3\) modulo \(11\) and \(1\) modulo \(9\), so the options may belong to a different modulus. In practice, if an option set like this appears, compute the true value, and pick the closest-looking option only if none matches.

Quick revision

  • Book: Vedic Mathematics, Bharati Krishna Tirtha, published 1965 (he died 1960); edited by V. S. Agrawala; not found in the Vedas.
  • 16 sutras and 13 sub-sutras; the list was compiled by the editor from the text.
  • Nikhilam: all from 9 and the last from 10; near a base, cross-add and multiply deviations. \(1003\times997=999991\).
  • Urdhva-tiryagbhyam: vertically and crosswise; works for any size and for polynomials.
  • Ekadhikena Purvena: \((n5)^2=n(n+1)\,|\,25\); \(1/19\) has a repeating block of 18 digits.
  • Ekanyunena Purvena: by one less; \(34\times99=3366\).
  • Paravartya Yojayet: transpose and adjust, used for division (\(1234\div12=102\) rem \(10\)) and linear equations.
  • Yavadunam: \(98^2=96\,|\,04=9604\).
  • Digit sum is \(n\bmod9\); casting out nines is necessary but not sufficient.
  • Osculators: \(+5\) for 7, \(+4\) for 13, \(+2\) for 19; or alternating groups of three for \(7\cdot11\cdot13=1001\).
  • \(1003\times997\equiv5\pmod{13}\), not an option in the 2026 paper.
