  1. Question 1Easy
    The solution of \(7x\equiv3\pmod{20}\) with \(0\le x<20\) is

    Why

    \(\gcd(7,20) = 1\), so there is exactly one solution. \(7^{-1}\equiv3\pmod{20}\) since \(21\equiv1\). Then \(x\equiv3\cdot3 = 9\). Check: \(7\cdot9 = 63 = 60+3\).
  2. Question 2Medium
    The remainder when \(\dbinom{14}{6}\) is divided by \(5\) is

    Why

    Lucas’s theorem: \(14=2\cdot5+4\) and \(6=1\cdot5+1\) in base \(5\), so \(\binom{14}6\equiv\binom21\binom41=2\cdot4=8\equiv3\pmod5\). Check: \(\binom{14}6=3003=5\cdot600+3\).
  3. Question 3Easy
    The number of solutions of \(8x\equiv6\pmod{12}\) modulo \(12\) is

    Why

    \(\gcd(8,12) = 4\) does not divide \(6\), so there is no solution. (The left side is always even and a multiple of \(4\) modulo \(12\), while \(6\) is not.)
  4. Question 4Medium
    The smallest positive integer \(x\) with \(x\equiv1\pmod4\), \(x\equiv2\pmod5\), \(x\equiv3\pmod7\) is

    Why

    Moduli are pairwise coprime, so the solution is unique modulo \(140\). \(x\equiv2\pmod5\) and \(x\equiv3\pmod7\) give \(x\equiv17\pmod{35}\), and \(17\equiv1\pmod4\). So \(x = 17\). The other options fail modulo \(7\): \(57\equiv1\), \(37\equiv2\), \(77\equiv0\).
  5. Question 5Medium
    The number of solutions of \(x^3\equiv8\pmod{13}\) modulo \(13\) is

    Why

    \(\gcd(3,12)=3\) and \(8^{12/3}=8^4\equiv1\pmod{13}\) (as \(8^2=64\equiv-1\)), so the congruence is solvable and has exactly \(3\) solutions. From \(x=2\), multiply by the cube roots of unity \(1,3,9\): \(x\equiv2,6,18\equiv5\). Check: \(5^3=125\equiv8\) and \(6^3=216\equiv8\).
  6. Question 6Easy
    The remainder when \(18!\) is divided by \(19\) is

    Why

    Wilson’s theorem for the prime \(19\): \(18!\equiv-1\equiv18\pmod{19}\).
  7. Question 7Hard
    The last digit of \(7^{(7^7)}\) is

    Why

    Last digits of powers of \(7\) repeat with period \(4\): \(7,9,3,1\). Exponent \(7^7\equiv(-1)^7\equiv3\pmod4\), so the digit is the third, \(3\). (Check: \(7^3 = 343\).)
  8. Question 8Easy
    The number of primitive roots modulo \(17\) is

    Why

    A prime \(p\) has \(\varphi(p-1)\) primitive roots: \(\varphi(16) = 8\). They are \(3,5,6,7,10,11,12,14\).
  9. Question 9Medium
    Which of the moduli \(8,\ 9,\ 12,\ 18\) possess a primitive root?

    Why

    Primitive roots exist iff \(m = 1,2,4,p^k,2p^k\) (\(p\) odd prime). \(9 = 3^2\) and \(18 = 2\cdot3^2\) qualify (for example \(2\) is a primitive root of \(9\), and \(5\) and \(11\) are the primitive roots of \(18\)). \(8 = 2^3\) and \(12 = 4\cdot3\) do not.
  10. Question 10Medium
    The order of \(10\) modulo \(13\) is

    Why

    \(10\equiv-3\); \(10^2\equiv9\), \(10^3\equiv90\equiv12\equiv-1\), so \(10^6\equiv1\) and the order divides \(6\) but not \(3\) (as \(10^3\equiv-1\)) or \(2\) (\(10^2\equiv9\)). The order is \(6\).
  11. Question 11Easy
    The smallest Carmichael number is

    Why

    \(561 = 3\cdot11\cdot17\) is square-free with \(2,10,16\) all dividing \(560\) (Korselt). \(341\) is only the smallest base-\(2\) pseudoprime; \(1105\) and \(1729\) are the next Carmichael numbers.
  12. Question 12Hard
    A solution of \(x^2\equiv2\pmod{343}\), obtained by lifting the root \(10\) of \(x^2\equiv2\pmod{49}\), is

    Why

    Put \(x=10+49t\). Then \(x^2\equiv100+980t\pmod{343}\), so we need \(98+980t\equiv0\pmod{343}\). Dividing by \(49\): \(2+20t\equiv0\pmod7\), i.e. \(6t\equiv5\), so \(t\equiv2\) and \(x=10+98=108\). Check: \(108^2=11664=34\cdot343+2\).
  13. Question 13Hard
    The number of solutions of \(x^2\equiv1\pmod{120}\) modulo \(120\) is

    Why

    \(120 = 8\cdot3\cdot5\). Modulo \(8\) there are \(4\) roots (\(1,3,5,7\)), modulo \(3\) there are \(2\), modulo \(5\) there are \(2\). By CRT: \(4\cdot2\cdot2 = 16\). (Lagrange’s bound of \(2\) roots holds only for prime moduli.)
  14. Question 14Medium
    The last two digits of \(13^{2025}\) are

    Why

    \(\lambda(100)=20\), so \(13^{20}\equiv1\pmod{100}\) and \(2025=20\cdot101+5\). Then \(13^{2025}\equiv13^5\): \(13^2=169\equiv69\), \(13^4\equiv69^2=4761\equiv61\), \(13^5\equiv61\cdot13=793\equiv93\).
  15. Question 15Medium
    Assertion (A): there is no primitive root modulo \(15\). Reason (R): every unit modulo \(15\) satisfies \(x^4\equiv1\), whereas \(\varphi(15)=8\). Choose the correct option.

    Why

    A primitive root modulo \(15\) would have order \(\varphi(15)=8\). But all eight units \(1,2,4,7,8,11,13,14\) satisfy \(x^4\equiv1\), so no unit has order \(8\) (the group is \(C_2\times C_4\), not cyclic). R is exactly why A holds.
