Why it matters for the exam

This is the “history” part of Unit 10 and it is pure recall. In the 2025 paper two questions came from it: a sequence of four Indian texts (Q91) and a match-list on 1729, 1887, 6174 and the birth year of Bhaskara II (Q92). Expect the same style again: order the texts or people in time, match person to work, match number to its owner. Learn the chronology table and the “who wrote what” table; those two tables answer most questions.

Core ideas

The big picture in five periods

Period Who and what
Vedic and Sulba (c. 800 BCE onwards) Sulba Sutras: geometry of altars, Pythagorean statement, \(\sqrt2\)
Classical and post-classical (c. 200 BCE to 200 CE) Pingala (binary patterns, Pascal triangle), Jain mathematics, Bakhshali manuscript (zero as a dot)
Siddhanta period (499 to c. 900 CE) Aryabhata I, Varahamihira, Brahmagupta, Bhaskara I, Mahavira, Sridhara
Medieval (1100 to 1600 CE) Bhaskara II, Narayana Pandita, the Kerala school: Madhava, Parameshvara, Nilakantha, Jyesthadeva
Modern (20th century) Ramanujan (1887 to 1920), Kaprekar (1905 to 1986)

The Sulba Sutras

Appendices to the Vedic Kalpa-sutras that give the rules for building fire altars with ropes (sulba means rope). The four main ones are those of Baudhayana, Apastamba, Katyayana and Manava. Baudhayana’s is the oldest (dated roughly 800 to 500 BCE; scholars differ on the exact range).

  • States the Pythagorean theorem for the diagonal of a rectangle, in words, and lists Pythagorean triples such as \((3,4,5)\), \((5,12,13)\), \((8,15,17)\), \((12,35,37)\).
  • Approximation of \(\sqrt2\): \(\sqrt2\approx 1+\frac13+\frac1{3\cdot4}-\frac1{3\cdot4\cdot34}=\frac{577}{408}\approx1.414216.\)
  • Methods to turn a rectangle into a square of equal area and rough circle-to-square constructions.

Pingala

Author of the Chandahshastra (Chandahsutra), the Sanskrit text on prosody, dated about 200 BCE (3rd to 2nd century BCE in most estimates).

  • Lists all patterns of short (laghu) and long (guru) syllables of a given length. This is a binary system, so a metre of \(n\) syllables has \(2^n\) patterns.
  • The pattern table (prastara) and the “Meru Prastara”, which is the triangle of binomial coefficients now called Pascal’s triangle. The name Meru Prastara comes through the commentator Halayudha (10th century).
  • The sums along the diagonals of the triangle are the Fibonacci numbers (matra-vrtta). Later Indian authors called these the “Hemachandra numbers” after Hemachandra (12th century).

Bakhshali manuscript

A birch-bark manuscript found in 1881 at Bakhshali village near Peshawar (now in Pakistan), now in the Bodleian Library, Oxford. It deals with arithmetic, algebra, square roots and problems on profit and loss. It uses a dot as a placeholder for zero. Oxford radiocarbon dating in 2017 showed the leaves come from at least three different periods; the oldest sample gave about 224 to 383 CE. Do not quote an exact date in an exam: say “3rd or 4th century CE at the earliest”.

Earlier, the oldest known zero symbol on stone in India was the one in a Gwalior temple inscription (876 CE).

Aryabhata I (476 to c. 550)

Born 476 CE, probably at Kusumapura (near Patna). Wrote the Aryabhatiya in 499 CE, aged 23. Four parts: Gitikapada (introduction, large numbers), Ganitapada (mathematics), Kalakriyapada (time reckoning), Golapada (sphere and eclipses).

  • Ganitapada has 33 verses with 66 rules. It gives \(\pi\approx\dfrac{62832}{20000}=3.1416\), called “approximate” (asanna) in the text.
  • Sums: \(1^2+\dots+n^2=\dfrac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}6\) and \(1^3+\dots+n^3=\left(\dfrac{n(n+1)}2\right)^2\).
  • Kuttaka (“pulveriser”) for the linear indeterminate equation \(by=ax\pm c\), essentially the Euclidean algorithm.
  • Sine table at intervals of \(3^\circ45'\) with a difference rule; the word jya (chord) became “sine” by way of Arabic.
  • Alphabetic numeral notation (up to \(10^{18}\)), square and cube root methods, area of the triangle \(\tfrac12\times\) base \(\times\) height.
  • Held that the apparent westward motion of the stars is caused by the Earth turning on its axis.

Do not mix him up with Aryabhata II (a later astronomer, c. 950 CE, author of the Maha-siddhanta).

Varahamihira (505 to 587)

Worked at Ujjain. Pancha-siddhantika (575 CE) summarises five earlier astronomical schools: Surya, Romaka, Paulisa, Vasistha and Paitamaha. Brihat Samhita is his great encyclopedia of astronomy, weather, omens and architecture. Mathematical items: the identities \(\sin x=\cos(\pi/2-x)\), \(\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1\), an improved sine table, a method for binomial coefficients (Pascal’s triangle), and a pandiagonal \(4\times4\) magic square.

Brahmagupta (598 to c. 670)

Brahmasphutasiddhanta (628 CE), written at Bhillamala (modern Bhinmal, Rajasthan); 25 chapters. Also the Khandakhadyaka (665 CE). The key facts for the exam:

  • First text to give rules for arithmetic with zero and negative numbers. Zero is what you get when a number is subtracted from itself; \(a+0=a\), \(a-0=a\), \(a\times0=0\). Positive numbers are “fortunes”, negative numbers “debts”; the product of two debts is a fortune. He wrongly stated \(0/0=0\) and said little useful about \(a/0\).
  • Brahmagupta’s formula: for a cyclic quadrilateral with sides \(a,b,c,d\) and \(s=\tfrac12(a+b+c+d)\), \(K=\sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)}.\) It generalises Heron’s formula (put \(d=0\)). It is exact only for cyclic quadrilaterals, though Brahmagupta did not state that restriction.
  • Brahmagupta’s identity (also called Brahmagupta–Fibonacci identity), the composition law behind his Pell-type equations: \((a^2+nb^2)(c^2+nd^2)=(ac-nbd)^2+n(ad+bc)^2=(ac+nbd)^2+n(ad-bc)^2.\)
  • Solved \(nx^2+1=y^2\) (“Pell’s equation”) by composition (bhavana); his own example \(92x^2+1=y^2\) has least solution \(x=120,\ y=1151\). (The harder \(61x^2+1=y^2\), least solution \(x=226153980,\ y=1766319049\), is the standard chakravala example of Bhaskara II.)
  • Solution of the quadratic equation, second-order interpolation for sines, and the theorem that in a cyclic quadrilateral with perpendicular diagonals the line from the meeting point of the diagonals to the midpoint of a side is perpendicular to the opposite side (Brahmagupta’s theorem).

Bhaskara I (c. 600 to c. 680)

Follower of Aryabhata I, probably from the Saurashtra (Gujarat) region. Works: Mahabhaskariya, Laghubhaskariya and the Aryabhatiyabhashya (629 CE), a commentary on the mathematical verses of the Aryabhatiya. He is known for the rational approximation to the sine, which he credits to Aryabhata: \(\sin x\approx\frac{16x(\pi-x)}{5\pi^2-4x(\pi-x)}\qquad(0\le x\le\pi),\) with maximum absolute error about \(0.0016\) (exact at \(x=\pi/6,\ \pi/2\)); the relative error is largest near \(x=0\) and \(x=\pi\), up to about \(1.9\%\). He rejected \(\sqrt{10}\) as the value of \(\pi\). Not the same person as Bhaskara II.

Mahavira (c. 800 to c. 870)

Jain mathematician, probably from the Mysore region. Ganita Sara Sangraha (about 850 CE), nine chapters; the earliest surviving Indian text devoted wholly to mathematics. Covers fractions (splitting into unit fractions), permutations and combinations, the kuttaka, cube roots, rational right triangles, and approximate area and perimeter of an ellipse.

Sridhara

Wrote the Patiganita and the Trisatika (also called Patiganitasara, a summary of the Patiganita). His dates are disputed (older estimates place him in the 8th century; MacTutor gives about 870 to 930, best guess around 900). Credited with a rule for solving quadratic equations by completing the square (multiply by \(4a\)). For the exam it is enough to know he comes after Brahmagupta: this is exactly what the 2025 sequence question tests.

Bhaskara II, Bhaskaracharya (1114 to 1185)

Born in 1114 at Vijayapura (Bijapur region); worked at Ujjain. Siddhanta Siromani (1150 CE), written when he was 36, has four parts:

Part Subject
Lilavati arithmetic (13 chapters), traditionally named after his daughter
Bijaganita algebra (12 chapters)
Grahaganita mathematics of the planets
Goladhyaya the sphere

His other work is the Karanakutuhala (1183 CE). Results to know:

  • The chakravala (cyclic) method for \(Nx^2+1=y^2\), a complete solution of the Pell equation; Europe reached it with Brouncker, Fermat and Lagrange centuries later.
  • Treated division by zero: \(n/0\) is an “infinite quantity” (khahara). This is not correct in modern terms.
  • Early ideas of derivatives: the derivative vanishes at an extremum, a form of Rolle’s theorem. These are precursors; it is not a developed calculus.
  • Solved quadratic and indeterminate equations, and gave rules for permutations.

Narayana Pandita (14th century)

Ganita Kaumudi (1356 CE): combinatorics, magic squares, cyclic quadrilaterals, indeterminate equations.

The Kerala school

Founded by Madhava of Sangamagrama (c. 1340 or 1350 to 1425, near Cochin). None of his mathematical texts survives (a few astronomical works are attributed to him); his series are known through later Kerala authors.

  • Infinite series for \(\arctan x\) (the “Madhava–Gregory–Leibniz series”): \(\frac\pi4=1-\frac13+\frac15-\frac17+\cdots\) more than two centuries before Gregory (1671) and Leibniz (1674).
  • Series for \(\sin x\) and \(\cos x\) (later called Newton’s series): \(\sin x=x-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\frac{x^5}{5!}-\cdots,\qquad \cos x=1-\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^4}{4!}-\cdots\)
  • A fast series \(\pi=\sqrt{12}\left(1-\dfrac1{3\cdot3}+\dfrac1{5\cdot3^2}-\dfrac1{7\cdot3^3}+\cdots\right)\), giving \(\pi\) correct to 11 decimals with 21 terms, and end-correction terms for the slow series.

The others:

Name Dates Work
Parameshvara c. 1380 to 1460 Drigganita (observation-based astronomy); mean value ideas
Nilakantha Somayaji 1444 to 1544 Tantrasangraha (1501 CE); commentary on the Aryabhatiya; revised planetary model
Jyesthadeva c. 1500 to c. 1575 Yuktibhasa (c. 1530, in Malayalam): proofs of the series, regarded as the first text with derivations of infinite series
Sankara Variyar c. 1500 to 1560 Commentaries including Kriyakramakari

Dates for the Kerala school differ by up to a few decades between sources; the order Madhava, Parameshvara, Nilakantha, Jyesthadeva is firm.

Ramanujan (1887 to 1920)

Srinivasa Ramanujan, born 22 December 1887 at Erode, died 26 April 1920 at Kumbakonam. Self-taught; wrote to G. H. Hardy in 1913, went to Trinity College, Cambridge in 1914. Elected Fellow of the Royal Society in 1918 and, later that year, a Fellow of Trinity College (the first Indian Fellow of Trinity). National Mathematics Day in India (22 December) marks his birthday.

Partitions. Let \(p(n)\) be the number of partitions of \(n\) into positive integers, so \(p(4)=5\). Hardy and Ramanujan proved in 1918 the asymptotic formula \(p(n)\sim\frac1{4n\sqrt3}\,e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}\qquad(n\to\infty).\) Rademacher later turned it into an exact convergent series. Ramanujan found congruences: \(p(5n+4)\equiv0\pmod5,\quad p(7n+5)\equiv0\pmod7,\quad p(11n+6)\equiv0\pmod{11}.\) (For example \(p(4)=5\), \(p(9)=30\), \(p(14)=135\); \(p(5)=7\), \(p(12)=77\); \(p(6)=11\), \(p(17)=297\).)

The number 1729. Hardy visited him in hospital in a taxi numbered 1729 and called it dull; Ramanujan replied that it is the smallest number expressible as a sum of two positive cubes in two ways: \(1729=1^3+12^3=9^3+10^3.\) It is also \(7\cdot13\cdot19\) and a Harshad number (divisible by the sum of its digits \(1+7+2+9=19\)). Numbers with this property are called taxicab numbers; the next one is \(4104=2^3+16^3=9^3+15^3\), then \(13832\).

Highly composite numbers (1915). \(n\) is highly composite if \(\tau(n)>\tau(m)\) for every \(m<n\). The sequence starts \(1,2,4,6,12,24,36,48,60,120,180,240,360,720,840,1260,1680\). Ramanujan found their structure: the exponents of the primes are non-increasing and the last exponent is 1 (except for 4 and 36).

Hardy–Ramanujan normal order theorem (1917): the number of distinct prime factors \(\omega(n)\) has normal order \(\log\log n\).

Series for \(1/\pi\) (1914): \(\frac1\pi=\frac{2\sqrt2}{9801}\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(4k)!\,(1103+26390k)}{(k!)^4\,396^{4k}}.\) Each term adds about eight correct digits. The first term alone gives \(\pi\approx3.14159273\).

Mock theta functions. In his last letter to Hardy (January 1920) he gave 17 functions with the behaviour of theta functions but not of the same type; Zwegers explained them in 2002.

Other items: Ramanujan tau function and its multiplicativity (conjectured by Ramanujan, proved by Mordell in 1917); Ramanujan sums \(c_q(n)=\sum_{d\mid(q,n)}\mu(q/d)\,d\); Ramanujan primes; the Rogers–Ramanujan identities; the Ramanujan–Nagell equation \(x^2+7=2^n\), whose only solutions have \(n=3,4,5,7,15\); the “lost notebook” found by George Andrews in 1976; and his \(4\times4\) magic square built on his birth date 22-12-1887, every row, column and diagonal summing to 139.

D. R. Kaprekar (1905 to 1986)

Schoolteacher at Devlali, Maharashtra.

  • Kaprekar’s constant 6174. Take a four-digit number with at least two different digits (keep leading zeros). Arrange its digits in descending order, subtract the ascending arrangement, repeat. You reach \(6174\) in at most 7 steps, and then \(7641-1467=6174\) again. Example: \(3524\to5432-2345=3087\to8730-0378=8352\to8532-2358=6174\). Repdigits such as 1111 go to 0 and are excluded. Announced in 1949.
  • For three digits the constant is \(495\) (at most 6 steps). For two digits there is no constant: the process falls into the cycle \(9\to81\to63\to27\to45\to9\).
  • Kaprekar numbers. \(n\) is a Kaprekar number if \(n^2\) can be split into two parts (the right part not zero) whose sum is \(n\). Examples: \(9^2=81\to8+1\); \(45^2=2025\to20+25\); \(55^2=3025\to30+25\); \(297^2=88209\to88+209\); \(703^2=494209\to494+209\). List: \(1,9,45,55,99,297,703,999,2223,2728,\dots\)
  • Also named: Harshad (Niven) numbers (divisible by their digit sum), self numbers (Devlali numbers, with no generator under \(n\to n+\) digit sum: \(1,3,5,7,9,20,31,\dots\)) and Demlo numbers (squares of repunits such as \(111^2=12321\); Demlo is a railway station).

Worked examples

Example 1: Brahmagupta’s formula

Find the area of a cyclic quadrilateral with sides \(7,24,15,20\).

\(s=\tfrac12(7+24+15+20)=33\). Then \(s-a=26\), \(s-b=9\), \(s-c=18\), \(s-d=13\), and \(K=\sqrt{26\cdot9\cdot18\cdot13}=\sqrt{234\cdot234}=234.\)

Example 2: Bhaskara I’s sine approximation

Estimate \(\sin45^\circ\). Put \(x=\pi/4\), so \(x(\pi-x)=3\pi^2/16\): \(\sin\tfrac\pi4\approx\frac{16\cdot3\pi^2/16}{5\pi^2-3\pi^2/4}=\frac{3\pi^2}{17\pi^2/4}=\frac{12}{17}\approx0.7059,\) against the true value \(0.7071\).

Example 3: A Kaprekar chain

Start with \(3524\).

  • \(5432-2345=3087\)
  • \(8730-0378=8352\)
  • \(8532-2358=6174\)

Three steps. After that every step returns \(6174\).

Example 4: The next taxicab number

We want two different pairs with the same sum of cubes. Searching sums above \(1729\) gives \(2^3+16^3=8+4096=4104\) and \(9^3+15^3=729+3375=4104\). So the next taxicab number is \(4104\).

Example 5: Ramanujan’s congruence in use

Show \(5\mid p(14)\). Since \(14=5\cdot2+4\), the congruence \(p(5n+4)\equiv0\pmod5\) with \(n=2\) applies. Indeed \(p(14)=135=5\cdot27\).

Common traps

  • Bhaskara I and Bhaskara II are different people, 500 years apart (c. 600 and 1114). The birth year 1114 belongs to Bhaskara II; Bhaskara I wrote the Aryabhatiya commentary.
  • Aryabhata I wrote the Aryabhatiya (499); Aryabhata II is a later astronomer.
  • Lilavati and Bijaganita are parts of Bhaskara II’s Siddhanta Siromani, not separate works of different authors or periods.
  • Patiganita and Trisatika are both Sridhara’s, and he comes after Brahmagupta (this is the order that appears in the 2025 question).
  • Brahmagupta’s area formula is for cyclic quadrilaterals; “any quadrilateral” is wrong. His rule \(0/0=0\) is also wrong.
  • Pell’s equation was solved by Brahmagupta and Bhaskara II centuries before Pell (the name is a historical mis-attribution); the chakravala is the cyclic method, not the same as Brahmagupta’s composition.
  • Madhava came before Gregory and Leibniz by about 250 years; but his own writings are lost, so the proofs we have are in Yuktibhasa (Jyesthadeva, in Malayalam).
  • 1729 is a sum of two cubes, not squares. 6174 is Kaprekar’s, 495 is the 3-digit constant. Do not attach 6174 to Ramanujan, or 1729 to Kaprekar.
  • Hardy–Ramanujan 1918 is about partitions; the 1917 paper is about the normal order of \(\omega(n)\).
  • In “arrange in order” questions, convert each item to a year first; do not trust the order in which the names are printed.

Previous-year questions

2025, Q91 (official key B)

Arrange in chronological order: I. Aryabhattiya, II. Trisatika, III. Brahmasphuta Siddhanta, IV. Panchasiddhantika. Options: (A) I, II, IV, III (B) I, IV, III, II (C) II, III, IV, I (D) I, III, II, IV.

Answer: B. Aryabhatiya (499) comes first; Panchasiddhantika (575) of Varahamihira; Brahmasphutasiddhanta (628) of Brahmagupta; Trisatika of Sridhara is last (c. 9th century on the best estimate; every estimate puts it after Brahmagupta). Order I, IV, III, II.
