Why it matters for the exam

Series, zeros and singularities are the language in which residues, poles and conformal maps are stated, so the topic feeds every later one. Direct questions seen: radius of convergence of \(\sum(3+4i)^nz^n\) (2019 Q34), which statement about \(e^z/(e^z-1)\) is false (2019), which statement about zeros is false (2019), residue of \(z\cos(1/z)\) at \(0\) (2025 Q61), and the pole-order statement for \(p/q\) (2025 Q66). Expect “classify the singularity” and “find the Laurent coefficient” questions.

Core ideas

Power series and radius of convergence

A power series \(\sum_{n\ge0}a_n(z-a)^n\) has a radius of convergence \(R\in[0,\infty]\): it converges absolutely for \(|z-a|<R\), diverges for \(|z-a|>R\), and anything can happen on \(|z-a|=R\).

\(\frac1R=\limsup_{n\to\infty}|a_n|^{1/n}\quad\text{(Cauchy-Hadamard)},\qquad \frac1R=\lim\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|\ \text{if this limit exists.}\)

  • Convergence is uniform on compact subsets of the open disc. The sum is analytic there and can be differentiated and integrated term by term; \(\sum a_nz^n\), \(\sum na_nz^{n-1}\) and \(\sum\frac{a_n}{n+1}z^{n+1}\) all have the same \(R\).
  • \(\sum(3+4i)^nz^n\): \(|a_n|^{1/n}=5\), so \(R=1/5\) (2019 Q34).
  • \(\sum z^n\): \(R=1\). \(\sum\frac{z^n}{n!}\): \(R=\infty\). \(\sum n!\,z^n\): \(R=0\). \(\sum n^nz^n\): \(R=0\). \(\sum\frac{z^n}{n^n}\): \(R=\infty\).
  • \(\sum\frac{n!}{n^n}z^n\): ratio \(\to1/e\), so \(R=e\).
  • \(\sum z^{n^2}\) and \(\sum z^{n!}\): \(R=1\) (lacunary: coefficients are \(0\) or \(1\)). For gap series use \(|a_n|^{1/n}\), not ratio.
  • \(\sum a_nz^{2n}\): if \(\sum a_nw^n\) has radius \(\rho\) then \(R=\sqrt\rho\).
  • Product of series: radius at least \(\min(R_1,R_2)\) (Cauchy product).

Taylor’s theorem

If \(f\) is analytic in \(|z-a|<R\), then \(f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(z-a)^n,\qquad a_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_{|z-a|=r}\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}dz\quad(0<r<R).\)

Radius of convergence of the Taylor series of \(f\) at \(a\) = distance from \(a\) to the nearest singularity of \(f\) (nearest point where \(f\) fails to be analytic, branch points included). This is much faster than the root test.

  • \(\dfrac1{1+z^2}\) at \(0\): nearest singularities \(\pm i\), \(R=1\). At \(a=1\): \(R=\sqrt2\). At \(a=2\): \(R=\sqrt5\).
  • \(\tan z\) at \(0\): nearest poles \(\pm\pi/2\), \(R=\pi/2\).
  • \(\operatorname{Log}(1+z)\) at \(0\): \(R=1\) (branch point at \(-1\)).
  • \(\dfrac{z}{e^z-1}\) at \(0\): singularities at \(\pm2\pi i\), \(R=2\pi\).
  • Taylor series of an entire function converges on all of \(\mathbb C\).

Laurent’s theorem

If \(f\) is analytic in the annulus \(A:\ r<|z-a|<R\) (\(0\le r<R\le\infty\)), then it has a unique expansion \(f(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_n(z-a)^n,\qquad a_n=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint_{|z-a|=\rho}\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}dz\quad(r<\rho<R),\) converging in \(A\) (uniformly on compact subsets).

  • The analytic part is \(\sum_{n\ge0}a_n(z-a)^n\), the principal part is \(\sum_{n<0}a_n(z-a)^n\).
  • The expansion is unique for a given annulus. The same function has different Laurent series in different annuli bounded by its singularities. Always identify the region first.
  • \(a_{-1}\) is the residue of \(f\) at \(a\) (when \(a\) is the centre of an annulus \(0<|z-a|<R\)).
  • If \(f\) is analytic in the whole disc \(|z-a|<R\) the Laurent series is the Taylor series (\(a_n=0\) for \(n<0\)).
  • To expand: use geometric series with the right ratio. For \(|z|<1\) write \(\frac1{1-z}=\sum z^n\); for \(|z|>1\) write \(\frac1{1-z}=-\frac1z\cdot\frac1{1-1/z}=-\sum_{n\ge1}z^{-n}\).

Example (three regions for one function). \(f=\dfrac1{(z-1)(z-2)}=\dfrac1{z-2}-\dfrac1{z-1}\).

Region Laurent series
\(\lvert z\rvert<1\) \(\sum_{n\ge0}\left(1-2^{-(n+1)}\right)z^n\) (Taylor)
\(1<\lvert z\rvert<2\) \(-\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}-\sum_{n\ge1}z^{-n}\)
\(\lvert z\rvert>2\) \(\sum_{n\ge0}(2^n-1)z^{-n-1}\)

Further expansions. \(e^{1/z}=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{1}{n!z^n}\) on \(0<|z|<\infty\); \(z\cos\frac1z=z-\frac1{2z}+\frac1{24z^3}-\cdots\) (residue \(-\frac12\)); \(\dfrac1{z(z-1)}=-\frac1z-\sum_{n\ge0}z^n\) on \(0<|z|<1\) and \(=\sum_{n\ge2}z^{-n}\) on \(|z|>1\).

Zeros of analytic functions

\(a\) is a zero of order \(m\) (\(m\ge1\)) of \(f\) if \(f(z)=(z-a)^mg(z)\) with \(g\) analytic and \(g(a)\ne0\). Equivalent: \(f(a)=f'(a)=\dots=f^{(m-1)}(a)=0\) and \(f^{(m)}(a)\ne0\); equivalent: the Taylor series at \(a\) starts with \(c_m(z-a)^m\), \(c_m\neq0\).

  • Zero of order \(1\) is a simple zero.
  • Orders add under multiplication: \(\operatorname{ord}(fg)=\operatorname{ord}f+\operatorname{ord}g\). If \(f\) has a zero of order \(m\) then \(f'\) has a zero of order \(m-1\) there, and \(f^2\) has order \(2m\).
  • Orders at \(z=0\): \(\sin z-z\): \(3\). \(1-\cos z\): \(2\). \(e^z-1-z\): \(2\). \(e^z-1\): \(1\). \(z^2\sin z\): \(3\). \(\sin^2z\): \(2\). \(\tan z-z\): \(3\). \(e^{z^2}-1\): \(2\). \(2\cos z-2+z^2\): \(4\). \(\sinh z-\sin z\): \(3\). (All checked from Taylor series.)
  • \(\sin z\) has simple zeros at \(n\pi\); \(\cos z\) at \(\frac\pi2+n\pi\); \(e^z-1\) at \(2\pi in\) (simple).

Zeros are isolated. If \(f\) is analytic and not identically zero on a domain \(D\), then each zero has a punctured neighbourhood with no other zero. So a non-zero analytic function can never have a zero of infinite order: if all derivatives vanish at \(a\) the Taylor series is \(0\) near \(a\), and then \(f\equiv0\) on \(D\).

Zeros are isolated in \(D\), but zero sets can accumulate on the boundary or at a singularity: \(\sin(1/z)\) has zeros \(1/(n\pi)\) accumulating at \(0\), which is not in the domain \(\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}\) where it is analytic. No contradiction.

Identity theorem

Statement. Let \(f,g\) be analytic on a domain \(D\) (open and connected). If \(f=g\) on a set that has a limit point in \(D\), then \(f\equiv g\) on \(D\). (Equivalently: if the zero set of \(f\) has a limit point in \(D\) then \(f\equiv0\).)

  • Typical: \(f(1/n)=0\) for all large \(n\), \(f\) analytic near \(0\) implies \(f\equiv0\).
  • If \(f(1/n)=1/n\) for all \(n\ge1\) and \(f\) is analytic on a domain containing \(0\) then \(f(z)=z\). If \(f(1/n)=1/n^2\), then \(f(z)=z^2\).
  • If \(f(1/n)=(-1)^n/n\): no analytic \(f\) exists on a neighbourhood of \(0\) (subsequences \(n\) even/odd would force both \(f=z\) and \(f=-z\)).
  • The limit point must lie in the domain; and the domain must be connected.
  • Real analytic functions \(e^x,\sin x\) have unique analytic extensions \(e^z,\sin z\) to \(\mathbb C\). So real identities like \(\sin^2+\cos^2=1\) extend automatically.

Isolated singularities

\(a\) is an isolated singularity of \(f\) if \(f\) is analytic in some punctured disc \(0<|z-a|<\rho\) but not at \(a\) (or not defined). Use the Laurent series \(f=\sum a_n(z-a)^n\) in that punctured disc.

Type Principal part Behaviour of \(f\) as \(z\to a\)
Removable none (\(a_n=0\) for \(n<0\)) \(\lim f\) exists and is finite; \(f\) is bounded near \(a\)
Pole of order \(m\) finite: \(a_{-m}\ne0\), \(a_n=0\) for \(n<-m\) \(\lvert f\rvert\to\infty\); \(\lim(z-a)^mf=a_{-m}\ne0\)
Essential infinitely many \(a_n\ne0\) with \(n<0\) no limit, finite or infinite

Tests:

  • Riemann’s removable singularity theorem: \(f\) bounded near \(a\) implies removable. (Also: \(\lim(z-a)f(z)=0\) implies removable.)
  • Pole of order \(m\) iff \(f(z)=\dfrac{g(z)}{(z-a)^m}\) with \(g\) analytic at \(a\) and \(g(a)\neq0\), iff \(1/f\) has a zero of order \(m\) at \(a\) (after removing the singularity).
  • Quotient \(f=p/q\) with \(p,q\) analytic at \(a\), \(q\) of zero order \(m\) at \(a\), \(p\) of order \(k\) at \(a\) (\(k=0\) if \(p(a)\ne0\)): pole of order \(m-k\) if \(m>k\), removable if \(m\le k\). In particular \(p(a)\ne0\) and \(q\) has a zero of order \(m\) gives a pole of order \(m\) (2025 Q66). Without \(p(a)\neq0\) the pole order can drop.
  • If \(|f(z)|\le M|z-a|^{-s}\) near \(a\), the singularity is removable or a pole of order \(\le s\); it is not essential.
  • To see an essential singularity: Laurent series with infinitely many negative powers, or two sequences tending to \(a\) with different limits of \(f\).

Casorati-Weierstrass. If \(a\) is an essential singularity of \(f\), then for every punctured neighbourhood \(U\) of \(a\), \(f(U)\) is dense in \(\mathbb C\). Great Picard theorem (stronger): \(f(U)\) is all of \(\mathbb C\) with at most one exception. \(e^{1/z}\) omits only \(0\).

Examples:

Function Point Type
\(\sin z/z\), \((e^z-1)/z\), \((1-\cos z)/z^2\) \(0\) removable
\(z/(e^z-1)\) \(0\) removable
\(1/z^3\), \(1/\sin z\), \(\cot z\), \(1/(e^z-1)\) \(0\) pole of order \(3\), \(1\), \(1\), \(1\)
\(1/(1-\cos z)\) \(0\) pole of order \(2\)
\(1/(z-\sin z)\) \(0\) pole of order \(3\)
\(\dfrac{1}{(z-1)^2(z+2)}\) \(1\); \(-2\) double pole; simple pole
\(e^{1/z}\), \(\sin(1/z)\), \(z\cos(1/z)\) \(0\) essential
\(\tan z\) \(\frac\pi2+n\pi\) simple poles
\(\dfrac{e^z}{e^z-1}\) \(2\pi ik\) simple poles

Not isolated. \(1/\sin(1/z)\) has poles at \(1/(n\pi)\) accumulating at \(0\), so \(0\) is a non-isolated singularity. \(\operatorname{Log}z\) and \(\sqrt z\) at \(0\) are branch points, not isolated singularities in the sense above.

Other facts:

  • A function with a pole at \(a\): \(f'\) has a pole of order \(m+1\); \(e^f\) has an essential singularity.
  • If \(f\) has a zero (pole) of order \(m\) at \(a\) then \(f'/f\) has a simple pole at \(a\) with residue \(m\) (\(-m\)).
  • At an essential singularity \(f'\) is again essential.

Singularity at infinity

Classify \(f\) at \(\infty\) by \(g(w)=f(1/w)\) at \(w=0\).

  • Removable at \(\infty\): \(f\) bounded near \(\infty\) (e.g. \(1/z\), \(\dfrac{z+1}{z-1}\)).
  • Pole of order \(m\) at \(\infty\): \(f(z)\sim cz^m\); for an entire function this means \(f\) is a polynomial of degree \(m\).
  • Essential at \(\infty\): \(e^z\), \(\sin z\), \(\cos z\), \(\cosh z\) (any entire function that is not a polynomial).

Consequences: an entire function with a removable singularity at \(\infty\) is constant (Liouville). An entire function with a pole at \(\infty\) is a polynomial. A function analytic on the extended plane (including \(\infty\)) is constant. A function meromorphic on the extended plane \(\mathbb C\cup\{\infty\}\) is a rational function.

Meromorphic functions

\(f\) is meromorphic on a domain \(D\) if it is analytic on \(D\) except for poles (isolated, so in a compact set there are finitely many).

  • Examples: rational functions, \(\tan z\), \(\cot z\), \(\sec z\), \(1/\sin z\), \(\dfrac{e^z}{e^z-1}\), \(\Gamma(z)\), \(\dfrac1{\sin\pi z}\).
  • Not meromorphic on \(\mathbb C\): \(e^{1/z}\), \(\operatorname{Log}z\), \(\sqrt z\), \(1/\sin(1/z)\) (essential singularity or branch/non-isolated singularity).
  • Every meromorphic function on \(\mathbb C\) is a quotient of two entire functions.
  • For \(f=\dfrac{e^z}{e^z-1}\) (2019): poles exactly where \(e^z=1\), i.e. \(z=2\pi ik\), \(k\in\mathbb Z\). Each is simple because \(e^z-1\) has a simple zero (\((e^z)'=e^z=1\ne0\)) and the numerator equals \(1\) there. So \(f\) is meromorphic, has infinitely many poles all on the imaginary axis, each simple, and is not entire.

Formulas and results

  • \(1/R=\limsup|a_n|^{1/n}\); ratio test \(1/R=\lim|a_{n+1}/a_n|\).
  • Taylor radius at \(a\) = distance to nearest singularity.
  • Taylor coefficients \(a_n=f^{(n)}(a)/n!=\frac1{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f}{(z-a)^{n+1}}\); same formula for Laurent coefficients \(a_n\), \(n\in\mathbb Z\).
  • Laurent series unique per annulus; \(a_{-1}\) is the residue.
  • Zero of order \(m\): \(f=(z-a)^mg\), \(g(a)\neq0\); derivatives up to \(m-1\) vanish.
  • Zeros of a non-zero analytic function on a domain are isolated; no infinite-order zeros.
  • Identity theorem: agreement on a set with a limit point inside the domain forces equality.
  • Removable: bounded. Pole: \(|f|\to\infty\), \(\lim(z-a)^mf\neq0\). Essential: otherwise.
  • Casorati-Weierstrass: dense image near an essential singularity. Picard: all values but one.
  • At \(\infty\): polynomial of degree \(m\) has pole of order \(m\); transcendental entire has essential singularity.
  • Meromorphic on the extended plane means rational.
  • If \(f\) has pole of order \(m\) at \(a\), \(f'/f\) has residue \(-m\) there.

Worked examples

Example 1: radius of convergence

Find \(R\) for \(\sum\frac{n!}{n^n}z^n\). Ratio: \(\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\frac{(n+1)!}{(n+1)^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{n^n}{n!}=\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^n\to\frac1e\). So \(R=e\).

Example 2: Laurent series in an annulus

Expand \(f(z)=\dfrac1{(z-1)(z-2)}\) for \(1<|z|<2\). Partial fractions \(f=\frac1{z-2}-\frac1{z-1}\). For \(|z|<2\): \(\frac1{z-2}=-\frac12\cdot\frac1{1-z/2}=-\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}\). For \(|z|>1\): \(\frac1{z-1}=\frac1z\cdot\frac1{1-1/z}=\sum_{n\ge1}z^{-n}\). So \(f(z)=-\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}-\sum_{n\ge1}z^{-n}.\)

Example 3: residue from a Laurent series (2025 Q61)

\(\cos w=1-\frac{w^2}2+\frac{w^4}{24}-\cdots\) with \(w=1/z\) gives \(z\cos\frac1z=z-\frac1{2z}+\frac1{24z^3}-\cdots\) on \(0<|z|<\infty\). The coefficient of \(z^{-1}\) is \(-\frac12\). So the residue at \(0\) is \(-\tfrac12\) and \(0\) is an essential singularity.

Example 4: order of a zero and type of singularity

\(f(z)=\dfrac1{z-\sin z}\) at \(0\). \(z-\sin z=\frac{z^3}6-\frac{z^5}{120}+\cdots\) has a zero of order \(3\), so \(f\) has a pole of order \(3\): \(f=\frac6{z^3}+\frac3{10z}+\cdots\) (residue \(\frac3{10}\)).

Example 5: identity theorem

Is there an entire function with \(f(1/n)=\dfrac1{n+1}\), \(n\ge1\)? Candidate \(g(z)=\dfrac z{1+z}\) is analytic near \(0\) and agrees on \(1/n\). By the identity theorem any analytic \(f\) on a domain containing \(0\) and these points equals \(g\) there. But \(g\) has a pole at \(-1\), so no entire \(f\) exists, while an \(f\) analytic on \(|z|<1\) exists (it is \(g\)). Always state the domain.

Example 6: classification at \(0\) and \(\infty\)

\(f(z)=\dfrac{\sin z}{z^2}\): \(\sin z=z-\frac{z^3}6+\cdots\), so \(f=\frac1z-\frac z6+\cdots\), a simple pole at \(0\), residue \(1\). At \(\infty\): \(f\to0\) along the real axis but along the imaginary axis \(\sin z\) grows exponentially, so \(\infty\) is an essential singularity.

Common traps

  • The radius of convergence for the Taylor series is the distance to the nearest singularity, not the distance to the nearest zero or to the origin. It depends on the centre.
  • Always read the annulus in a Laurent question. The same \(f\) has different series on \(|z|<1\), \(1<|z|<2\) and \(|z|>2\).
  • Removable singularity: \(\sin z/z\) at \(0\) is not a pole. A quotient \(p/q\) with \(q(a)=0\) is a pole only when the numerator does not vanish to at least the same order.
  • Pole order \(=\) order of zero of denominator only if \(p(a)\ne0\) (see 2025 Q66: the reason \(p(z_0)\neq0\) is essential).
  • Essential singularity: \(e^{1/z}\) has no limit at \(0\) (it \(\to\infty\) along the positive real axis, \(\to0\) along the negative real axis).
  • A nonzero analytic function cannot have a zero of infinite order (isolated zeros). “Zeros can have infinite order” is the false statement in 2019.
  • Identity theorem needs a connected domain and a limit point inside it. \(\sin(1/z)\) vanishes at \(1/(n\pi)\) but is not zero.
  • \(e^z/(e^z-1)\) is not entire; it has infinitely many simple poles \(2\pi ik\).
  • Polynomials have a pole at \(\infty\); \(e^z\), \(\sin z\), \(\cos z\) have an essential singularity at \(\infty\); \(1/z\) has a zero at \(\infty\).
  • The residue is the coefficient of \((z-a)^{-1}\) in the Laurent expansion about \(a\), not the coefficient of the leading term.
  • Ratio test fails on gap series (\(\sum z^{n!}\)); use the root test.
  • Casorati-Weierstrass says the image is dense, Picard says all values but one. Neither holds at a pole.

Previous-year questions

2019 Q34: radius of convergence of the series with coefficients \((3+4i)^n\)

Answer: \(1/5\). The series is \(\sum(3+4i)^nz^n\) (the variable symbol was garbled in the recovered source). \(|a_n|^{1/n}=|3+4i|=5\) so \(R=1/5\).

2019 (unnumbered, no. 1): for \(f(z)=\dfrac{e^z}{e^z-1}\) which statement is NOT true? (entire; has only poles as singularities; infinitely many poles on the imaginary axis; each pole simple)

Answer: “\(f\) is entire”. \(f\) is meromorphic with simple poles at \(z=2\pi ik\).

2019 (unnumbered, no. 4): which statement about analytic functions is NOT true? (zeros isolated; identity theorem; poles isolated; a non-zero analytic function can have a zero of infinite order)

Answer: “a non-zero analytic function can have a zero of infinite order”. Zeros of a non-identically-zero analytic function have finite order and are isolated.

2025 Q61: residue of \(z\cos(1/z)\) at \(z=0\)

Answer: (C) \(-\frac12\). \(z\cos\frac1z=z-\frac1{2z}+\cdots\); the coefficient of \(\frac1z\) is \(-\frac12\).

2025 Q66: assertion-reason on poles

Assertion (A): the quotient \(p(z)/q(z)\) has a pole of order \(m\) at \(z_0\). Reason (R): \(p(z_0)\ne0\) and \(q\) has a zero of order \(m\) at \(z_0\).

Answer: (A) both true and R explains A. With \(p(z_0)\ne0\) (here \(p\) analytic at \(z_0\)), \(q=(z-z_0)^mh\), \(h(z_0)\ne0\), so \(p/q=\frac{p/h}{(z-z_0)^m}\) with numerator non-zero at \(z_0\): a pole of order \(m\). (This is the official key’s answer.)

Quick revision

  • \(1/R=\limsup|a_n|^{1/n}\); \(\sum(3+4i)^nz^n\) has \(R=1/5\).
  • Taylor radius at \(a\) = distance to the nearest singularity of \(f\).
  • Laurent series: unique per annulus; \(a_{-1}\) = residue; principal part = negative powers.
  • Zero of order \(m\): \(f=(z-a)^mg\), \(g(a)\neq0\); \(\sin z-z\) has order \(3\) at \(0\).

  • At \(\infty\): polynomial is a pole, \(e^z\) is essential; meromorphic on the extended plane means rational.
  • \(e^z/(e^z-1)\): simple poles at \(2\pi ik\), not entire.
  • Residue of \(z\cos(1/z)\) at \(0\) is \(-\frac12\).
