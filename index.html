Where the 70 questions come from

The paper follows the syllabus units almost exactly. No unit can be skipped: each was worth about 14 of the 200 marks, and is worth about 27 of the 360 in the new pattern.

A 12-week plan

Every unit is worth about 14 marks, so the plan starts with the most marks per hour, not the hardest units. Short on time? Keep the order and shorten each phase.

  1. Weeks 1–2: repeats and the short units

    Work through the 21 ideas asked in both papers until you can do each with new numbers. Then number theory and the methods unit: both are short, formula-driven and grew in 2026. See the repeats

  2. Weeks 3–6: the big calculation units

    Linear algebra (up to 10 questions), complex analysis, ODE and PDE, and OR and numerical. Practise two-step problems, most of all in linear algebra.

  3. Weeks 7–10: the theory units

    Algebra, analysis, topology and geometry, and mechanics. Learn the standard examples and counterexamples: most of these questions ask which statement must hold.

  4. Weeks 11–12: gaps and timed papers

    One pass over the topics not asked yet, listed on each unit page. Then sit the 2025 and 2026 papers under exam timing and mark them with the solved keys.

How the paper is changing

2026 asked for answers, not recognition

2025 had 4 assertion–reason, 3 match-the-list and 3 sequence questions. 2026 had none. Numeric questions rose from 20 to 31, and many were chained, for example: the minimal polynomial is (x − 2)³(x − 1), find the Jordan blocks, then a rank. Practise solving to a number.

Topics that moved

Number theory went from 3 to 7 and integral equations from 2 to 6. Real analysis fell from 7 to 3, and history of maths from 3 to 0. Manifolds and viscous flow are new in the syllabus and appeared in 2026.

Back to 2019
